The chapter follows the three key points in the curriculum: what sustainable development means; using energy-consumption and reserve statistics to show that resources are finite, and the environmental and ecological impacts of resource use; and how understanding the Earth supports sustainability in its different aspects. ## Sustainable development: using now so that later generations can too

<Term t="chemistry.sustainable-development">Sustainable development</Term> means meeting the needs of the present generation without harming the ability of later generations to meet theirs. To judge whether a way of using a resource can last, think of the resource as a **stock**: nature puts some back each year, and people take some out each year.

- If it is replenished at least as fast as it is used, the stock does not shrink and can be used indefinitely.
- If it is used faster than it is replenished, the stock shrinks year by year and one day runs out.

Fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas take a very long time to form. Measured against the time over which people use them, they are replenished at a rate of almost zero, so they are **non-renewable resources**. Solar and wind energy keep arriving afresh, so they are **renewable resources**.

<Perspective title="Keeping the account by generations">

Sustainable development is about "later generations", so instead of drawing a curve over time you can split time into generations and share the resource out. Suppose a non-renewable resource has a stock of 90 (illustrative data) and a generation is about 30 years:

| Way of using it | 1st generation | 2nd generation | 3rd generation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 1st generation uses 60 first | 60 | 30 | 0 |
| Each generation takes an equal share | 30 | 30 | 30 |

Equal shares give all three generations some, but from the 4th generation on there is still none: with zero replenishment the total is 90, and however you share it, it runs out.

Now take a renewable forest that grows by 3 a year (illustrative data), so 90 in one 30-year generation. If each generation takes only 90, the forest's stock stays the same, and the 4th and 5th generations can take 90 too. **Non-renewable resources must be used sparingly and replaced; renewable ones can be used generation after generation as long as use does not exceed replenishment.**

</Perspective>

## Resources are finite: statistics as evidence

The most direct evidence that a resource is finite is to compare two statistics: the **reserves** (the stock that is known and can be extracted) and the **annual use**. If each year's use stays the same, you can work out how many more years it will last:

$$
\text{years it lasts}=\frac{\text{reserves}}{\text{annual use}}
$$

For example, a country's coal reserves are 600 and its annual use is 20 (in millions of tonnes, illustrative data). If annual use stays the same, the coal lasts $600\div20=30$ more years. This number holds only under the assumption that annual use stays the same, and as the opening question showed, use usually does not stay the same.

<Predict id="coal-years" prompt="The same 600 of coal, but now use starts at 20 a year and then rises steadily, by 1 a year every year. After about how many years will the coal run out?" answer={20} unit="years">

About 20 years. Over those 20 years the rate of use climbs steadily from 20 to 40 a year, so on average it is 30 a year, and $30\times20=600$. Use grows by only 1 a year, yet the number of years drops from 30 to 20.

</Predict>

The graph below shows the "cumulative use": the total used from this year on. The year in which cumulative use catches up with the reserves is when the resource runs out.

<Perspective title="A year-by-year ledger: working it out without a graph">

Without a curve, you can also add up one year at a time. A country's oil reserves are 300; it uses 50 in the first year and 10 more each year than the year before (illustrative data):

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Use that year | 50 | 60 | 70 | 80 | 90 |
| Cumulative use | 50 | 110 | 180 | 260 | 350 |

By the end of year 4 the total is 260, leaving 40; year 5 needs 90, which is not enough, so the oil runs out during year 5. Using "reserves ÷ first year's use" gives $300\div50=6$ years, an overestimate of more than a year. **When use grows each year, the real number of years is always shorter than this ratio.**

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="If the calculation says 30 years, does that mean there will be nothing left after 30 years?">

Not necessarily. The number is an indicator worked out under the assumption that annual use and the reserves both stay the same; it is not a forecast. Use changes with demand, and the estimated reserves are revised as exploration and extraction technology change. Its value is as evidence: as long as a resource is used faster than it is replenished, the stock keeps shrinking.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Use growing by a fixed percentage: finding the years with logarithms" needs="geometric series and logarithms (the maths chapter on growth models)" href="/en/math/s116-growth-models/">

If each year's use is a fixed fraction $p$ more than the year before (for example $p=5\%$), the cumulative use over $n$ years is a geometric series:

$$
P\left[1+(1+p)+\cdots+(1+p)^{n-1}\right]=\frac{P\left[(1+p)^n-1\right]}{p}
$$

Here $P$ is the first year's use. Setting this equal to the reserves $R$ gives $(1+p)^n=1+\dfrac{pR}{P}$. Take $\dfrac{R}{P}=30$ and $p=0.05$: then $(1.05)^n=2.5$, so

$$
n=\frac{\log 2.5}{\log 1.05}\approx\frac{0.398}{0.0212}\approx18.8
$$

Less than 19 years, much shorter than 30.

</Perspective>

## The environmental and ecological impacts of using resources

Besides resources being finite, extracting and using them changes the environment:

- Mining changes the land surface and destroys habitats.
- Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, which strengthens the <Term t="chemistry.greenhouse-effect">greenhouse effect</Term>; fuels that contain sulfur also release sulfur dioxide, which causes <Term t="chemistry.acid-rain">acid rain</Term>.

Groundwater is a stock too: rain soaks into the ground and replenishes it, and people pump it out of wells. Here are some numbers to show what happens when it is pumped too fast.

<Predict id="groundwater-years" prompt="On a coastal plain, groundwater is recharged by 30 each year and 50 is pumped out each year (in millions of cubic metres, illustrative data). How many years does it take for the groundwater stock to fall by 400?" answer={20} unit="years">

The stock falls by $50-30=20$ a year, so $400\div20=20$ years. Note that what counts is "pumping minus recharge", not the pumping alone.

</Predict>

When pumping exceeds recharge for a long time, the water table keeps falling. The layers of soil underground lose the support of the water and are squeezed more tightly together, so the ground sinks: this is **land subsidence**. Ground that has sunk can hardly recover fully, even if pumping stops later. In coastal areas, seawater can also seep into the aquifer and make the groundwater salty.

## Understanding the Earth supports sustainability in every aspect

The curriculum's third key point is that understanding the Earth supports sustainability in its **economic, ecological, cultural and policy** aspects. What you learned about the Earth in earlier chapters all comes into play:

- Taiwan lies on a <Term t="earth.convergent-plate-boundary">convergent plate boundary</Term> and has frequent earthquakes. Knowing where the faults are lets us plan land use and strengthen buildings, cutting disaster losses.
- Typhoons bring disasters, but they also bring a lot of rain, an important source of water for reservoirs. Understanding typhoons helps both with preventing disasters and with managing water resources.
- Preserving distinctive landforms and geological landscapes also preserves the local stories and culture linked with them.

<StudentAsks q="Earthquakes can't be predicted to the exact time, so what's the use of understanding faults and plates?">

Even without knowing the day, we still know where earthquakes are more likely and how hard the ground may shake. <Perspective title="One decision, four ledgers: tidal power on a coast">

Apply the four aspects to one decision. A county wants to build a tidal power station on its coast and has two candidate sites (illustrative data): site A has a tidal range of 1 metre and site B 4 metres.

1. **Economic**: the larger the tidal range, the more seawater flows in and out with each tide, and the bigger the height difference. As a rough estimate, with 4 times the tidal range, about 4 times as much water flows through and the average drop is also about 4 times as large, so about $4\times4=16$ times as much electricity can be generated. On this ledger alone, site B is far better. The tidal range has to be worked out from tide records, a skill from the tides chapter.
2. **Ecological**: a barrage changes how the tide flows in and out, which affects life in the intertidal zone. If the two shores slope about the same, site B's larger tidal range gives it a wider intertidal zone, so the area affected may be larger.
3. **Cultural**: fishing villages and traditional fishing activities on the coast may change.
4. **Policy**: before development, the environmental impact must be assessed as the rules require, and local residents must be consulted.

The four ledgers need not agree: the economic ledger favours site B, while the ecological ledger warns that site B's cost may also be larger. Understanding the Earth lets each ledger be worked out from evidence rather than by feel. GSAT 115 Q50–53 used tides and tidal power as their setting, combined with physics.

</Perspective>
