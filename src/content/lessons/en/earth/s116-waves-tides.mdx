## Waves: the energy moves on, the water circles in place

From junior high, a wave has crests and troughs, and the distance between neighbouring crests is the <Term t="physics.wavelength">wavelength</Term>. For sea waves we also use the **wave height**, from crest to trough.

Wave energy comes mainly from **wind on the sea surface**, which hands energy to the water. In general, the stronger the wind and the longer and farther it blows, the bigger the waves. **Submarine landslides** and **submarine earthquakes** also make waves; the huge waves from a submarine earthquake are a tsunami.

What a wave carries forward is energy and its shape. In deep water the water itself mostly moves in a small circle and returns to where it was.

<Perspective title="By numbers: how far the crest and the water go">

With illustrative numbers: out at sea the waves have a wavelength of $80$ m, and a crest passes every $8$ s (a period of $8$ s). Each period, the crest moves on one wavelength, while the water goes once round and is back where it started.

| Time passed | 8 s | 16 s | 24 s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crest moves on | 80 m | 160 m | 240 m |
| Net movement of the water | about 0 | about 0 | about 0 |

After 24 s the crest has gone 240 m, but the parcel of water is still circling in place. So a wave can deliver energy to a distant shore without the water itself going there.

</Perspective>

## Wind waves and swell

Where the wind is blowing, waves come in all sizes and directions, with sharp, steep crests: **wind waves** (<Zh>風浪</Zh>). When waves leave the windy area, or the wind drops, they keep travelling, and one rule decides which go far: **the longer the wavelength, the more slowly a wave loses energy, and the farther it travels**. So the waves that reach far away are long, with smooth, regular, rounded crests. This is <Term t="earth.swell">swell</Term>.

<Perspective title="The sea as a sieve">

Another angle: the windy area sends out a mix of waves of every wavelength, and the sea sieves out the short ones on the way. Here is the graph's illustrative data as a table of relative heights; the last row divides the long wave's height by the short wave's:

| Distance from the windy area | 0 | 500 km | 1000 km | 2000 km |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The farther they go, the bigger the ratio, until 2000 km away the long wave is about 50 times as high. A mix that sets out together ends up almost all long waves, so distant coasts receive regular long swell. This also answers the opening question: the long swell a typhoon raises can arrive before the typhoon.

</Perspective>

### Pre-typhoon swell and rogue waves

A typhoon's strong winds cover a wide area, and the waves they raise spread out as swell, which may hit the coast while the typhoon is still far away and the weather on shore is still fine. A **rogue wave** (<Zh>瘋狗浪</Zh>, the usual name in Taiwan) is a sudden wave, much bigger than those before and after it, that surges onto the shore or the rocks. Because it often comes in calm-looking weather, people fishing or playing on rocks and breakwaters are easily swept away.

<StudentAsks q="It's sunny, there's little wind and the waves don't look high. Why is it still so dangerous?">

These waves are not made by the local weather; they are swell from far away. With its long wavelength and rounded crests, swell looks like little out at sea and only rises suddenly in shallow water (next section). So nice weather does not mean a safe shore.

</StudentAsks>

## Waves change the coast

As a wave nears the shore, the water gets shallower and the wave slows down, grows taller and steeper, and finally breaks, so the water rushes up the shore.

When waves approach a curved coast, the part of a crest line that reaches shallow water first slows down first, so the crest line bends until it is roughly parallel to the shore. In front of a jutting **headland** (<Zh>岬角</Zh>) the water gets shallow sooner, so the waves bend in on it from both sides: **wave energy is concentrated on the headland**, which is strongly eroded, often into sea cliffs and wave-cut platforms. In a curved-in **bay** (<Zh>海灣</Zh>) the energy is spread out, so sand settles and beaches often form. Waves hitting the shore at an angle also push sand along the coast, building spits and sandbars. The CEEC exam specification has an item set (<Zh>題組</Zh>) set on <Zh>外傘頂洲</Zh>, a sandbar off Taiwan's west coast.

<Perspective title="By wave speed: why the crest lines bend">

Another view uses one rule: **the shallower the water, the slower the waves**. This is <Term t="physics.refraction">refraction</Term>, as in the physics chapter [Wave properties, the Doppler effect and light as a wave](/en/physics/s116-waves-light/): when the wave speed changes, the direction of travel changes.

With illustrative numbers: a straight crest line heads for a headland. On both sides the water is deeper and the wave travels $2$ m each second; right in front of the headland it is shallower and the wave travels only $1$ m each second. After $10$ seconds the sides have gone $20$ m and the middle only $10$ m. The middle lags and the sides get ahead and wrap in towards the headland, concentrating the energy there, as the arrows show.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Why do waves grow taller near the shore and then curl over and break?">

Because waves slow down in shallow water while the waves behind are still faster and keep catching up. The wavelength shortens and the same energy is squeezed into a shorter distance, so the wave grows taller and steeper, until the water at its top outruns the water below, tips forward and breaks into white foam.

</StudentAsks>

## Tides: the sea rising and falling each day

Stand by a harbour all day and you see a slower rise and fall: about every half day the sea slowly rises and falls. These are the tides. The highest level is **high tide** (<Zh>滿潮</Zh>), the lowest **low tide** (<Zh>乾潮</Zh>). Tide levels are measured from a fixed reference height, and levels below it are negative. The difference between a high tide and the neighbouring low tide is the <Term t="earth.tidal-range">tidal range</Term>.

Here are one day's tide levels at a harbour (illustrative data):

| Time | 03:00 | 09:12 | 15:25 | 21:37 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tide level (m) | 1.9 | −1.2 | 1.9 | −1.2 |
| | high tide | low tide | high tide | low tide |

<Predict id="tidal-range" prompt="From the table, what is the tidal range between the 03:00 high tide and the 09:12 low tide, in metres?" answer={3.1} unit="m">

The tidal range is high tide minus low tide: $1.9-(-1.2)=1.9+1.2=3.1$ m. A low tide of $-1.2$ m means $1.2$ m below the reference height, not "no water", so it is added back. Question 50 of the 115 GSAT asked candidates to plot tide data and work out the tidal range; the CEEC's report on the marking says most plotted correctly, but only about half got the range right.

</Predict>

Now look at the times. The two high tides in the table are at 03:00 and 15:25, 12 hours 25 minutes apart.

<Predict id="tide-delay" prompt="Keeping this rhythm, how many minutes later than today's 03:00 will tomorrow's first high tide be?" answer={50} unit="minutes">

High tide today is at 03:00; 12 hours 25 minutes later is 15:25, and 12 hours 25 minutes after that is 03:50 tomorrow. So tomorrow's high tide is about $50$ minutes later. A tidal "day" is about 24 hours 50 minutes, not 24 hours.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="By the Moon's position: the tidal clock follows the Moon">

If the Sun mainly caused the tides, high tide would come at the same time each day, since the Sun is highest at about the same time each day. Instead the data show high tide about 50 minutes later each day: the Moon's rhythm.

From junior high, the Moon revolves around Earth from west to east, and its phases repeat about every $29.5$ days. Each day it moves east by about $\frac{1}{29.5}$ of a circle, so Earth must turn that extra bit to face it again, which takes about

$$
24\times60\div29.5\approx49\ \text{minutes}
$$

plus a little more for the Moon's further motion in that time: about 50 minutes. **The tidal clock matches the Moon, not the Sun**: the observed data show that the Moon is the main cause of the tides.

</Perspective>

## Spring and neap tides: the Sun lends a hand

```dialogue
S: If the tides follow the Moon, does the Sun do nothing at all?
T: The Sun has an effect too, just a smaller one than the Moon's. Together they make the tidal range grow and shrink over a month. Make a guess first.
T: Over a month, the Moon goes from new moon to first quarter, full moon and last quarter. Around which phase do the days with the largest tidal range come?
>> turn: q-spring
S: So when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, the two effects add together?
T: Right. Let's see why with a figure.
```

The Moon's <Term t="physics.universal-gravitation">universal gravitation</Term> raises the sea in two bulges, one facing the Moon and one facing away from it (the student question below explains why). As Earth rotates, each place passes both bulges and both low parts in about a day (24 hours 50 minutes), so many places get two high tides and two low tides. The Sun raises two smaller bulges.

- **New moon and full moon**: the Sun, Earth and Moon are roughly in a line, and their bulges add up. High tides are especially high and low tides especially low, so the tidal range is largest. These are **spring tides** (<Zh>大潮</Zh>).
- **First and last quarter**: the Moon's direction is $90°$ from the Sun's. The Sun's bulges fall right where the Moon makes low water, so the two partly cancel and the tidal range is smallest. These are **neap tides** (<Zh>小潮</Zh>).

<StudentAsks q="The Moon is on one side of Earth. Why is there a high tide on the side facing away from it too?">

Gravitation weakens with distance. The near-side water is pulled more than Earth's centre, and Earth's centre more than the far-side water. So relative to Earth's centre, the near water is pulled towards the Moon and the far water is "left behind": both sides bulge. You only need to know these two bulges exist; no calculation.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="By adding: how much spring and neap tides differ">

Without a graph, split the tide into a Moon part plus a Sun part. Illustrative numbers: the Moon moves the sea $1$ m up and down, the Sun half as much, $0.5$ m.

- Spring tide (in a line): the two high tides fall together. High tide $1+0.5=1.5$, low tide $-1.5$, tidal range $1.5-(-1.5)=3$ m.
- Neap tide ($90°$ apart): the Sun's high tide falls on the Moon's low tide. High tide $1-0.5=0.5$, low tide $-0.5$, tidal range $1$ m.

The ratio is $3:1$, matching the curve above. Because the Moon's effect is bigger than the Sun's, they never cancel completely, so the range never drops to 0.

## Tides and tidal currents in the Taiwan Strait

As the sea rises and falls, the water also flows sideways: a **tidal current** (<Zh>潮流</Zh>). It runs one way while the tide comes in and the opposite way while it goes out, turning round about every six hours. Unlike last chapter's surface currents, which keep much the same direction for a long time, a tidal current reverses with the tide.

Tide data from the Central Weather Administration show that the tides differ along the Taiwan Strait. The usual textbook explanation: the tide behaves like a very long wave that enters the strait from both its northern and southern ends and meets in the middle. So the tidal range is large in the middle (near <Zh>臺中</Zh>, Taichung) and small at the northern end (near <Zh>基隆</Zh>, Keelung) and the southern end (near <Zh>高雄</Zh>, Kaohsiung). While the tide comes in, the current runs roughly south in the northern part of the strait and roughly north in the southern part, both towards the middle; while it goes out, the other way round.

<Perspective later title="Tidal power: why a large tidal range helps" needs="gravitational potential energy (physics: forms of energy, conversion and conservation)" href="/en/physics/s116-energy-conservation/">

Questions 50 to 53 of the 115 GSAT put tides, tidal power and electromagnetic induction in one item set (<Zh>題組</Zh>). One way to use the tide: at high tide, shut seawater into a basin; at low tide, let it flow out through a generator. The water's gravitational potential energy is proportional to its mass and to the height it falls. Double the tidal range and both the water shut in and its average fall double, so the energy becomes about 4 times as large. For generators, see the physics chapter [Magnetic effects of current and electromagnetic induction](/en/physics/s116-induction/).
