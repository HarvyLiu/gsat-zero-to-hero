This is an exam-skills chapter of the 116 GSAT sprint, for you if you studied required Earth science in school. It teaches no new Earth science. Instead it takes what the earlier chapters covered and practises how to read data, how to plot and how to write. Apart from the first chart, on how the paper awards points, every table and graph in this chapter is illustrative data, not a real record.

## Where Earth science sits in the Science paper

CEEC's 116 brochure gives the Science (<Zh>自然</Zh>) scope as required physics, chemistry, biology and Earth science (including inquiry and practice), with the four subjects carrying roughly equal shares of the points. On the 112 to 115 papers (the 116 layout is not yet confirmed):

- **Part 1** had 9 Earth science choice items every year (18 points), in a block whose position rotates by year; in 115 it was items 1–9.
- **Part 2** had one Earth science item set every year, plus one cross-subject set combining Earth science with another subject: physics in 112 (items 51–54), chemistry in 113 (items 50–53), biology in 114 (items 40–43) and physics in 115 (items 50–53, on tides and tidal power).
- The material often uses Taiwanese observation data: items 44–46 in 114 used typhoon forecast data from the Central Weather Administration (<Zh>中央氣象署</Zh>), and items 50–53 in 115 used tide data. The exam specification also says the material, and the background knowledge needed, may involve junior-high content.

The written items in Part 2 must **show your calculation or your reasons**. In 115 there were 8 written items, 4 points each, two per subject. The two Earth science ones were item 50 (plot tide data and work out the tidal range) and item 54 (tick one answer and give the reason in no more than 20 Chinese characters).

## Reading tables and plotting: tide data

A plotting item tests whether you put the data on the graph correctly; a calculation item tests whether you get the right result from the data. Here is half a day's record from a tide station (illustrative data):

| Time (h) | Tide level (m) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.7 |
| 2 | 1.3 |
| 4 | 0.7 |
| 6 | −0.5 |
| 8 | −1.1 |
| 10 | −0.5 |
| 12 | 0.7 |

When you plot, do four things:

1. **Label each axis with the quantity and its unit**: "time (h)" across, "tide level (m)" up.
2. **Use an even scale** whose range covers all the data, with **negative tide levels below 0**.
3. **Plot every point in the right place.**
4. **Join the points.** The tide level changes continuously and smoothly, so a smooth curve is closer to the real change than a zigzag of straight segments; if the question says how to join them, do what it says.

Drag $t$ to 7. The table has no reading at 7 h: what tide level do you read off the curve? Joining the 6 h and 8 h points with a straight line would give $\frac{-0.5+(-1.1)}{2}=-0.8$ m at 7 h. The smooth curve gives about −0.94 m, lower, because the curve is rounded near low tide.

Then write the working. In this half day the high tide is at 2 h and the low tide at 8 h:

$$
\text{tidal range}=\text{high-tide level}-\text{low-tide level}=1.3-(-1.1)=2.4\ \text{m}
$$

Write down the formula and the numbers you put in, so the marker can see you kept the minus sign.

<Perspective title="On a number line: the tidal range is the distance between two points">

You can also see the tidal range as a distance. Mark the high tide and the low tide on an upright tide gauge, and the tidal range is how far apart the two points are. Below, the gauge is drawn lying down.

<NumberLine desc="A number line of tide level from −2 to 2 m, with a tick every metre. There is a point at the low tide, −1.1 m, and one at the high tide, 1.3 m. The stretch from the low tide to 0 is shaded blue, and the stretch from 0 to the high tide is shaded red." from={-2} to={2} step={1}
  intervals={[{ a: -1.1, b: 0, color: 'blue' }, { a: 0, b: 1.3, color: 'red' }]}
  points={[{ x: -1.1, label: 'low' }, { x: 1.3, label: 'high' }]} />

Read the two lengths: the blue stretch from the low tide to 0 is 1.1 m long, and the red stretch from 0 to the high tide is 1.3 m. The points lie on either side of 0, so the distance is the sum of the two stretches, $1.1+1.3=2.4$ m, the same as by subtraction. This also shows why "subtracting a negative number adds its size" makes sense. Only when the high and low tide are on the same side of 0 is the distance the difference of their sizes.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="When I plot, must I join the points with a smooth curve? Will I lose points for joining them with a ruler?">

Follow the question first: if it says how to join the points, join them that way. If it doesn't, think about how the quantity changes. The tide rises and falls continuously and smoothly, so a smooth curve is closest to the real change; as with reading the 7 h level above, a curve and a zigzag give different readings. Whichever way you join them, the positions of the points and the quantities and units on the axes must be right. That is the basic requirement of any plot.

</StudentAsks>

<TryIt>

Mark each of the five statements "supported", "contradicted" or "not covered" yourself before reading on.

</TryIt>

Taking them one by one:

- (1) Supported: the July dot is highest, at 29 ℃.
- (2) Supported: highest 29 ℃, lowest 16 ℃, and $29-16=13$ ℃.
- (3) Contradicted: January and February are both 16 ℃, so February is not higher than January. A statement that says "every month" is contradicted by a single counterexample on the chart.
- (4) Not covered: the chart shows temperature only, not rainfall.
- (5) Not covered: the chart only tells you July is hottest, not why; besides, as you learned in junior high, the seasons are caused mainly by the tilt of the Earth's axis, not by the Earth–Sun distance.

So the answer is (1)(2). Multi-select items are scored like this: <Fact id="exam.multi-select-scoring" />

For example, if someone chooses (1)(2)(4), option (4) is judged wrong, $k=1$, and they earn $\frac{5-2\times1}{5}=\frac{3}{5}$ of the points.

<Perspective title="Turn each statement into a question you can check on the chart">

Another way to judge: first rewrite each statement as "what to look for on the chart". If you cannot rewrite it, the data does not cover it.

| Statement | The question to check on the chart | Result |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | Is the July dot the highest? | Yes |
| (2) | Is the highest dot minus the lowest dot 13 ℃? | Yes |
| (3) | From January to July, is any segment flat or going down? | Yes, January to February is flat |
| (4) | Does the chart show rainfall? | No, cannot be checked |
| (5) | Does the chart show the Earth–Sun distance? | No, cannot be checked |

This method is especially good at catching statements that "sound reasonable". A "because…" sentence like (5) often states a cause that is not on the chart.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="If I'm not sure about an option, isn't it safer to leave it unchosen?">

Not necessarily. The brochure says each option is judged on its own, so leaving an option unchosen is a judgment too: if that option is in fact correct, leaving it blank counts as one wrong judgment and costs as much as one wrong choice. No option can be skipped so that it doesn't count. It is better to go back to the data for evidence, judge every option as supported, contradicted or not covered as above, and then decide.

</StudentAsks>

## Word-limited reasons: choose the right tool and say why

Written items often ask you to make a choice first and then give the reason. A reason needs two things: **evidence** (a comparison you read or worked out from the data) and **a rule** (the Earth science rule that takes the evidence to the conclusion).

The setting (illustrative): a layer of volcanic ash is thought to be about 20,000 years old, and you want to confirm its age by <Term t="earth.radiometric-dating">radiometric dating</Term>. Two imaginary isotopes are available, X and Y (written <Zh>甲</Zh> and <Zh>乙</Zh> in the Chinese answers below): X has a <Term t="earth.half-life">half-life</Term> of 5000 years and Y a half-life of 1 billion years. This chapter assumes that a measurement is reliable only when between 1% and 99% of the parent is left.

<Predict id="ash-fraction" prompt="If you use X, what fraction of X is left after 20,000 years? Enter the denominator." answer={16}>

20,000 years is $20000\div5000=4$ half-lives, so $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^4=\tfrac{1}{16}$ is left, which is 6.25%: between 1% and 99%, so X gives a reliable age. What about Y? 20,000 years is only one fifty-thousandth of Y's half-life, so about 99.999% of Y is left. It has hardly decayed, and the change is too small to measure reliably.

</Predict>

So you tick "Y is unsuitable", and the reason is limited to 20 characters. Compare three answers (the exam is in Chinese, so they are given in Chinese with a translation):

- <Zh>乙不適合。</Zh> ("Y is unsuitable.") A conclusion only, with no reason.
- <Zh>因為半衰期的關係。</Zh> ("Because of the half-life.") Only a keyword: too long or too short? Compared with what? It doesn't say.
- <Zh>乙半衰期遠長於2萬年，幾乎未衰變</Zh> ("Y's half-life is far longer than 20,000 years; it has hardly decayed.") It first compares the half-life with the age (evidence), then states the consequence, "hardly decayed": a change too small to measure cannot date anything (the rule). 16 characters, punctuation included.

Item 54 of the 115 GSAT was this kind of item: tick one of several isotopes and give the reason in no more than 20 characters. CEEC's marking notes say about 60% of candidates ticked the right one, but about half left the reason blank or gave a wrong one.

<Perspective title="By a ratio: age divided by half-life">

Without a graph, work out one number: $r=\dfrac{\text{age}}{\text{half-life}}$, the number of half-lives that have passed. The fraction left is $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^r$ (some values in the table are rounded):

| $r$ | $\tfrac{1}{1000}$ | $\tfrac{1}{50}$ | $1$ | $4$ | $6$ | $7$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Left (%) | 99.93 | 98.6 | 50 | 6.25 | 1.6 | 0.8 |

Under this chapter's 1%-to-99% assumption, any $r$ between about $\tfrac{1}{50}$ and $6$ gives a reliable age. A very small $r$ means the half-life is too long, so hardly anything has decayed; a very large $r$ means the half-life is too short, so no parent is left. For the ash: X has $r=4$, which works; Y has $r=\tfrac{20000}{10^9}=\tfrac{1}{50000}$, far smaller even than $\tfrac{1}{1000}$, which does not. This ratio is also exactly the evidence your reason should state: which is bigger, the half-life or the age, and by how much.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="The limit is 20 characters. What if I can't fit my reason in?">

First cut anything that restates the question, such as "from the data in the question we know" or "so I think": you don't need it. Keep two things: the comparison (the half-life is far longer, or far shorter, than the age) and the consequence (hardly decayed, or long since decayed away). Write numbers in digits: <Zh>2萬年</Zh> is shorter than <Zh>兩萬年左右</Zh>. When you have finished, count the characters.

</StudentAsks>

## Relations in a picture: ordering a cross-section

A cross-section is data too, and its evidence is "what lies on top of what, and what cuts through what". Recall the two principles from the chapter [The solar system, habitable Earth and geologic time](/en/earth/s116-earth-history/): the <Term t="earth.law-of-superposition">law of superposition</Term> says that in strata that have not been overturned, lower layers are older than upper ones; the principle of cross-cutting says that a rock body that cuts through other rocks (a dyke or a fault, for example) is younger than the rocks it cuts.

<Graph desc="A schematic rock cross-section. A (blue) and B (amber) are two layers of sedimentary rock. A slanting fault F cuts up and to the right from the bottom through A and B, and A and B on the right of the fault sit a little lower than on the left. As the reason in a written item, it is again evidence, rule, conclusion: "F cuts A and B but not C (evidence); whatever cuts is younger, and whatever lies on top is younger (rule); so F is younger than B and older than C (conclusion)."

<Perspective title="Peeling back from the youngest">

Another way to order the events skips listing the relations one by one and plays history backwards. Each time, find an event that **nothing cuts through and nothing covers**: it is the youngest of the events left. Remove it, then find the next one.

1. D reaches the surface, and nothing cuts or covers it: D is the youngest. Remove D.
2. Now C, at the top, is not cut by anything: C is next. Remove C.
3. The upper end of F is now exposed, and nothing cuts F: F comes before that. Remove F.
4. That leaves B lying on A: B is younger than A.

Reversed, oldest to youngest, that is A, B, F, C, D, the same as the answer in the dialogue. If at some step two events could be removed at once, the data is not enough to give a single order.

</Perspective>

Answer: after removing C, both D and F are exposed, and neither cuts the other, so either could be removed. The data only says that D and F are both younger than B and older than C; it cannot decide between D and F. This is another case of "not covered by the data": your answer should say "cannot be determined" instead of forcing an order.
