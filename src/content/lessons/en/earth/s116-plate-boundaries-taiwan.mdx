This is a 116 sprint review: it assumes you studied required Earth science in school and remember from junior high that earthquakes and volcanoes are mostly found along plate boundaries. The curriculum leaves out kinds of volcanic eruption here, and for Taiwan it keeps to what the evidence supports: Taiwan lies on a convergent plate boundary, with two subduction zones nearby.

## Three kinds of plate boundary: look at the relative motion

The place where two plates meet is a plate boundary. By how the two plates move relative to each other, there are three kinds:

- **Divergent** <Zh>張裂型</Zh>: the two plates move apart.
- <Term t="earth.convergent-plate-boundary">Convergent plate boundary</Term>: the two plates move towards each other.
- **Transform** <Zh>錯動型</Zh>: the two plates slide horizontally past each other along the boundary, neither approaching nor separating.

### The evidence each boundary leaves

You cannot see the plates' relative motion, but it leaves evidence in the rocks and in the earthquakes. The curriculum compares four things: magmatism, metamorphism, geologic structures and focal distribution.

| | Divergent | Convergent (subduction) | Transform |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landform examples | mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys | trenches, island arcs, high mountain ranges | transform faults that offset mid-ocean ridges |
| Magmatism | yes: mantle material rises and forms magma, making new oceanic crust at ridges | yes: volcanoes on the overriding plate beside the trench | almost none |
| Metamorphism | not prominent | prominent: squeezing and burial, high pressure and temperature | not prominent |
| Geologic structures | tension: normal faults | compression: reverse faults, folds | sliding past: strike-slip faults |
| Focal distribution | shallow | shallow to deep, in a sloping band | shallow |

Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift are divergent. Two continental plates colliding is also convergent: the Indian plate colliding with the Eurasian plate pushed up the Himalayas.

<StudentAsks q="Mid-ocean ridges keep making new oceanic crust. Is the Earth getting bigger and bigger?">

No. New crust forms at divergent boundaries, while old lithosphere goes back into the mantle at the subduction zones of convergent boundaries. Made in one place, sunk in another, so the Earth's surface area stays about the same. This extends last chapter's sea-floor spreading: sea floor forms at mid-ocean ridges and disappears at trenches.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Read the table backwards: from evidence to boundary">

Exam questions often don't tell you the kind of boundary; they only give what was observed. Then use the table backwards, starting from the evidence that tells the kinds apart best:

1. Are there **deep foci**? If so, and they form a sloping band, only a convergent boundary with subduction does that.
2. If all the foci are shallow, look at the **structures**: normal faults mean divergent, reverse faults and folds mean convergent, strike-slip faults mean transform.
3. Then confirm with **magma**: transform boundaries have almost none.

Try one: a place has only shallow earthquakes, mainly strike-slip faults and no volcanoes. Step 1: no deep foci. Step 2: strike-slip faults point to transform. Step 3: no magma, which fits. So it is a transform boundary.

</Perspective>

## Subduction zones: a plate slanting into the Earth

When two plates converge and one of them is oceanic, the denser oceanic lithosphere slopes down beneath the other plate and goes back into the mantle. This zone is a <Term t="earth.subduction-zone">subduction zone</Term>. Where it meets the sea floor there is a deep trench.

The foci start shallow near the trench and **get deeper towards the overriding plate**, because they happen in and around the plate that slopes down. The volcano is on the overriding plate, some distance from the trench, and such volcanoes form a line parallel to the trench; in the ocean, such a line of volcanoes is an island arc.

<StudentAsks q="Why does the oceanic plate go under, and not the continental one?">

Oceanic lithosphere is denser than continental lithosphere, so when they meet the oceanic lithosphere sinks beneath. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so they squeeze each other and push up high mountains, like the Himalayas. When two oceanic plates meet, one subducts beneath the other.

</StudentAsks>

## Taiwan: on a convergent plate boundary

Taiwan lies between the Eurasian plate and the Philippine Sea plate. To decide what kind of boundary this is, use the method of the last section: look at the foci and the geologic structures.

```dialogue
T: First, use foci to judge one place. Off the north-east of Taiwan there is a trench, with the Philippine Sea plate to its south and the Eurasian plate to its north. There, the foci get deeper towards the north.
>> turn: q-dip
S: Foci deeper towards the north means the plate going down slopes down towards the north, and the overriding plate is to the north. So the Philippine Sea plate subducts beneath the Eurasian plate.
T: Exactly. South of Taiwan there is another subduction zone, but the other way round. Look at the map below.
```

### The evidence on Taiwan itself

Besides the two subduction zones nearby, the island of Taiwan itself carries the evidence of a convergent boundary, and each item matches the table above:

- **Geologic structures**: folds and reverse faults are everywhere on the island, all made by compression.
- **Metamorphism**: the Central Range <Zh>中央山脈</Zh> has wide areas of metamorphic rock, formed when rocks were squeezed and buried.
- **Landforms**: high mountain ranges, the result of squeezing and uplift.
- **Foci**: earthquakes are very frequent, mostly shallow beneath the island; the deeper foci are concentrated off the north-east.

Put together, these give the conclusion the curriculum asks for: Taiwan lies on a convergent plate boundary. The curriculum also points out that many models of the details of Taiwan's tectonics are still unsettled, and the GSAT scope stops here.

<StudentAsks q="Most earthquakes under Taiwan itself are shallow. Does it still count as a convergent boundary?">

Yes. Deep foci only occur where a plate slopes down, that is, in the two subduction zones nearby. The island is where the two plates squeeze and collide; the crust breaks under the squeezing, and most earthquakes happen in the shallower crust. You can't judge a boundary from one piece of evidence alone: the island's folds, reverse faults and metamorphic rocks all point to compression, so it is still convergent.

</StudentAsks>

## How big was the earthquake?

In an earthquake, the point where the rock starts to break is the focus <Zh>震源</Zh>, and the point on the surface right above it is the epicentre <Zh>震央</Zh>. Taiwan has frequent earthquakes. Besides shaking that damages buildings directly, they can trigger other hazards, such as landslides, soil liquefaction and fires, and a large earthquake under the sea can also cause a tsunami.

```dialogue
T: After an earthquake, the news reports how big it was. For the same earthquake, will the values reported for Hualien, near the epicentre, and for faraway Taipei be the same?
>> turn: q-mag
S: I thought both places would report the same number... but it depends which quantity: the one about the earthquake itself has only one value, while the one about how each place shook is different everywhere.
T: Right. To describe how "big" an earthquake is, there are two quantities that are often mixed up: magnitude and intensity.
```

- <Term t="earth.earthquake-magnitude">Earthquake magnitude</Term> describes how much energy the earthquake itself releases. **One earthquake has one magnitude**, wherever you are.
- <Term t="earth.earthquake-intensity">Earthquake intensity</Term> describes how hard the ground shakes at one place. **For the same earthquake, the intensity differs from place to place**: it usually gets smaller farther from the epicentre, and also depends on the depth of the focus and the local ground; soft soil, for example, often shakes harder than solid bedrock.

<StudentAsks q='The news said "maximum intensity level 4". Does that mean the earthquake was magnitude 4?'>

No. "Maximum intensity" is about the one station, out of all of them, where the shaking was strongest, and it uses the intensity levels; magnitude is a different quantity, with its own numbers describing the earthquake itself. In one earthquake report the magnitude is a single number, while the intensities at different places are listed in a table.

</StudentAsks>

Think of the earthquake as a light bulb in a room. The bulb has one wattage, like magnitude; how bright it is on your desk depends on how far you are from the bulb, like intensity.

## Earthquake early warning: racing the S waves

Last chapter you learned that among seismic waves, P waves are faster than S waves. The shaking from S waves is usually stronger than from P waves, and so is the damage. **Earthquake early warning** <Zh>地震預警</Zh> uses this time gap: as soon as seismic stations near the epicentre detect the P waves, the system uses data from several stations to work out the focus and the magnitude quickly, then sends a warning by electronic signals. Electronic signals are far faster than seismic waves, so places a bit farther away can get the warning before the S waves arrive.

Note: early warning is not prediction. It is issued after the earthquake has **already started**, racing to give notice before the strong shaking arrives. Question 31 of the 113 GSAT tested earthquake early warning; questions 7–8 of the 115 GSAT used the 311 earthquake as their setting and tested the timing of earthquake and tsunami warnings.
