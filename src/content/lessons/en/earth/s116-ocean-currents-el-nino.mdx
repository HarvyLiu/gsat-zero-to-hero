## Surface salinity: the water budget sets it

Seawater has many salts dissolved in it. **Salinity** <Zh>鹽度</Zh> is the number of grams of salts in 1000 g of seawater, written in ‰ (parts per thousand). For example, if 1000 g of seawater contains 34 g of salts, its salinity is 34‰.

Water enters and leaves the sea surface, but the salts almost all stay put:

- Evaporation takes away only water and leaves the salt behind, so salinity **rises**.
- Precipitation adds fresh water, so salinity **falls**.
- River input pours large amounts of fresh water into the sea, so salinity near a river mouth **falls**.

<StudentAsks q="The equator is the hottest place, so evaporation should be strongest there. Why isn't the highest surface salinity at the equator?">

Because salinity depends on the **difference** between evaporation and precipitation, not on evaporation alone. Near the equator there are many clouds and a lot of rain, which more than replaces the water lost to evaporation. Over the oceans at about 20° to 30° latitude the weather is clear and dry for most of the year, so evaporation far exceeds precipitation, and that is where surface salinity is highest.

</StudentAsks>

## Ocean temperature: with latitude and with depth

**Horizontally**: low latitudes receive more solar radiation per unit area, so the surface water is warmer there; the further towards high latitudes, the colder the surface water.

**Vertically**: the Sun mainly heats the top layer of the sea, and deeper down, where sunlight does not reach, the water is colder. In the open ocean at low latitudes there are often three layers from top to bottom:

- **Mixed layer** <Zh>混合層</Zh>: wind and waves stir the surface water evenly, so its temperature hardly changes with depth.
- **Thermocline** <Zh>斜溫層</Zh>: the temperature falls quickly with depth.
- **Deep layer** <Zh>深水層</Zh>: the water is cold and changes very little with depth.

<StudentAsks q="In winter the sea surface is colder. Does the mixed layer get thinner?">

Usually it gets thicker. In winter the wind and waves are stronger, and cold air keeps cooling the sea surface; the cooled surface water is heavier than the water below, so it sinks and mixes with it. Together, stirring and surface cooling carry the mixed layer deeper. In summer the wind and waves are weaker and the Sun warms the surface, so the mixed layer is usually thinner.

</StudentAsks>

## Surface currents: pushed along by the prevailing winds

The wind that blows most often in a region over a long time is its **prevailing wind** <Zh>盛行風</Zh>. Over the oceans:

- At low latitudes (about 0° to 30°) the **trade winds** <Zh>信風</Zh> blow: the northeast trades in the Northern Hemisphere and the southeast trades in the Southern Hemisphere, both blowing from east to west.
- At middle latitudes (about 30° to 60°) the **westerlies** <Zh>盛行西風</Zh> blow from west to east.

As the wind blows over the sea, friction drags the surface water along, forming **surface currents** <Zh>表面海流</Zh>. **The direction of surface currents is closely tied to the prevailing winds.**

<StudentAsks q="If currents are driven by the wind, do they flow in exactly the same direction as the wind?">

Not exactly. Surface currents broadly follow the prevailing winds, but a continent blocks and turns the water, and the Coriolis force from Earth's rotation also acts on moving water, so the current's direction differs somewhat from the wind's. The required course only asks you to link surface currents to the prevailing winds: west at low latitudes, east at middle latitudes, plus the continents that block the flow, and you can work out which way an ocean's loop turns.

</StudentAsks>

## Ocean–atmosphere interaction: dynamic and thermal

The ocean and the atmosphere constantly affect each other; this is **ocean–atmosphere interaction** <Zh>海氣交互作用</Zh>. It has two sides:

- **Dynamic** effects: the atmosphere pushes the water with a force. For example, the wind drives the surface water into currents, or blows the surface water away so that water from below rises to replace it.
- **Thermal** effects: the ocean and the atmosphere exchange heat and water vapour. For example, a warm sea surface heats the air above it and evaporates a lot of water vapour; the heated air rises, forming low pressure near the surface, and as the vapour rises and cools, clouds and rain form easily.

<TryIt>

Decide whether each of these is a dynamic or a thermal effect: (1) the westerlies drive the surface water at middle latitudes eastwards; (2) a warm sea surface makes the air above it warm and moist; (3) a wind along a coast blows the surface water away from the shore, and colder water from below rises up.

</TryIt>

(1) and (3) are the wind exerting a force on the water, so they are dynamic effects; (2) is the ocean passing heat and water vapour to the atmosphere, a thermal effect. The flow in (3), with water from below rising to replace the surface water, is called **upwelling** <Zh>湧升流</Zh>. The upwelled water is colder and rich in nutrients, so it often makes good fishing grounds.

## The equatorial Pacific: its usual pattern

The western side of the equatorial Pacific is around Indonesia and Australia, and the eastern side is off the west coast of South America. Normally:

- The trade winds blow from east to west and push the warm surface water to the west, where a large mass of warm water piles up and the warm layer is thick.
- In the east, the surface water is blown away and cold water wells up from below, so the surface is cooler and rich in nutrients, and fish catches are good.
- Over the warm western sea the air is heated and rises, with many clouds and much rain; over the cooler eastern sea the air sinks, and it is drier with little rain.

Both kinds of effect are at work here: the trade winds driving the water is a dynamic effect; the difference in sea temperature between east and west, which makes the air rise in the west and sink in the east, is a thermal effect.

```dialogue
T: That's the usual pattern. Now imagine a year in which the trade winds over the equatorial Pacific become much weaker.
T: What happens to the surface sea temperature in the eastern Pacific, off the west coast of South America? And which way does the cloudy, rainy region move?
>> turn: q-east
S: I thought that with weaker wind the east would get even colder... but with weaker wind, less surface water is blown away, so the cold water can't well up, and the warm water piled up in the west flows back east. So the east actually gets warmer.
T: Exactly. The warm water spreads east, and the region where the air rises and it rains a lot moves east with it. This is what we call El Niño.
```

Start at $w=1$ (normal): the red warm layer is thick in the west and thin in the east, and upwelling in the east brings cold water close to the surface. Drag $w$ to 0.2: how much thicker does the warm layer in the east become? How does the upwelling arrow change? Which way does "rainy" move? Then drag $w$ to 1.4, with trade winds stronger than usual: the east is colder and the rainy region further west. This opposite of El Niño is called La Niña <Zh>反聖嬰現象</Zh>.

<StudentAsks q="During El Niño the eastern Pacific gets warmer. Is that because the sunshine there is stronger that year?">

No. The warming comes from how the water moves, not from the sunshine. Once the trade winds weaken, the surface water in the east is no longer blown away in large amounts, and the cold water below cannot well up; the warm water that was piled up in the west spreads east. The surface water in the east is replaced by warm water, and that is why the sea temperature rises. This is a dynamic effect changing the sea temperature.

</StudentAsks>

| Year C | 28.9 | 23.3 | 5.6 |

Decide for yourself which year looks most like an El Niño year before you read on.

How to do it: compare each year with "normal". In Year B the east is $26.2-23.0=3.2$℃ warmer than normal while the west is $0.6$℃ cooler, and the east–west difference shrinks from 6.0℃ to 2.2℃. A warmer east and a smaller difference are exactly the result of weaker trade winds and warm water moving east, so Year B looks most like an El Niño year. In Year A the east is colder than normal and the difference larger, more like a La Niña year with stronger trade winds; Year C differs only a little from normal and shows no clear anomaly.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Draw it as a loop: dynamic and thermal effects reinforce each other">

Once El Niño starts, it often strengthens itself. Join the causes and effects into a loop:

<Graph desc="A triangular loop diagram. The arrow from weaker trades to east warms is labelled dynamic; the arrow from east warms to smaller pressure gap is labelled thermal; the arrow from smaller pressure gap back to weaker trades is labelled less push." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 5]} height={240} axes={false}
  circles={[{ center: [2, 4], r: 0.25, color: 'ink' }, { center: [8, 4], r: 0.25, color: 'red' }, { center: [5, 1], r: 0.25, color: 'blue' }]}

  labels={[{ at: [0.3, 4.4], text: 'weaker trades' }, { at: [7.0, 4.4], text: 'east warms', color: 'red' }, { at: [4.05, 0.2], text: 'smaller', color: 'blue' }, { at: [3.35, -0.4], text: 'pressure gap', color: 'blue' }, { at: [4.2, 3.45], text: 'dynamic' }, { at: [7.0, 2.1], text: 'thermal' }, { at: [0.6, 2.1], text: 'less push' }]} />

Go round the loop: weaker trade winds mean weaker upwelling and warm water moving east, so the east warms and the east–west sea temperature difference shrinks, a result of the dynamic effect; the warm sea heats the air above it, so the rising air and low pressure move east and the east–west pressure difference shrinks (thermal); a smaller pressure difference pushes the trade winds less, and they weaken further. Each time round, the change is reinforced. Conversely, when the trade winds strengthen, the same loop makes the east colder and colder.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Does deep seawater move too?" needs="the advanced elective Earth science course (thermohaline circulation; not on the GSAT)">

The currents in this chapter are all surface currents driven by the wind. The density of seawater depends on its temperature and salinity: the colder and saltier, the denser. The large-scale circulation through the deep ocean driven by density differences is called the thermohaline circulation; it belongs to the elective Earth science course and is outside the GSAT.

</Perspective>
