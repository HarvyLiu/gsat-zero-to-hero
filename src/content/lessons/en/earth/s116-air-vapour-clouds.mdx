The curriculum covers only the **basic causes** of how temperature and pressure change with height. It does not cover supersaturation, atmospheric stability, or rules for how fast rising air cools. ## Pressure: the weight of the air above

Air has weight. The air pressure at a given height can be seen as the weight, per unit area, of the whole column of air from that height upwards. The higher you go, the less air there is above you, so the lower the pressure.

Air can also be compressed. The lower air is squeezed by the air above it, so it is denser; the upper air is thinner. So near the ground, rising just a little leaves a lot of air below you, and the pressure drops fast; higher up, the pressure drops more and more slowly.

<StudentAsks q="Air pressure is the air above pressing down. So why is the crisp bag pushed outwards?">

Gas pressure acts in every direction, not only downwards. When the bag is sealed on the plains, the air inside is at the plains' pressure. Up the mountain the pressure outside drops while the pressure inside does not, and the difference pushes the bag outwards until inside and outside roughly balance.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="A pile of quilts: pressure tells you how much air is still above">

Think of the atmosphere as a tall pile of quilts. The bottom quilt is squashed flattest, because every quilt above presses on it. The pressure on any layer is proportional to the total weight of the quilts above it.

Air works the same way: **the pressure at a height, as a fraction of the ground pressure, is about the fraction of the atmosphere's mass that lies above that height.** With the illustrative numbers above, the pressure at 5.5 km is 500 hPa, half the ground value, so half of the atmosphere's mass lies below 5.5 km. Most of the atmosphere's mass is crowded near the ground.

Where the picture breaks: quilts lie still, but air molecules dash about all the time, which is why air pressure acts in every direction.

</Perspective>

## Temperature: follow the heat source

Pressure falls all the way up, but temperature does not. From junior high, the atmosphere is divided into four layers by how its temperature changes with height. From the bottom up they are the troposphere <Zh>對流層</Zh>, the stratosphere <Zh>平流層</Zh>, the mesosphere <Zh>中氣層</Zh> and the thermosphere <Zh>增溫層</Zh>.

<TryIt>

Follow the blue zigzag from the bottom up and find the two layers where temperature **rises** with height. Then think: where might the heat in those two layers come from?

</TryIt>

The answer is the stratosphere and the thermosphere. All four layers can be explained by asking where the heat comes from:

- **Troposphere**: most sunlight passes through the air and is absorbed by the ground; the warmed ground then heats the air touching it. The heat source is below, so it gets colder with height. (Being a few kilometres closer to the Sun hardly matters next to the Earth–Sun distance.) Warm below and cold above, the air overturns easily, and almost all the water vapour is in this layer, so weather such as cloud and rain happens mainly here.
- **Stratosphere**: the <Term t="chemistry.ozone-layer">ozone layer</Term> here absorbs the Sun's ultraviolet light and warms up, so temperature rises with height.
- **Mesosphere**: there is little ozone here and it absorbs hardly any sunlight, so temperature falls with height again.
- **Thermosphere**: the very thin gas absorbs high-energy solar radiation directly, so temperature rises with height.

<Perspective title="No graph needed: derive the four layers from how they are heated">

Remember one rule: **a layer heated from below gets colder with height; a layer heated inside itself, by absorbing sunlight that comes from above, gets warmer with height.** Write down how each layer is heated:

| Layer | How it is heated | Which kind | Temperature with height |
|---|---|---|---|
| Troposphere | the ground absorbs sunlight and heats the air | from below | falls |
| Stratosphere | ozone absorbs UV | sunlight absorbed inside | rises |
| Mesosphere | absorbs hardly any sunlight; the warmer stratosphere is below | from below | falls |
| Thermosphere | thin gas absorbs high-energy radiation | sunlight absorbed inside | rises |

From this table alone you can draw the zigzag: falls, rises, falls, rises. Turned round, a profile's slope in each layer tells you how that layer is heated.

</Perspective>

## Saturation vapour pressure: temperature sets the ceiling

Take a cold drink can out of the fridge and its outside is soon covered in drops. The drops do not leak out of the can; they come from water vapour in the air. To explain them, start with the ceiling on water vapour.

Water vapour in the air exerts its own pressure, called the **vapour pressure** <Zh>水氣壓</Zh>; it is part of the air pressure. At a given temperature the vapour pressure has a maximum, called the <Term t="earth.saturation-vapour-pressure">saturation vapour pressure</Term>. When the vapour pressure reaches it, the air is **saturated** <Zh>飽和</Zh>; any more vapour condenses into tiny drops, or below 0 ℃ may deposit straight into ice crystals. **The higher the temperature, the higher the saturation vapour pressure.**

| Temperature (℃) | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 | 35 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturation vapour pressure (hPa) | 6 | 9 | 12 | 17 | 23 | 32 | 42 | 56 |

This table is illustrative data in whole numbers; every example here uses it.

<Perspective title="By the ceiling: the same vapour at another temperature">

A worked case with the table (illustrative): some air has a vapour pressure of 23 hPa. At 35 ℃ its ceiling is 56 hPa, so 23 hPa is far below it and the air is unsaturated. Cool it to 10 ℃ and the ceiling is only 12 hPa: 23 hPa is 11 hPa over the ceiling, so the extra vapour must condense until the vapour pressure is back down to 12 hPa.

The vapour has not increased at all; the ceiling has dropped. That is where the drops on a cold can come from: the air against the can is cooled until its ceiling falls below its vapour pressure.

The usual sponge picture (a warm sponge soaks up more) helps you remember "warmer means a higher ceiling", but it breaks here: the ceiling is set by temperature; air does not "hold" vapour like a container.

</Perspective>

## Dew point: cooled until just saturated

Take some unsaturated air and lower its temperature while its water vapour stays the same: its saturation vapour pressure falls. At some temperature the saturation vapour pressure exactly equals its vapour pressure, and the air is just saturated. That temperature is its <Term t="earth.dew-point">dew point</Term>.

<Predict id="p-dew-point" prompt="Illustrative: air at 30 ℃ has a vapour pressure of 17 hPa. Using the illustrative table above, about what is this air's dew point in ℃?" answer={15} unit="℃">

Look in the table for the temperature whose saturation vapour pressure is 17 hPa: 15 ℃. This air is saturated as soon as it cools to 15 ℃, so its dew point is about 15 ℃. Notice that the dew point depends only on the vapour pressure, not on the air being at 30 ℃ now.

</Predict>

Set $a$ to 30 and $v$ to 17 and check the answer above. Then drag only $a$: does the red point move? It does not. **The dew point is set by the amount of water vapour alone**: the more vapour (the larger $v$), the higher the dew point.

In dew-point terms: a cold can cools the air touching it below its dew point, and the extra vapour condenses on the can. On a clear night the ground loses heat and cools, the air against it cools to its dew point, and dew forms on the grass; if the dew point is below 0 ℃, the vapour deposits straight into frost.

<Perspective title="Measure the dew point with a cup of ice water">

You can also measure the dew point directly. Put water in a metal cup, add ice a little at a time while stirring, and read the water temperature. At the moment the outside of the cup **first** mists over, the air next to the cup has just cooled to saturation, so the water temperature then is about the dew point.

Example: a room is at 25 ℃, and the cup starts to mist at 10 ℃. From the table, the saturation vapour pressure at 10 ℃ is 12 hPa, so the room air's vapour pressure is about 12 hPa. Compared with 32 hPa, the saturation vapour pressure at 25 ℃, the air is still far from saturated.

This method runs the curve backwards: the curve goes "know the vapour pressure, find the dew point"; the ice cup goes "measure the dew point, work back to the vapour pressure".

</Perspective>

## Relative humidity and absolute humidity

"Still far from saturated" can be made precise with a ratio:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\text{relative humidity}\\&\quad=\frac{\text{actual vapour pressure}}{\begin{array}{c}\text{saturation vapour pressure}\\\text{at the same temperature}\end{array}}\times100\%\end{aligned}
$$

This is the <Term t="earth.relative-humidity">relative humidity</Term>, and it says how close the air is to saturation. For example, air at 30 ℃ with a vapour pressure of 17 hPa has a relative humidity of $\frac{17}{42}\times100\%\approx40\%$. Saturated air has a relative humidity of 100%.

The other quantity is the **absolute humidity** <Zh>絕對濕度</Zh>: the mass of water vapour in each cubic metre of air, in g/m³. It says how much vapour there actually is.

```dialogue
T: At dawn the temperature is 15 ℃ and the relative humidity 90%. By noon the temperature has risen to 25 ℃, and the amount of water vapour in the air has not changed. What happens to the relative humidity at noon?
>> turn: q-noon
S: The vapour hasn't changed, so the top of the fraction hasn't... but the temperature has risen, so the saturation vapour pressure on the bottom is bigger. The relative humidity goes down.
T: Right. Over a day, relative humidity is often high at dawn and low in the afternoon, even though the air has not lost any vapour at all.
```

<Perspective title="Work it out by mass: absolute humidity and the saturation vapour amount">

Relative humidity can also be worked out by mass. Call the mass of water vapour in each cubic metre of saturated air the **saturation vapour amount** <Zh>飽和水氣量</Zh>; it also grows with temperature (illustrative data, rounded):

| Temperature (℃) | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 | 35 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saturation vapour amount (g/m³) | 5 | 7 | 9 | 13 | 17 | 23 | 30 | 40 |

$$
\begin{aligned}&\text{relative humidity}\\&\quad=\frac{\text{absolute humidity}}{\begin{array}{c}\text{saturation vapour amount}\\\text{at the same temperature}\end{array}}\times100\%\end{aligned}
$$

At the same temperature the mass of vapour is proportional to the vapour pressure. For the earlier air at 30 ℃, 17 hPa is $\frac{17}{42}$ of the 42 hPa ceiling, so its vapour is also about $\frac{17}{42}$ of the 30 g/m³ ceiling: an absolute humidity of about $30\times\frac{17}{42}\approx12$ g/m³, and a relative humidity of $\frac{12}{30}\times100\%=40\%$, the same as from the vapour pressures. Reading 13 g/m³ at the 15 ℃ dew point instead gives about 43%: that is the value after the air has cooled to 15 ℃ and shrunk, so it is only an approximation.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="During a cold surge the relative humidity is 90%; on a summer afternoon it is only 60%. So winter air holds more water vapour?">

Not necessarily, and in this example it is the other way round. Using the saturation vapour amount table above: in the cold surge at 10 ℃, air at 90% holds about $0.9\times9\approx8$ g/m³; on the summer afternoon at 30 ℃, air at 60% holds about $0.6\times30=18$ g/m³, more than twice as much. Relative humidity says how close the air is to saturation; absolute humidity says how much vapour there is.

</StudentAsks>

The curriculum suggests estimating relative humidity with a **wet-and-dry-bulb thermometer** <Zh>乾濕球溫度計</Zh>. It has two thermometers: the dry bulb measures the air temperature; the wet bulb is wrapped in wet gauze, and the evaporating water carries heat away, so it reads lower. The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the bigger the gap between them; in saturated air there is no net evaporation, and the two read the same.

<TryIt>

At place A the dry bulb reads 28 ℃ and the wet bulb 27 ℃; at place B the dry bulb reads 28 ℃ and the wet bulb 21 ℃. Where is the air drier? If at place C both thermometers read 24 ℃, what is the relative humidity there?

</TryIt>

B is drier: at the same air temperature, its two thermometers differ by more. At C the two are equal, so the air is saturated and the relative humidity is 100%.

## Rising air: it expands, cools and forms cloud

```dialogue
S: Rising air gets colder because it's colder up there anyway, so the cold air around it chills it, right?
T: If the surroundings only chilled it slowly, a parcel of air would take a long time to cool. The real reason lies in the parcel itself. Remember from the first section: the higher you go, the lower the pressure.
S: So as it rises, the pressure outside drops, and it expands...
T: Yes. To expand it has to push the surrounding air aside, which uses up its own energy, so its temperature falls. It has almost no time to exchange heat with its surroundings, so we treat it as adiabatic.
```

This is **adiabatic expansion cooling** <Zh>絕熱膨脹冷卻</Zh>: rising air cools because it expands, not because it meets colder air. The other way round, sinking air is compressed and warms. Air rises for many reasons, for example when the ground heats it or when it is pushed up a mountain slope.

As it rises, the air's vapour pressure also falls because of the expansion, so its dew point falls a little too, but much more slowly than its temperature. At the height where the temperature catches up with the dew point, the air is saturated and the vapour starts to condense into tiny drops or deposit into ice crystals: this is the bottom of the cloud, the **cloud base** <Zh>雲底</Zh>. How fast things cool will be given in the question.

<StudentAsks q="Is a cloud just a big mass of water vapour?">

No. Water vapour is an invisible gas. You can see a cloud because the vapour has condensed into countless tiny drops, or deposited into tiny ice crystals. Fog is the same, except that it sits on the ground: on a clear, calm night the ground loses heat and cools, the air against it cools to its dew point, and fog forms. Cloud comes from air rising, expanding and cooling; fog often comes from air cooling against the ground; both are "cooled to the dew point, so the vapour condenses".

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Above the cloud base, does the rising air keep cooling?" needs="Elective Earth science (atmospheric stability)">

Above the cloud base the air keeps rising, keeps expanding and cooling, and its vapour keeps condensing. Condensation releases heat, so air inside the cloud cools more slowly than below the cloud base. Whether the air keeps rising depends on whether it is warmer or colder than the air around it, which belongs to atmospheric stability in the elective course, outside the required course. The required course only asks you to find, from the data given, the height where temperature equals dew point, as Question 34 of the 113 GSAT did.

</Perspective>
