## A change in speed means a new layer

How fast a wave travels is set by the medium; this is its <Term t="physics.wave-speed">wave speed</Term>. Seismic waves are no different: when the physical state of the medium (solid or liquid, hard or soft) or its chemical composition changes, the wave speed changes too. So if you plot seismic wave speed against depth, **a depth where the speed changes suddenly is a boundary between two layers**.

Drag the dashed line d slowly from 0 to 6350 and read the two lines as you go: at what depth does the red S line suddenly vanish? What do the P waves do at the same depth? Further down, where do S waves appear again?

What you read off is the Earth's main layering:

- From the surface down to about 2900 km (illustrative), both waves mostly get faster. The very thin layer at the top is the crust; the great stretch below it is the <Term t="earth.mantle">mantle</Term>.
- At the bottom of the mantle the S waves suddenly vanish and the P waves suddenly slow down: here the outer layer of the core begins, called the outer core. S waves cannot cross a liquid, so **the outer core is liquid**.
- Further down the P waves suddenly speed up again, and the graph shows S waves again: this is the inner core, which is solid.

<StudentAsks q="P waves slow down in the outer core too. Why do we say the outer core is liquid, and not just a softer solid?">

The key is that the S speed becomes zero, not just smaller. In a softer solid S waves still get through, only more slowly; only in a liquid can S waves not travel at all. P waves travel through both solids and liquids, so in the outer core they only slow down and do not vanish. To decide whether a layer is liquid, look at the S waves, not the P waves.

</StudentAsks>

Reading the graph: the first boundary is at 35 km (illustrative): both waves suddenly speed up as they pass from the crust into the mantle. The second feature is deeper: below about 100 km both waves become a little slower. This stretch is called the low-velocity zone.

- The low-velocity zone is the <Term t="earth.asthenosphere">asthenosphere</Term>. Its rock is softer and can flow slowly over very long times.
- Above the asthenosphere, the crust together with the uppermost mantle forms the rigid <Term t="earth.lithosphere">lithosphere</Term>.

**The bottom of the lithosphere is not the bottom of the crust**: the lithosphere also includes a slice of the mantle.

## Continental drift: Wegener's evidence

In the 1910s the German scientist Alfred Wegener <Zh>韋格納</Zh> proposed the idea of <Term t="earth.continental-drift">continental drift</Term>: long ago today's continents were joined in one huge supercontinent, which later broke apart, and the pieces drifted to where they are now. The evidence he gave included:

- The coastlines on the two sides of the Atlantic (such as the east coast of South America and the west coast of Africa) have matching shapes and can be fitted together.
- The same kind of fossil organism is found on both sides, an organism that could not have crossed an ocean.
- The rock layers and mountain belts on the two sides join into one belt once the continents are fitted together.
- Traces of ancient glaciers appear in places that are warm today, which only makes sense if the continents were once together.

## Sea-floor spreading: evidence from the sea floor

In the mid-20th century, large-scale ocean surveys found that the ocean floors have very long undersea mountain ranges, called mid-ocean ridges. In the early 1960s, scientists proposed the theory of <Term t="earth.sea-floor-spreading">sea-floor spreading</Term>: mantle material rises at mid-ocean ridges, cools to form new sea floor, and the new sea floor then moves away to both sides.

The idea predicted several things that could be tested, and all of them were later found on the sea floor:

1. **Magnetic stripes are mirror images on the two sides.** The direction of the Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times in the past. When new sea-floor rock cools, it "records" the direction of the field at that time. Because the sea floor forms and moves away to both sides at once, the rocks on the two sides of the ridge record the field as stripes that mirror each other.
2. **Sea-floor rock is older the farther it is from the ridge.**
3. **Sea-floor sediment is thicker the farther it is from the ridge**, because the sea floor there has existed longer and has had more time to collect sediment.

## Plate tectonics: joining up the evidence

In the late 1960s, scientists brought together the evidence from seismic waves, the sea floor and the land, and built the theory of <Term t="earth.plate-tectonics">plate tectonics</Term>:

- The lithosphere is divided into many pieces, called plates. The plates move on top of the asthenosphere.
- Mid-ocean ridges are where two plates separate and new lithosphere forms.
- As you learned in junior high, earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated in belts, and most of these belts lie at the boundaries between plates.

Here the layers from earlier come into play: what moves is the rigid lithosphere, and it can move because the asthenosphere beneath it is softer and can flow slowly.

<StudentAsks q="So do continents paddle their own way across the sea floor?">

No. Wegener pictured the continents themselves moving, which is why he could not say where the force came from. Plate tectonics says something different: a continent and the sea floor next to it often belong to the same plate, and the whole slab of lithosphere moves together on the asthenosphere. A continent is like a passenger riding on the plate; it does not have to push its way across the sea floor.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Read it backwards: explain Wegener's evidence with plate tectonics">

So far we went from evidence to theory. You can also go the other way: start from plate tectonics and explain each of Wegener's pieces of evidence in turn.

| Wegener's evidence | Explanation by plate tectonics |
|---|---|
| Matching coastlines | The two continents were once joined; a mid-ocean ridge formed between them, and sea-floor spreading pushed them apart, so the Atlantic appeared |
| Same fossils on both sides | When the organism lived, the two continents were still joined |
| Rock layers and mountains join into one belt | They are one rock layer or mountain belt, later split between two plates |
| Ancient glaciers in warm regions | The continents moved with their plates, from cold places to where they are now |
| (The gap) the force that moves continents | What moves is the whole plate; the asthenosphere is softer, which lets the lithosphere move on top of it |

The last row matters most: what plate tectonics supplied was exactly the link Wegener could not give.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="If sea floor keeps forming, does the Earth keep getting bigger?" needs="the types of plate boundary (next chapter)" href="/en/earth/s116-plate-boundaries-taiwan/">

No. Mid-ocean ridges keep forming new lithosphere, while at other plate boundaries old lithosphere sinks back into the mantle, and the two roughly balance. The kinds of plate boundary and the geological processes at each are the topic of the next chapter.

</Perspective>
