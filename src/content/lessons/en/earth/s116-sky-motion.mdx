## Diurnal motion: the celestial sphere turns once a day

Imagine a huge spherical shell centred on you, with every star stuck to it. This is the **celestial sphere** (<Zh>天球</Zh>). The ground blocks the lower half of the shell, so you see only the half above the horizon.

Earth rotates from west to east, about once a day. Standing on the ground, you do not feel yourself turning. What you see is the whole celestial sphere turning from east to west about an axis, about once a day. That axis is Earth's axis extended, and the two points where it meets the sphere are the north and south celestial poles. Polaris lies very close to the north celestial pole, so it hardly moves all night.

Every star, and the Sun and the Moon too, rises in the east and sets in the west each day as the sphere turns. This motion is called <Term t="earth.diurnal-motion">diurnal motion</Term>. Because the whole sphere turns together, every star turns through the same angle.

</Predict>

<StudentAsks q="Does Polaris stay still all night because it is the brightest star in the sky?">

No. Polaris hardly moves because it happens to lie very close to the north celestial pole, the point that Earth's axis extended points to. That has nothing to do with how bright it is. Last chapter showed that how bright a star looks is set by its luminosity and its distance; that has nothing to do with where it sits in the sky. Polaris is in fact not the brightest star in the night sky.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Let Earth turn instead: riding a merry-go-round">

Another way to see it is to keep the sphere still and let yourself turn. On a merry-go-round turning one way, you see the people and trees around you sweep past the **opposite** way, and when the ride turns once, the surroundings seem to go once round you.

Apply that to Earth, one step at a time:

1. Earth rotates from west to east, carrying you eastward.
2. So things in the sky seem to move the opposite way, from east to west: they rise in the east and set in the west.
3. Earth turns once in about a day, so the sphere seems to turn once in about a day too, about 15° per hour.
4. As you turn, the direction of Earth's axis stays the same, so the north celestial pole it points to seems to stand still, and every other star turns around it.

The two descriptions are of the same thing: "the sphere turns east to west" is how it looks from the ground, and "Earth turns west to east" is the real cause. The analogy only goes as far as the direction: a merry-go-round's axle is upright, but Earth's axis is tilted, and the next section needs that.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can you still see Polaris from the Southern Hemisphere?">

No. On the equator Polaris already sits on the horizon; go further south and the north celestial pole drops below the northern horizon. People in the Southern Hemisphere see the south celestial pole above their southern horizon, at an altitude equal to their southern latitude, and the stars turn around it. Because they face south to look at it, they see the stars turn clockwise around the south celestial pole, the opposite of the anticlockwise turning seen facing north in the Northern Hemisphere.

</StudentAsks>

## Annual motion: the sky at a fixed clock time drifts west

If you look at the same part of the sky at the same clock time every night, say 9 p.m., you find the stars a little further west each day. A few months later, a constellation that stood high in the south at 9 p.m. has moved over to the west; a while after that you cannot see it at night at all, because it has moved into the daytime sky. About a year later the sky at that clock time looks as it did at the start. This is the **annual motion** (<Zh>周年運動</Zh>) of the stars, and its cause is Earth's revolution round the Sun. That is why the night sky shows somewhat different constellations in each season.

<StudentAsks q="Doesn't Earth turn exactly once a day? Why do the stars still drift at a fixed clock time?">

Because Earth rotates and revolves at the same time. Our "day" is set by the Sun: from one moment of highest Sun to the next. During that time Earth moves a little further along its orbit, so it has to turn a little more than one full turn relative to the stars before the Sun is highest again. So at a fixed clock time the stars sit a little further west each day.

</StudentAsks>

## The Sun's daily path changes with the seasons

The Sun takes part in diurnal motion too: each day it rises in the east and sets in the west along a path parallel to the celestial equator. The difference is that the Sun's daily path moves north and south with the seasons. The cause is the axial tilt you met in junior high: Earth's axis is tilted to the plane of its orbit, about 23.5° from upright. On the four days the paths are:

- Spring and autumn equinoxes (<Zh>春分、秋分</Zh>): the Sun's path is the celestial equator. The Sun rises due east and sets due west, and day and night are about equally long everywhere.
- Summer solstice (<Zh>夏至</Zh>): the path moves about 23.5° north. The Sun rises north of east and sets north of west, and in the Northern Hemisphere the day is longer than the night; north of the Tropic of Cancer, the noon Sun on this day is the highest of the year.
- Winter solstice (<Zh>冬至</Zh>): the path moves about 23.5° south. The Sun rises south of east and sets south of west, the Northern Hemisphere has days shorter than nights, and the noon Sun is the lowest of the year.

"Noon" here means the local moment when the Sun is highest, and "equally long" is a rough statement that leaves out details such as the atmosphere.

You can also skip the figure and use one rule: north of the Tropic of Cancer, the noon altitude at the equinoxes is $90°-$ latitude; at the summer solstice add 23.5°, and at the winter solstice subtract 23.5°. At 45°N it fills in the whole table at once:

| Day | Sunrise | Sunset | Noon altitude (45°N) | Day and night |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spring and autumn equinoxes | due east | due west | $90°-45°=45°$ | about equal |
| Summer solstice | north of east | north of west | $45°+23.5°=68.5°$ | day longer |
| Winter solstice | south of east | south of west | $45°-23.5°=21.5°$ | day shorter |

South of the Tropic of Cancer the rule cannot be used as it stands: at 20°N on the summer solstice it would give $93.5°$, more than 90°. Then the subsolar point is north of the observer, and you need the next view instead.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="From space: where is the Sun overhead?">

At noon, the point where sunlight strikes the ground straight down is the subsolar point (<Zh>太陽直射點</Zh>): on the equator at the equinoxes, at 23.5°N (the Tropic of Cancer, <Zh>北回歸線</Zh>) at the summer solstice, and at 23.5°S (the Tropic of Capricorn, <Zh>南回歸線</Zh>) at the winter solstice.

<StudentAsks q="Is summer hotter because Earth is closer to the Sun then?">

No. The seasons are caused mainly by the tilt of Earth's axis. Around the summer solstice the noon Sun is higher in the Northern Hemisphere, so sunlight comes down more steeply and is more concentrated on each patch of ground, and the days are longer. The Southern Hemisphere's seasons are the opposite of the Northern Hemisphere's: if distance caused the seasons, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time.
