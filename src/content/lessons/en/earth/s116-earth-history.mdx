## The solar nebula hypothesis: the solar system began as a cloud

The <Term t="earth.solar-nebula">solar nebula</Term> hypothesis says that the solar system formed from a nebula (a molecular cloud) of gas and dust: the cloud contracted under gravity and became the solar nebula; most of its matter gathered at the centre and formed the Sun; the matter left over gathered into the planets and the other members of the solar system.

So **the Sun and the planets came from the same nebula**: it is not that the Sun came first and the planets then split off it.

<StudentAsks q="Were the planets thrown off the Sun after it had formed?">

No. By the solar nebula hypothesis, the planets gathered from the gas and dust of the nebula that did not end up at the centre. They came from the same nebula as the Sun; they are not pieces that broke off it.

<Perspective later title="How did the nebula turn into the solar system?" needs="elective Earth science (how the solar nebula evolved, and the evidence)">

The elective goes on to how the nebula evolved: as it contracted it spun faster and faster and flattened into a disc; most of the matter gathered at the centre and formed the Sun, and the matter left in the disc gathered into the planets. Spinning faster as it shrinks is like a figure skater who pulls in her arms during a spin and turns faster. The analogy covers only the spin rate: a skater does not flatten, but the nebula spreads out into a disc.

The elective also discusses the evidence for it. For example, all eight planets orbit the Sun close to one plane and in the same direction. If the planets grew out of one spinning disc, that is just the arrangement they would be left with.

</Perspective>

### The members of the solar system

The Sun sits at the centre of the solar system and holds by far most of its mass. Orbiting the Sun are:

- **Planets**: from the inside out, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
- **Moons**: they orbit planets; for example, the Moon is Earth's moon (a satellite).
- **Asteroids**: most of them lie in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Comets**: made mainly of ice and dust; near the Sun they grow a tail.

From junior high: the four inner planets are terrestrial planets, fairly small and made mainly of rock. The four outer planets are Jovian planets, large and made mainly of gas.

## Habitable Earth: liquid water, air and a magnetic field

Living things need liquid water. The curriculum asks you to compare Earth with the other terrestrial planets and see which conditions Earth has:

- **A suitable distance from the Sun**: the surface temperature lets most water stay liquid.
- **A suitable atmosphere**: it keeps surface temperatures from swinging wildly and keeps the surface warm through the <Term t="chemistry.greenhouse-effect">greenhouse effect</Term>; its ozone layer blocks most ultraviolet light.
- **A global magnetic field**: around Earth it forms a magnetosphere, which holds off most of the charged particles streaming from the Sun.
- **Enough mass**: its gravity can hold on to an atmosphere.

Look at temperature alone first. The three planets on the number line below are imaginary, the values are illustrative data, and each has a surface pressure of about 1 atm.

## How the air became what it is today

Now back to the opening question. Earth's atmosphere went through several stages:

1. **The early atmosphere**: soon after Earth formed, volcanoes gave off huge amounts of gas, mainly water vapour and carbon dioxide, with some nitrogen and almost no oxygen.
2. **The oceans form**: the surface slowly cooled, and the water vapour condensed and fell as rain, forming the oceans.
3. **Carbon dioxide falls**: a great deal of carbon dioxide dissolved in seawater, settled on the sea floor and became rocks such as limestone.
4. **Oxygen appears**: living things in the oceans that carry out <Term t="biology.photosynthesis">photosynthesis</Term>, such as cyanobacteria, gave off oxygen. At first the oxygen combined with iron dissolved in seawater and settled as layer upon layer of iron-rich rock, called banded iron formations; only after that did oxygen build up in the air. Roughly two billion years ago or a little more, the oxygen in the atmosphere rose markedly: the Great Oxidation Event.
5. **The ozone layer forms**: high in the atmosphere, oxygen formed the <Term t="chemistry.ozone-layer">ozone layer</Term>, which blocks most ultraviolet light, so life could move onto land.

In today's air, nitrogen makes up about four fifths and oxygen about one fifth. Nitrogen takes little part in reactions, so it built up into the main component. Questions 50 to 53 of the 113 GSAT were a joint chemistry and Earth science item set on exactly this: the Great Oxidation Event and banded iron formations.

## Relative age: what came first

Strata are a record of Earth's history. In junior high you learned rules for reading them; here are two of them first:

- **Original horizontality**: sediment settles in water layer by layer, and the layers start out roughly horizontal.
- The <Term t="earth.law-of-superposition">law of superposition</Term>: in layers that have not been overturned, a lower layer was deposited first and is older than the layers above it.

It is like a pile of newspapers with a new one put on top every day: the one at the bottom is the oldest. Where the analogy breaks: rock layers can be squeezed, bent and even overturned, so you must first make sure they are not overturned before using "older below, younger above".

The third rule is the principle of cross-cutting: **a body of rock that cuts other rocks (a dyke or a fault, for example) is younger than the rocks it cuts**. So in this figure the order from oldest to youngest is A, B, C, X. One Earth science sample item in the CEEC's exam specification combines superposition, cross-cutting and fossils to order strata.

### Fossils: matching strata from place to place

Some organisms lived only during one particular period; their fossils are called index fossils. Strata that contain the same index fossil formed at about the same time, even when the two places are far apart. So fossils also let you match strata from different places.

<StudentAsks q="Is a more weathered rock older?">

Not necessarily. How weathered a rock is depends mainly on the kind of rock and its surroundings, not on its age. The tools for putting rocks in order are superposition, cross-cutting and fossils.

## Absolute age: radiometric dating and half-life

Relative age says only what came first, not how many years ago. To get a number of years you need <Term t="earth.radiometric-dating">radiometric dating</Term>.

From chemistry: atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons are <Term t="chemistry.isotope">isotopes</Term> of each other (recap: [Atomic structure, isotopes and the periodic table](/en/chemistry/s116-atomic-structure/)). Some isotopes are unstable and decay by themselves into atoms of another kind: the atom before decay is the parent, and the atom produced is the daughter. The decay is regular: the time it takes for half of the parent atoms to decay is called the <Term t="earth.half-life">half-life</Term>. Each isotope has a fixed half-life, not changed by temperature or pressure.

When magma cools and minerals crystallise, the parent atoms are locked into the crystals, and the "clock" starts from that moment. That is why radiometric dating is used most often on igneous rocks.

<Predict id="eighth-left" prompt="An igneous rock contains an imaginary isotope with a half-life of 500 million years. Assume the rock had no daughter atoms when it formed. Now only one eighth of the original parent is left. About how many million years ago did the rock form?" answer={1500} unit="million years">

Each half-life leaves half the parent: $1\to\frac12\to\frac14\to\frac18$, so $\frac18=\left(\frac12\right)^3$ and 3 half-lives have passed. The age is about $3\times500=1500$ million years, or 1.5 billion years.

</Predict>

Check: at 1.5 billion years the parent is at 12.5%, one eighth. The curves cross at $x=T$, where parent and daughter are 50% each. Wherever you set $T$, the crossing is at one half-life.

<Perspective title="No graph, just a halving table">

The curve can also be turned into a table. Assume the rock had no daughter atoms when it formed; after $n$ half-lives:

| Half-lives passed, $n$ | Parent left | Daughter | Daughter : parent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $1$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| 1 | $\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac12$ | $1$ |
| 2 | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac34$ | $3$ |
| 3 | $\tfrac18$ | $\tfrac78$ | $7$ |
| 4 | $\tfrac{1}{16}$ | $\tfrac{15}{16}$ | $15$ |

The pattern: the parent left is $\left(\tfrac12\right)^n$, and the daughter-to-parent ratio is $2^n-1$. For example, if daughter : parent $=3:1$, the table gives $n=2$, and the age is two half-lives.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can carbon-14 be used to date a meteorite?">

No. The half-life of carbon-14 is about six thousand years, while meteorites are billions of years old: that is hundreds of thousands of half-lives, and the parent has long since dropped too low to measure. For dating you choose an isotope whose half-life suits the age: too short, and no parent is left; too long, and the change is too small to measure well. Question 54 of the 115 GSAT tested exactly this: pick out the one isotope, from several, that is unsuitable for dating meteorites, and write the reason.

</StudentAsks>

The sandstone lies on the lava flow, so it is younger than 300 million years; the dyke cuts the sandstone, so the sandstone is older than 200 million years. Sedimentary rocks are usually hard to date directly, so the igneous rocks below and across them "bracket" their age: relative and absolute age working together.

<Perspective title="What if it is not a whole number of half-lives?">

This uses logarithms from the maths chapter [Exponential and logarithmic functions; growth models](/en/math/s116-growth-models/). The fraction $r$ of the parent left and the time $t$ are linked by the exponential function $r=\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T}$. Taking logarithms of both sides gives

$$
t=T\log_2\frac{1}{r}
$$

For example, with $r=0.3$:

$$
\log_2\frac{10}{3}=\frac{1-\log3}{\log2}\approx\frac{0.523}{0.301}\approx1.74
$$

so $t\approx1.74T$.

</Perspective>
