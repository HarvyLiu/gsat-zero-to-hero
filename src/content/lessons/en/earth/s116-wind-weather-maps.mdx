## Pressure and isobars: the pressure gradient force

You can think of the air pressure at a place as the weight of the whole column of air above it pressing down. On a map, lines joining points of equal pressure are **isobars** (<Zh>等壓線</Zh>).

When two places have different pressures, the air feels a force pointing from high pressure to low pressure, called the <Term t="earth.pressure-gradient-force">pressure gradient force</Term> (PGF). It has two features:

- Direction: **perpendicular** to the isobars, from high pressure to low pressure.
- Size: for the same pressure difference, the shorter the distance, the bigger the force. So **the closer together the isobars, the bigger the pressure gradient force**, and usually the stronger the wind.

<Perspective title="Treat isobars as contour lines">

Another way to see it borrows the contour lines of a map. Think of pressure as "height": isobars are then like contour lines, and the pressure gradient force is like the downhill direction, always perpendicular to the contours and towards lower ground, and steeper where the contours are closer.

Try one: at place A, along the direction perpendicular to the isobars, pressure falls 4 hPa every 100 km; at place B it takes 200 km to fall 4 hPa. Where is the pressure gradient force bigger? A's "slope" is twice as steep as B's, so A's pressure gradient force is about 2 times B's.

Where does the analogy break? A ball on a slope rolls straight down; air does not blow straight into the low. The opening question already told you: another force is at work.

</Perspective>

## The Coriolis force: Earth's rotation turns the wind

The Earth rotates. Seen from the ground, air moving horizontally is gradually turned aside, as if a sideways force acted on it. This is the <Term t="earth.coriolis-force">Coriolis force</Term>. Its features:

- Direction: **perpendicular** to the motion. In the Northern Hemisphere it turns the air to the right of its direction of motion; in the Southern Hemisphere, to the left.
- It changes only the direction of motion, not the speed.
- Still air feels no Coriolis force; the faster the wind, the bigger the Coriolis force.

The size of the Coriolis force also depends on latitude: it is largest near the poles.

<Predict id="equator-coriolis" prompt="A parcel of air moves horizontally at a fixed speed. Call the size of the Coriolis force on it near the North Pole 1. What is the Coriolis force on the same air at the equator?" answer={0}>

The answer is 0. Air moving horizontally at the equator feels no Coriolis force, and the higher the latitude, the bigger the Coriolis force. So the same wind turns noticeably at high latitudes, and hardly turns at all near the equator.

</Predict>

<StudentAsks q="Is something really pushing the air when there is a Coriolis force?">

No. No object pushes the air. We watch from a rotating Earth, and from there the air's straight-line motion looks as if it is turning; drawing that effect as a force makes the analysis easier. So the Coriolis force appears only in the description "as seen from the ground", and it only changes the direction: it never makes the wind faster or slower.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Think of the Coriolis force on a turntable">

Picture a big disc that turns anticlockwise when seen from above. You stand at the centre and roll a ball straight outwards. Someone standing off the disc sees the ball move in a straight line. You, standing on the disc and turning with it, see the disc turn away under the ball, so the ball's path seems to bend to the right.

Seen from above the North Pole, the Earth also turns anticlockwise, so people in the Northern Hemisphere see moving air turn to the right. Seen from above the South Pole, the Earth turns clockwise, so in the Southern Hemisphere the turn is to the left. With this picture you don't have to memorise which hemisphere turns which way: just ask how the Earth turns when seen from above that pole.

</Perspective>

## Geostrophic wind: two forces in balance

High above the ground, friction can be ignored, and the air feels only the pressure gradient force and the Coriolis force. If the isobars are straight, the air is first pushed towards the low; as soon as it moves, the Coriolis force turns it to the right (in the Northern Hemisphere). The wind speeds up, and the Coriolis force grows, until the Coriolis force is equal in size and opposite in direction to the pressure gradient force. Then the two forces balance, and the wind blows **along the isobars**. Such a wind is a <Term t="earth.geostrophic-wind">geostrophic wind</Term>.

Back to the opening question: high to the south and low to the north, so the pressure gradient force points north. In the Northern Hemisphere the Coriolis force is to the right of the wind, and to cancel the pressure gradient force it must point south, so the wind blows east.

## Near-surface wind: add friction

Close to the ground, the air rubs against the surface. Friction acts **opposite** to the wind's motion and slows the wind. A slower wind means a smaller Coriolis force, which can no longer match the pressure gradient force, so the wind stops running parallel to the isobars and instead **crosses them at an angle, towards the low**. Now three forces balance: the Coriolis force and friction together exactly cancel the pressure gradient force.

<StudentAsks q="When the forecast says a 'north wind', does it mean the wind blows towards the north?">

No. A wind's direction is **where it comes from**. A north wind comes from the north and blows southwards; the eastward-blowing wind in the opening question is called a west wind. Arrows in a force diagram show where the wind blows to, so turn them round when you name the wind.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Without drawing forces: back to the wind, low on the left">

The force diagram can be packed into one sentence. In the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind, so that it blows from behind you:

- For the geostrophic wind high up, the low is straight to your left.
- For near-surface wind, which crosses the isobars towards the low, the low is to your front left.

Check with a force diagram: the pressure gradient force points north-west towards the low, and the wind is to its right, crossing the isobars at an angle, blowing exactly north. In the Southern Hemisphere, swap "left" for "right".

</Perspective>

## Highs and lows: convergence, divergence and weather

Now make the isobars closed loops. At the centre of a surface low the pressure is lowest, so all around it the pressure gradient force points to the centre; in the Northern Hemisphere the near-surface wind is turned to the right and also crosses towards the low, so it **turns anticlockwise and blows in towards the centre**. Air gathers near the centre: this is **convergence** (<Zh>輻合</Zh>). At the centre of a surface high it is the other way round: the wind **turns clockwise and blows outwards**, and the air spreads out: this is **divergence** (<Zh>輻散</Zh>).

Check: on the east side of a Northern Hemisphere low the wind blows roughly north, tilted a little towards the centre. In the Southern Hemisphere the low draws air in clockwise and the high pushes it out anticlockwise. **The sense of turning reverses between hemispheres, but "lows converge, highs diverge" holds in both.**

Convergence and divergence then decide the weather. At a low's centre, the air converging near the ground has nowhere to go but up; as last chapter showed, rising air expands and cools, and when it cools to its dew point, cloud forms. So **lows tend to bring cloud and rain**. At a high's centre, the air near the ground spreads out and air from above sinks to replace it; sinking air is compressed and warms up, moving further from saturation, so **highs mostly bring clear weather**.

<StudentAsks q="Why does sinking air in a high make rain unlikely?">

Cloud forms only when air cools to its dew point, and the main way air cools a lot is by rising. Air at the centre of a high is sinking; as it sinks it is compressed and warms, its relative humidity falls and its water vapour hardly condenses, so there is little cloud and little rain.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Use your right hand to tell rising from sinking">

Instead of drawing a cross-section, you can use your hand. In the Northern Hemisphere, hold out your right hand and curl your fingers the way the surface wind turns (seen from above):

- A low's wind turns anticlockwise; with your right fingers curling anticlockwise, your thumb points up: the air rises.
- A high's wind turns clockwise; you have to turn your right hand over for the fingers to curl clockwise, and then your thumb points down: the air sinks.

In the Southern Hemisphere use your left hand: a Southern Hemisphere low draws air in clockwise, and with your left fingers curling clockwise your thumb points up, again rising. So in either hemisphere your thumb tells you: **lows rise, highs sink.**

</Perspective>

## Weather maps: drawn from station data

Where does a surface weather map come from? Weather stations in many places observe pressure, wind, temperature and the weather at the same time; the data are gathered and plotted on a map, and points of equal pressure are joined into isobars.

When you read a weather map, look for these first:

- **High pressure**: closed isobars with pressure rising towards the centre, often marked <Zh>高</Zh> or H.
- **Low pressure**: closed isobars with pressure falling towards the centre, often marked <Zh>低</Zh> or L.
- **How close the isobars are**: the closer, the bigger the pressure gradient force and the stronger the wind.
- **Fronts** (<Zh>鋒面</Zh>): where cold and warm air masses meet. In junior high you learned that fronts bring cloud and rain; after a cold front passes the temperature drops, and the stationary front at the turn of spring and summer brings the plum rains (<Zh>梅雨</Zh>).
- **Typhoons**: a very strong low, with isobars that are close together and nearly circular.

<StudentAsks q="A mountain station's pressure is low to begin with. Won't that spoil the isobars?">

It would, so a surface weather map does not use the pressures the stations measure directly. Last chapter showed that pressure falls with height; compared directly, a high mountain station would always look like a low. So each station's pressure is first converted to sea-level pressure, and only then compared and used to draw isobars.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Look at a satellite cloud image instead">

Isobars tell you the pressure; a satellite cloud image photographs the clouds directly from space, with clouds showing white. Use the two together: near lows and fronts the air rises, and the cloud image often shows a large sheet or a long band of white cloud; near highs the air sinks, and the image mostly shows clear, cloudless areas.

Try one with the illustrative map above: if a cloud image were taken at the same time, what would the area near the high's centre look like? From "highs sink and diverge", it should have little cloud. A cloud image shows only clouds, not pressure, so it supplements the weather map; it cannot replace the station data.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Typhoons: an especially strong low" needs="how typhoons form and their structure (the typhoons chapter)" href="/en/earth/s116-typhoons/">

On a weather map a typhoon is a low with a very low central pressure and isobars that are close together and nearly circular. With isobars that close, the pressure gradient force is large, so the winds are strong; near the ground in the Northern Hemisphere it turns anticlockwise, like any other low. What a typhoon needs to form, and what it looks like inside, wait for the typhoons chapter.

</Perspective>
