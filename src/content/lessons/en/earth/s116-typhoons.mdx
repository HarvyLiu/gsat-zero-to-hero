## How a typhoon forms

A <Term t="earth.typhoon">typhoon</Term> is a very strong low that forms over warm tropical sea. It works like an engine, and its fuel is the water vapour that evaporates from the warm sea:

1. Near the centre of the low, the air near the ground blows in towards the centre (convergence), so it has to rise.
2. The rising air expands and cools; once it cools to its <Term t="earth.dew-point">dew point</Term>, the water vapour condenses into cloud and rain.
3. As you learned in junior high, water vapour releases heat when it condenses into water. That heat makes the air near the centre warmer and more ready to rise, so the pressure at the centre falls further and even more air blows in.

As long as the sea below keeps supplying warm water vapour, this cycle keeps running.

Check your answers: water vapour evaporates from the warm sea and is carried in by the inflowing wind towards the centre; on the way up it condenses into cloud and releases heat. Over cold sea, less water vapour evaporates, so the "fuel supply" step weakens first, and the typhoon weakens with it.

The curriculum lists three conditions a typhoon needs to form:

- **A warm sea surface**: it supplies plenty of water vapour and heat.
- **Enough Coriolis force**: it makes the inflowing air start turning.
- **A suitable atmospheric environment**: other conditions in the atmosphere must also be right. The curriculum only asks you to know that this condition exists, and this chapter does not go into it.

<StudentAsks q="The sea near the equator is warm too. Why don't typhoons form right on the equator?">

Fuel alone is not enough; the air also has to start turning. In the chapter "What drives the wind, and weather maps" you saw that horizontally moving air feels no <Term t="earth.coriolis-force">Coriolis force</Term> on the equator. Without it, the inflowing air rushes straight into the centre and soon "fills in" the low, so it never starts turning.

</StudentAsks>

With a Coriolis force, the inflowing air in the Northern Hemisphere is turned to the right and circles the centre anticlockwise, and only then can the low last and deepen. So **even though the sea at the equator is warm, typhoons do not form right on the equator**.

## The structure of a typhoon

Seen from above, a typhoon's isobars form nearly circular rings, with the pressure falling towards the centre, so **the pressure is lowest at the centre**. In the Northern Hemisphere the near-surface wind does what it does around any low: it **turns anticlockwise and blows in towards the centre at an angle**. In the Southern Hemisphere it turns the other way, clockwise, but still blows in towards the centre.

The strongest winds are in the ring just outside the centre; the centre itself has very light wind, almost calm, and this is the **eye** of the typhoon. The cloud and rain are arranged in spiral **rainbands** that curl in around the centre.

<StudentAsks q="The pressure is lowest at the centre, so shouldn't the pressure gradient force be biggest there? Why is the wind weakest at the centre?">

The size of the pressure gradient force depends on how close together the isobars are, that is, on how **fast the pressure changes**, not on how low the pressure is. Right at the centre the pressure has already bottomed out, and the pressure all around is almost the same, so the force pushing the air is small. That is why the eye has the lowest pressure but the lightest wind.

</StudentAsks>

## As a typhoon passes: how pressure and wind change

If a typhoon's centre passes directly over a weather station, what does the station record? Think it through from the structure: as the typhoon approaches, the pressure keeps falling, the wind grows stronger and the rainbands sweep over one after another; when the ring just outside the centre goes over, the wind is strongest; then the eye arrives, the wind suddenly becomes very light and the pressure reaches its lowest. After the eye has passed, the strong wind comes back and the pressure starts to rise.

<Predict id="eye-turn" prompt="In the Northern Hemisphere, a typhoon's centre passes directly over a weather station. Compare the strong wind at the station before the eye arrives with the strong wind after the eye has passed. By about how many degrees do their directions differ?" answer={180} unit="degrees">

By about 180 degrees: the wind direction is reversed. Before the eye arrives, the station is on one side of the centre; after it has passed, the station is on the other side. A wind that turns anticlockwise around the centre and also blows in towards it points in exactly opposite directions on the two sides of the centre.

</Predict>

<StudentAsks q="When the eye passes and the wind and rain suddenly die down, does that mean the typhoon has gone?">

No. The eye is only the centre. The strong winds on its other side arrive soon, and from the opposite direction. Going outside because you think the typhoon has gone is very dangerous.

</StudentAsks>

## Where a typhoon goes: the Pacific High

Once a typhoon has formed it does not wander about on its own. Like a leaf on a stream, it is carried along by the large-scale flow around it. The most important influence on the tracks of typhoons near Taiwan is a very large high-pressure system over the North Pacific called the **Pacific High** (<Zh>太平洋高壓</Zh>). In the chapter "What drives the wind, and weather maps" you saw which way the wind turns around a high in the Northern Hemisphere.

```dialogue
T: (Illustrative) Suppose a typhoon lies due south of the centre of the Pacific High and is carried along by the flow around the high.
S: Carried along means it moves with the wind there?
T: Yes. Roughly which way does it move next?
>> turn: q-track
S: Around a high in the Northern Hemisphere the wind turns clockwise. On the south side of the high, clockwise means westwards, so the typhoon moves west.
T: Exactly. Keep following the clockwise turn: on the west side of the high it turns north, and further on it turns north-east.
```

So typhoons mostly **travel clockwise around the edge of the Pacific High**. How far west the high reaches decides roughly where a typhoon turns, so the tracks differ too.

<Perspective title="Without a map: the high is on the typhoon's right">

You can also decide without drawing a map. A typhoon moves with the flow around the high, and from the chapter "What drives the wind, and weather maps": in the Northern Hemisphere, with your back to the wind, the low is on your left, so the high is on your right. In other words, **as a typhoon moves, the centre of the high is on its right-hand side**.

| Typhoon is to the high's | High is to the typhoon's | Typhoon moves (keeping the high on its right) |
|---|---|---|
| south | north | west |
| west | east | north |
| north-west | south-east | north-east |

Check the first row: the high is to the north, and to keep the north on your right you have to face west, so the typhoon moves west. That matches travelling clockwise around the high.

## A typhoon's impact on Taiwan: track, terrain and circulation

The same typhoon can bring very different wind and rain to different parts of Taiwan. How hard a place is hit depends mainly on three things:

- **Track**: where the centre passes, and how close.
- **Terrain**: high mountains run down the middle of Taiwan. When the wind blows against them, the air is forced up the slopes and cools into cloud, so **the windward side easily gets heavy rain**; on the far side of the mountains there is usually much less rain.
- **Circulation**: the typhoon's near-surface wind, turning anticlockwise and blowing inwards, decides what wind each place gets, and so which side is the windward side.

After landfall there is no warm sea below to supply water vapour, friction over land is large, and the mountains break up the near-surface circulation, so a typhoon usually weakens.

The rule from the chapter "What drives the wind, and weather maps" works for typhoons too: in the Northern Hemisphere near the ground, stand with your back to the wind and the low is ahead of you on your left. Turn it round: if you know where the typhoon's centre is, you can find the wind direction.

Take the centre north of Taiwan: for the north to be ahead of you on your left, you have to face roughly north-east. Your back is then to the south-west, so the wind comes from the south-west and the windward side is Taiwan's west side, which matches the figure above. In the Southern Hemisphere, swap "left" for "right".
