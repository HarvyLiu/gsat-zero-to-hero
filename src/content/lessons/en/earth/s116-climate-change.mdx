## Climate changes on different timescales

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place over a short time, such as this afternoon's temperature and rain; **climate** is the long-term average weather of a place. Climate change is about the second: the long-term average has changed.

The curriculum wants you to know that climate changes on many timescales, and that different drivers act over times of very different length:

- **Plate motion**: continents ride on the plates and move only a few centimetres a year, but over tens of millions of years a continent can travel from a cold zone to the tropics, and its climate changes with it.
- **Mountain building**: mountain ranges rise over millions of years, changing the height of the land and the paths of air flow, which affects temperature and rainfall nearby.
- **Solar activity**: the energy the Sun sends to Earth varies slightly, on shorter timescales, from years to centuries.

## Glacial and interglacial periods

A <Term t="earth.glacial-period">glacial period</Term> is a time when Earth's climate is colder and the ice at high latitudes and on high mountains spreads widely; the warmer time between two glacial periods, when the ice retreats, is an <Term t="earth.interglacial-period">interglacial period</Term>. In Earth's past, glacial and interglacial periods have taken turns again and again.

The total amount of water on Earth hardly changes, but water moves between the oceans and the ice on land. In a glacial period, water evaporated from the oceans falls at high latitudes and on high mountains, freezes and stays on land instead of flowing back to the sea; the oceans hold less water, so sea level falls. In an interglacial period the ice melts, the water flows back to the oceans, and sea level rises again.

The answer is $s=-80$ metres. In a glacial period a shallow strait can become land, and plants and animals on land can cross it and spread to the island; in an interglacial period sea level rises again, the strait reappears, and life on the island is cut off from the mainland once more.

Temperature changes also move where living things live. In a glacial period, cold-tolerant plants spread to lower latitudes and lower altitudes; as an interglacial period warms, they retreat to high latitudes or high mountains. In a table:

| | Glacial period | Interglacial period |
|---|---|---|
| Global mean temperature | Lower | Higher |
| Ice on land | Spreads widely | Retreats |
| Sea level | Lower; shallow sea floor may become land | Higher; the coastline moves inland |
| Where living things live | Cold-tolerant life spreads to lower latitudes and altitudes | Cold-tolerant life retreats to high latitudes and mountains |

<StudentAsks q="If the Arctic sea ice melts, will sea level rise a lot because of it?">

No. Ice floating on the sea already pushes aside seawater of the same weight as itself; when it melts, the water roughly fills the space it was pushing aside, so sea level hardly changes. What changes sea level noticeably is the ice **on land**, the ice sheets and glaciers: their meltwater is newly added to the ocean. Also, seawater expands slightly when it warms, which raises sea level too.

</StudentAsks>

## Recent rapid warming: human activity is the main cause

The change from a glacial to an interglacial period is a natural warming. First, practise comparing rates with illustrative data.

<Predict id="warming-rate" prompt="Illustrative data: when a glacial period ended and an interglacial began, the global mean temperature rose by about 5 °C over about 10,000 years. If another warming raised the temperature by 1 °C in 100 years, how many times the earlier average rate of warming is its average rate?" answer={20} unit="times">

Put both as "degrees per 100 years": the first is $5\div100=0.05$ °C, the second 1 °C. $1\div0.05=20$, so the second is 20 times the first.

</Predict>

What you compare is the **rate**, not the total rise: the second warming rises only 1 °C, yet it is much faster. The illustrative numbers only show how to compare; the curriculum's point is that recent climate change is rapid, and the long-term natural drivers cannot fully explain it. Using the time axis from section one, check the possible drivers one by one:

| Possible driver | Timescale it acts on | Can it explain the recent rapid warming? |
|---|---|---|
| Plate motion | Tens of millions of years or more | No: too slow |
| Mountain building | Millions of years | No: too slow |
| Solar activity | Years to centuries | Its timescale is short enough, but the size and trend of its changes do not match the recent warming (see the illustrative figure below) |
| Human activity | Modern times | Yes: burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests add greenhouse gases |

Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, which strengthens the <Term t="chemistry.greenhouse-effect">greenhouse effect</Term>, and the surface warms. In the curriculum's words: long-term natural drivers cannot fully explain the recent rapid change, and **current evidence shows that human activity is the main factor**.

<Perspective later title="Why do greenhouse gases hold in heat?" needs="the electromagnetic spectrum and infrared (physics: electromagnetic waves)" href="/en/physics/s116-em-waves/">

Sunlight is mostly visible light, and most of it passes through the atmosphere to the ground; the warmed ground gives off mainly infrared. Greenhouse gases let visible light through but absorb infrared, and send part of it back down to the ground, so the surface is warmer than it would be without them. The more greenhouse gas, the more heat is held in. The electromagnetic spectrum and infrared are covered fully in the physics chapter on electromagnetic waves.

</Perspective>

## Mitigation and adaptation

As you learned in junior high, there are two kinds of action against climate change:

- **Mitigation** (<Zh>減緩</Zh>): cut greenhouse gas emissions or increase their uptake, slowing warming at its source.
- **Adaptation** (<Zh>調適</Zh>): adjust how people live, build and organise themselves, to reduce the impacts and losses climate change has brought or will bring.

Use the figure to place two measures on the chain: a flood warning system for a riverside town, and replacing a gas water heater with a solar water heater. Which arrow does each act on, and is it mitigation or adaptation?

The solar water heater burns less gas, so less greenhouse gas builds up: it acts on the first arrow and is mitigation. The flood warning system does not change the warming at all; it reduces the losses when floods come, so it acts on the last arrow and is adaptation.

Adaptation covers many areas, for example disasters, infrastructure, water resources, coasts, land use, agriculture and biodiversity, energy and industry, and health. The curriculum asks only for a brief introduction.

<StudentAsks q="If we can adapt, do we still need to cut emissions?">

Yes. Adaptation can only reduce losses; it cannot stop the warming. The worse the warming, the wider and costlier the adaptation needed, and some impacts are very hard to adapt to at all. So mitigation and adaptation have to go together.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Sort it the other way: one impact touches several areas">

Above, the areas came first. You can also start from an impact and see which areas it touches (the impacts are assumed scenarios):

| Assumed impact | Areas it touches | Example adaptation measures |
|---|---|---|
| More heavy rain | Disasters, infrastructure | Improve urban drainage, strengthen flood defences |
| Rising sea level | Coasts, land use | Strengthen sea walls; no new homes in low-lying areas |
| More hot summer days | Health, energy and industry | Warn people about heat illness; secure electricity at peak demand |
| Areas suited to a crop shift | Agriculture and biodiversity | Breed heat-tolerant varieties; change where crops are grown |

One impact often touches several areas, so adaptation needs planning across areas together.

</Perspective>
