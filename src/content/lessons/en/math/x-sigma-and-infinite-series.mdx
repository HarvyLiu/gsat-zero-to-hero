## $\Sigma$ notation: writing a sum in one line

A sum with a pattern can be written in one line with $\Sigma$ (the Greek capital letter sigma, matching the S of "sum"):

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots+a_n
$$

Read it as "the sum of $a_k$ from $k=1$ to $k=n$":

- The $k=1$ underneath says where the index $k$ starts; the $n$ on top says where it stops. $k$ goes up by $1$ each time.
- The $a_k$ to the right of $\Sigma$ says what each term looks like.
- From $k=m$ to $k=n$ there are $n-m+1$ terms (like counting trees from the $m$-th to the $n$-th).

For example

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{5}(2k-1)=1+3+5+7+9=25,\qquad \sum_{k=3}^{7}k^2=9+16+25+36+49=135
$$

The second sum starts at $k=3$ and has $7-3+1=5$ terms. Going the other way, each term of $2+4+6+\cdots+40$ is $2k$ with $k$ from $1$ to $20$, so it is $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20}2k$.

<StudentAsks q="If I change k to j, does the answer change?">

No. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_j$ both expand to $a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n$; $k$ is just a running label, and any name will do. $n$ is different: it decides where the sum stops, so changing $n$ changes the sum. That is why the answer to $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k$ contains $n$ but never $k$.

</StudentAsks>

### Linearity: a $\Sigma$ can be split

Because addition can be reordered and regrouped freely, and multiplication distributes over it, $\Sigma$ obeys three rules (every $\Sigma$ runs from $k=1$ to $n$, and $c$ is a constant):

$$
\sum(a_k+b_k)=\sum a_k+\sum b_k,\qquad \sum c\,a_k=c\sum a_k,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}c=nc
$$

The first says the two parts of each term can be added separately; the second says a factor $c$ in every term comes out in front; the third says $c$ is added $n$ times.

In the figure, $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}(k+2)=18$, and the blue and amber parts can be added separately: $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}k+\sum_{k=1}^{4}2=10+8$. The amber strip has width $4$ and height $2$, which is $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4}2=4\times 2$; a common slip is to add the $2$ only once.

**Careful**: products do not split. $\displaystyle\sum a_kb_k\ne\left(\sum a_k\right)\left(\sum b_k\right)$. For example with $a_k=b_k=k$ and $n=3$: the left side is $1+4+9=14$, the right side is $(1+2+3)^2=36$.

The three sum formulas from the sequences chapter, written with $\Sigma$, are

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2},\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3=\left[\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right]^2
$$

**Example**　Find $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{10}(3k^2-2k+1)$.

| The GSAT way (written out) | With $\Sigma$ and linearity |
|---|---|
| $(3\cdot1^2-2\cdot1+1)+\cdots+(3\cdot10^2-2\cdot10+1)$ | $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{10}(3k^2-2k+1)$ |
| Regroup: $3(1^2+\cdots+10^2)-2(1+\cdots+10)+(1+\cdots+1)$, the last group being ten $1$s | $\displaystyle 3\sum_{k=1}^{10}k^2-2\sum_{k=1}^{10}k+\sum_{k=1}^{10}1$ |
| $3\times 385-2\times 55+10=1055$ | $3\times 385-2\times 55+10=1055$ |

Both columns do the same thing. $\Sigma$ brings no new method; it only makes the "split into three groups" step visible at a glance, so you are less likely to forget the ten $1$s. The curriculum writes such sums out in full in grade 10 and introduces $\Sigma$ formally only in grade 12. On the GSAT, write sums out or just give the answer as usual; using $\Sigma$ on your scrap paper is fine.

<Perspective title="Check it: find the general formula, then try small n">

Split the same way, but add up to $n$ instead of $10$:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(3k^2-2k+1)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{2}-n(n+1)+n
$$

You can test this formula with small $n$ without writing out ten terms:

- $n=1$: $\frac{1\cdot2\cdot3}{2}-2+1=2$, and the first term is $3-2+1=2$. They agree.
- $n=2$: $\frac{2\cdot3\cdot5}{2}-6+2=11$, and the first two terms give $2+9=11$. They agree.

With both small cases right, put in $n=10$: $\frac{10\cdot11\cdot21}{2}-110+10=1155-110+10=1055$, the same as above. This check is quick on GSAT fill-in items: a wrong formula usually gives itself away at $n=1$ or $n=2$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Derive Σk by cancelling: the middle terms all drop out">

$\Sigma$ also makes cancelling easy to see. Look at $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[(k+1)^2-k^2\right]$ and write it out:

$$
(2^2-1^2)+(3^2-2^2)+(4^2-3^2)+\cdots+\left((n+1)^2-n^2\right)=(n+1)^2-1
$$

Every square in the middle appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, so only the two ends survive. On the other hand, each term is $(k+1)^2-k^2=2k+1$, and by linearity

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k+1)=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}k+n
$$

The two must be equal: $2\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k+n=n^2+2n$, so $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. This is a completely different route from the sequences chapter's picture of two staircases making a rectangle, and it reaches the same answer. Doing the same with $(k+1)^3-k^3$ gives the sum-of-squares formula.

</Perspective>

## Infinite geometric series: what does adding infinitely many terms mean?

Keep adding the terms of a geometric sequence $a,\ ar,\ ar^2,\ \dots$ and you get $a+ar+ar^2+\cdots$, an **infinite geometric series** <Zh>無窮等比級數</Zh>. You can never actually add infinitely many terms one by one, so look at the **partial sums** <Zh>部分和</Zh>, the sums of the first $n$ terms:

$$
S_n=a+ar+\cdots+ar^{n-1}=\frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}\quad(r\ne1)
$$

This is the finite <Term t="math.geometric-series">geometric series</Term> formula from the sequences chapter. For $1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots$ the partial sums are

$$
S_1=1,\quad S_2=1.5,\quad S_3=1.75,\quad S_4=1.875,\quad\dots,\quad S_n=2-\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1}
$$

The gap between $S_n$ and $2$ is $\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}$; it halves with every new term and can be made as small as you like. We then say the series **converges** <Zh>收斂</Zh> and its sum is $2$. In general:

- If the partial sums $S_n$ get closer and closer to one fixed number $S$ as $n$ grows (the gap can be made as small as you like), the series **converges** and its sum is $S$.
- If they do not approach any fixed number, the series **diverges** <Zh>發散</Zh> and has no sum.

### When is there a sum?

The key is the $r^n$ in $S_n=\dfrac{a}{1-r}-\dfrac{a}{1-r}\,r^n$. Take the first term $a\ne0$:

| Ratio $r$ | What $r^n$ and the partial sums $S_n$ do | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $-1<r<1$ | $r^n$ gets closer and closer to $0$, and $S_n$ approaches $\dfrac{a}{1-r}$ | converges, sum $\dfrac{a}{1-r}$ |
| $r=1$ | every term is $a$, so $S_n=na$ keeps growing (or keeps falling) | diverges |
| $r=-1$ | $S_n$ is $a,0,a,0,\dots$, jumping back and forth | diverges |
| $r>1$ or $r<-1$ | the size of $r^n$ keeps growing, and $S_n$ never settles near any number | diverges |

So an infinite geometric series with nonzero first term **converges exactly when $-1<r<1$**, and then

$$
a+ar+ar^2+\cdots=\frac{a}{1-r}
$$

**Example**　What is $8-4+2-1+\cdots$?

The ratio is $r=-\frac12$, which lies between $-1$ and $1$, so the series converges, with sum $\dfrac{8}{1-(-\frac12)}=\dfrac{8}{\frac32}=\dfrac{16}{3}$.

<Perspective title="With logarithms: how long until rⁿ is tiny?">

"$r^n$ gets closer and closer to $0$" can be made concrete with grade-10 common logarithms. Take $r=0.9$ and ask for $0.9^n<0.001$. Take logs of both sides:

$$
n\log 0.9<-3
$$

Given $\log 3\approx0.4771$, $\log0.9=\log9-1=2\log3-1\approx-0.0458$, a negative number. Dividing by a negative number flips the inequality:

$$
n>\frac{-3}{-0.0458}\approx65.5
$$

So from $n=66$ on, $0.9^n$ is below $0.001$. Any smaller target than $0.001$ works the same way: there is always a large enough $n$, which is what "as small as you like" means. The closer $r$ is to $1$, the closer $\log r$ is to $0$ and the larger the $n$ you need, which is why the points in the figure above crawl when $r$ is near $1$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="A series that contains itself: S = a + rS">

There is also a very short derivation. Write the series as $S=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots$. Take $r$ out of everything after the first term, and what is left is $S$ again:

$$
S=a+r\,(a+ar+ar^2+\cdots)=a+rS
$$

Solving gives $S=\dfrac{a}{1-r}$. This matches the result from partial sums getting closer and closer, but it **assumes that $S$ exists**, so it may only be used once you know $-1<r<1$ (see the question below).

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Using S = a + rS on 1 + 2 + 4 + ⋯ gives S = −1?">

Yes: forcing it gives $S=1+2S$, so $S=-1$, a negative total from positive terms, which is clearly wrong. The mistake is the very first step. The partial sums of $1+2+4+\cdots$ are $1,3,7,15,\dots$, which keep growing, so there is no sum, and "let it equal $S$" was already false. This is why you must check $-1<r<1$ before using $\dfrac{a}{1-r}$.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Making “closer and closer to 0” precise" needs="Limits of sequences and the squeeze theorem (Math Jia, elective trick)" href="/en/math/x-limits-steady-state/">

Math Jia <Zh>數學甲</Zh> (code <Zh>N-12甲-1</Zh>) uses limits of sequences and the squeeze theorem <Zh>夾擠定理</Zh> to make "$r^n$ approaches $0$ when $0<r<1$" precise. Take $r=0.9$: $\frac1{0.9}=1+\frac19$. By the binomial theorem, $(1+h)^n\ge1+nh$ for $h>0$ (all the other terms of the expansion are positive), so

$$
\left(\tfrac{10}{9}\right)^n\ge1+\frac n9,\qquad 0<0.9^n\le\frac{9}{9+n}
$$

The right-hand side $\frac{9}{9+n}$ clearly tends to $0$ as $n$ grows, and $0.9^n$ is squeezed between $0$ and it, so it must tend to $0$ as well. For example, at $n=81$, $0.9^{81}\le\frac{9}{90}=\frac1{10}$. The estimate is rough ($0.9^{81}$ is in fact far smaller than $\frac1{10}$), but it is enough to prove "tends to $0$". The GSAT does not need this part.

</Perspective>

## Repeating decimals as infinite geometric series

The real-numbers chapter showed that the decimal of a <Term t="math.rational-number">rational number</Term> either stops or repeats, and that a repeating decimal becomes a fraction by "multiply by a power of $10$ and subtract". Infinite geometric series explain what a repeating decimal **itself** means. For example

$$
0.\overline{36}=0.36+0.0036+0.000036+\cdots=\frac{36}{100}+\frac{36}{100^2}+\frac{36}{100^3}+\cdots
$$

This is an infinite geometric series with first term $\frac{36}{100}$ and ratio $\frac1{100}$, so

$$
0.\overline{36}=\frac{\frac{36}{100}}{1-\frac1{100}}=\frac{36}{99}=\frac4{11}
$$

A "decimal that repeats forever" is the number that the partial sums $0.36,\ 0.3636,\ 0.363636,\ \dots$ get closer and closer to:

<NumberLine desc="A zoomed-in number line from 0.355 to 0.365, marking the first partial sum S₁=0.36 and 4/11≈0.363636…. The second partial sum S₂=0.3636 is only about 0.000036 from 4/11 and already overlaps it in the picture; S₃=0.363636 is even closer. Each extra block makes the distance to 4/11 one hundredth of what it was." from={0.355} to={0.365} step={0.001} labelEvery={5}
  points={[{ x: 0.36, label: 'S₁' }, { x: 0.3636 }, { x: 0.36363636, label: '4/11' }]} />

In general, $0.\overline{d_1d_2\cdots d_m}$ with an $m$-digit repeating block has ratio $\frac1{10^m}$ and sum $\dfrac{d_1d_2\cdots d_m}{10^m-1}$, that is, "the repeating block over $m$ nines", exactly the shortcut from the real-numbers chapter.

**Example**　Write $0.1\overline{36}=0.13636\cdots$ as a fraction in lowest terms. The two methods side by side:

| Grade 10: multiply by a power of $10$ and subtract | Advanced tool: infinite geometric series |
|---|---|
| Let $x=0.13636\cdots$ | Split it as $0.1+0.036+0.00036+\cdots$ |
| $1000x=136.3636\cdots$ and $10x=1.3636\cdots$ | After the $0.1$ comes an infinite geometric series with first term $\frac{36}{1000}$ and ratio $\frac1{100}$ |
| Subtract; the repeating part cancels: $990x=135$ | $\dfrac1{10}+\dfrac{\frac{36}{1000}}{1-\frac1{100}}=\dfrac1{10}+\dfrac{36}{990}$ |
| $x=\dfrac{135}{990}=\dfrac3{22}$ | $=\dfrac{99+36}{990}=\dfrac{135}{990}=\dfrac3{22}$ |

The two methods give the same answer. In the exam room the left column is faster, and it is the method the GSAT expects. The right column's value is that it explains why the left one works: the subtraction method silently treats $0.13636\cdots$ as a definite number and then shifts infinitely many digits at once, and the infinite geometric series says exactly what that number is: the number the partial sums get closer and closer to. When you are unsure of an answer, you can also check it with the right column, or simply by long division, $3\div22=0.13636\cdots$.

<StudentAsks q="Is 0.999⋯ really equal to 1? Isn't it always a tiny bit less?">

It really is equal. $0.\overline9=\frac9{10}+\frac9{100}+\cdots$ is an infinite geometric series with first term $\frac9{10}$ and ratio $\frac1{10}$, whose sum is $\dfrac{\frac9{10}}{1-\frac1{10}}=1$. "A tiny bit less" describes the partial sums: $0.9,\ 0.99,\ 0.999$ fall short of $1$ by $\frac1{10},\ \frac1{100},\ \frac1{1000},\dots$, and each partial sum is below $1$. But $0.999\cdots$ is not any one of those partial sums; it is the number they get closer and closer to, which is $1$. The subtraction method agrees: $10x-x=9.999\cdots-0.999\cdots=9$, so $x=1$.

</StudentAsks>
