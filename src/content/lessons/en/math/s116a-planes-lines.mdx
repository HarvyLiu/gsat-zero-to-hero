## A plane: one point plus a normal vector

A nonzero vector perpendicular to a plane is a <Term t="math.normal-vector">normal vector</Term> of the plane. For equations of planes the curriculum lists three things: the normal vector and the standard form <Zh>標準式</Zh>, the angle between two planes, and the distance from a point to a plane. We review them in that order.

For the plane through $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ with normal vector $\vec{n}=(a,b,c)$, every point $P(x,y,z)$ of the plane has $\overrightarrow{P_0P}\perp\vec{n}$, so their <Term t="math.inner-product">inner product</Term> is $0$:

$$
a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0
$$

This is the point-normal form. Expanding gives the shape $ax+by+cz+d=0$. In reverse, from a plane $ax+by+cz+d=0$ you can read off the normal vector $(a,b,c)$ at once; any nonzero multiple of it is a normal vector too.

**Example.** Find the plane through the three points $A(1,2,0)$, $B(2,0,1)$ and $C(0,1,3)$.

The normal must be perpendicular to both $\overrightarrow{AB}=(1,-2,1)$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}=(-1,-1,3)$, so use the <Term t="math.cross-product">cross product</Term>:

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(-5,-4,-3)
$$

Take the normal $(5,4,3)$ and use the point-normal form with $A$: $5(x-1)+4(y-2)+3z=0$, that is, $5x+4y+3z-13=0$. Check: $B$ gives $10+0+3=13$ and $C$ gives $0+4+9=13$, so both lie on the plane.

<Perspective title="Without the cross product: set ax+by+cz=d and solve for the coefficients">

A plane can be written $ax+by+cz=d$. All three points lie on it, so substituting gives

$$
a+2b=d,\qquad 2a+c=d,\qquad b+3c=d
$$

That is three equations in four unknowns, but multiplying a plane's equation by a nonzero number gives the same plane, so we may pick one convenient value. The second equation gives $c=d-2a$; substituting into the third gives $b=d-3c=6a-2d$; substituting into the first gives $a+12a-4d=d$, that is, $13a=5d$. Take $d=13$: then $a=5$, $b=4$, $c=3$, and the plane is $5x+4y+3z=13$, the same as with the cross product.

This uses nothing but substitution, with no cross product to remember; the cost is solving a system in three unknowns. The cross product gives the normal in one step, which is usually faster when the coordinates are messy.

</Perspective>

## The angle between two planes

The angle between two planes is read from the angle between their normal vectors. With normals $\vec{n}_1$ and $\vec{n}_2$,

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\vec{n}_1\cdot\vec{n}_2}{\lvert\vec{n}_1\rvert\,\lvert\vec{n}_2\rvert}
$$

Two intersecting planes make two angles, $\theta$ and $180^\circ-\theta$, which are supplementary. In particular:

- normals parallel $\iff$ the planes are parallel or the same plane;
- normals perpendicular (inner product $0$) $\iff$ the planes are perpendicular.

**Example.** Find the angle between the planes $x+z=2$ and $y+z=5$.

The normals are $(1,0,1)$ and $(0,1,1)$. Their inner product is $1$ and both have length $\sqrt{2}$, so $\cos\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{2}}=\frac{1}{2}$ and $\theta=60^\circ$. The planes make angles of $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$.

<Perspective title="Measure the angle inside the two planes">

What the angle between two planes originally means is this: take a point on the line of intersection, draw in each plane a ray perpendicular to that line, and measure the angle between the two rays. We can compute exactly that.

The line of intersection lies in both planes, so its direction is perpendicular to both normals: $\vec{d}=\vec{n}_1\times\vec{n}_2=(1,0,1)\times(0,1,1)=(-1,-1,1)$. A direction inside the first plane and perpendicular to the line must be perpendicular to both $\vec{n}_1$ and $\vec{d}$, so take

$$
\vec{u}_1=\vec{n}_1\times\vec{d}=(1,-2,-1),\qquad \vec{u}_2=\vec{n}_2\times\vec{d}=(2,-1,1)
$$

Check: $\vec{u}_1\cdot\vec{n}_1=1-1=0$ and $\vec{u}_1\cdot\vec{d}=-1+2-1=0$; $\vec{u}_2\cdot\vec{n}_2=-1+1=0$ and $\vec{u}_2\cdot\vec{d}=-2+1+1=0$. So

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\vec{u}_1\cdot\vec{u}_2}{\lvert\vec{u}_1\rvert\,\lvert\vec{u}_2\rvert}=\frac{2+2-1}{\sqrt{6}\cdot\sqrt{6}}=\frac{1}{2}
$$

and $\theta=60^\circ$, as with the normals. This takes more steps, but it is the definition of the angle; using the normals is the shortcut.

## Distance from a point to a plane

The distance from $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ to the plane $E:ax+by+cz+d=0$ is

$$
d(P,E)=\frac{\lvert ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d\rvert}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}
$$

It looks almost exactly like the distance from a point to a line in the coordinate plane, with one extra $z$ term. The distance between the parallel planes $ax+by+cz+d_1=0$ and $ax+by+cz+d_2=0$ is $\frac{\lvert d_1-d_2\rvert}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}$; as with lines, **first make the coefficients exactly the same** (signs included).

**Example.** Find the distance from $P(4,-1,3)$ to the plane $E:2x-y+2z+3=0$.

$$
d(P,E)=\frac{\lvert 8+1+6+3\rvert}{\sqrt{4+1+4}}=\frac{18}{3}=6
$$

<Space3D desc="A piece of the plane E: 2x−y+2z+3=0 with corners (4, 5, −3), (0, 5, 1), (−4, −3, 1) and (0, −3, −3). The point P(4, −1, 3) is above the plane. The dashed segment PH is perpendicular to the plane, with foot H(0, 1, −1) and length 6, the distance from P to E. The arrow from H is the normal n=(2, −1, 2), pointing to (2, 0, 1); PH is parallel to n and twice as long." range={5}
  planes={[{ corners: [[4, 5, -3], [0, 5, 1], [-4, -3, 1], [0, -3, -3]], label: 'E' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [4, -1, 3], to: [0, 1, -1], dashed: true }]}
  vectors={[{ tail: [0, 1, -1], tip: [2, 0, 1], label: 'n', color: 'amber' }]}
  points={[{ at: [4, -1, 3], label: 'P' }, { at: [0, 1, -1], label: 'H' }]} />

<Perspective title="With the Cauchy inequality: the distance is the shortest segment">

The distance means the shortest of all the lengths from $P$ to points of the plane. Let $(x,y,z)$ be on $E$, so $2x-y+2z=-3$, and therefore

$$
2(x-4)-(y+1)+2(z-3)=(2x-y+2z)-15=-18
$$

By the <Term t="math.cauchy-inequality">Cauchy inequality</Term>,

$$
\left[(x-4)^2+(y+1)^2+(z-3)^2\right](2^2+1^2+2^2)\ge(-18)^2
$$

The bracket on the left is the squared distance from $P$ to $(x,y,z)$, so the squared distance is at least $\frac{324}{9}=36$ and the distance is at least $6$. Equality holds when $(x-4,y+1,z-3)$ is parallel to $(2,-1,2)$, that is, when we move along the normal: this is exactly the foot of the perpendicular. The shortest distance is $6$, matching the formula. This view ties the distance formula to finding a minimum.

</Perspective>

**Example.** Find the distance between the parallel planes $2x-y+2z+3=0$ and $4x-2y+4z-6=0$.

Divide the second plane by $2$: $2x-y+2z-3=0$. The distance is $\frac{\lvert 3-(-3)\rvert}{3}=2$.

<StudentAsks q="Where does the point-to-plane distance formula come from?">

Take a point $Q$ in the plane. The distance is the length of the projection of $\overrightarrow{QP}$ onto the normal $\vec{n}$, that is, $\frac{\lvert\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\vec{n}\rvert}{\lvert\vec{n}\rvert}$. Because $Q$ lies in the plane, $\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\vec{n}$ simplifies to exactly $ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d$, which gives the formula above. The same idea, projecting onto a normal, comes back in the last section for the distance between skew lines.

</StudentAsks>

## A line in space: parametric and symmetric forms

A line in space is fixed by **one point** and **one direction vector**. The line through $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ with direction vector $\vec{v}=(l,m,n)$ has the <Term t="math.parametric-equation-of-line">parametric equation</Term>

$$
x=x_0+lt,\quad y=y_0+mt,\quad z=z_0+nt\quad(t\text{ real})
$$

Each value of $t$ gives one point of the line. If $l$, $m$ and $n$ are all nonzero, solving each equation for $t$ gives the <Term t="math.symmetric-equation-of-line">symmetric form</Term> (the curriculum calls it the ratio form <Zh>比例式</Zh>):

$$
\frac{x-x_0}{l}=\frac{y-y_0}{m}=\frac{z-z_0}{n}
$$

**Example.** Find the line through $A(1,-1,2)$ and $B(3,0,1)$.

The direction vector is $\overrightarrow{AB}=(2,1,-1)$. The parametric form is $x=1+2t$, $y=-1+t$, $z=2-t$, and the symmetric form is

$$
\frac{x-1}{2}=\frac{y+1}{1}=\frac{z-2}{-1}
$$

At $t=1$ we get $(3,0,1)$, which is $B$.

<Space3D desc="The line x=1+2t, y=−1+t, z=2−t, drawn as the segment from the point (−1, −2, 3) at t=−1 to the point (5, 1, 0) at t=2. A(1, −1, 2) is t=0, B(3, 0, 1) is t=1, and the arrow v=(2, 1, −1) runs from A to B. Each time t goes up by 1, the point takes one more step along v." range={5}

<Perspective title="The symmetric form is really two planes">

The symmetric form $\frac{x-1}{2}=\frac{y+1}{1}=\frac{z-2}{-1}$ has two equals signs; split apart, it is two linear equations:

- $\frac{x-1}{2}=y+1$, which simplifies to $x-2y=3$;
- $y+1=\frac{z-2}{-1}$, which simplifies to $y+z=1$.

Each one is a plane, so this line is the intersection of the planes $x-2y=3$ and $y+z=1$. Check: $A(1,-1,2)$ gives $1+2=3$ and $-1+2=1$; $B(3,0,1)$ gives $3-0=3$ and $0+1=1$. Conversely, two planes taken together can be thought of as one line, which is exactly what the "Try it" below does.

<StudentAsks q="My parametric equation for a line is different from the textbook's. Am I wrong?">

Not necessarily. You may start from any point of the line and use any nonzero multiple of the direction vector, so one line has many parametric equations. To check, ask whether the two direction vectors are parallel and whether a point of your line lies on the other one. Also, if a direction vector has a zero component, such as $(2,0,1)$, you cannot write a symmetric form with $0$ as a denominator; write that coordinate on its own as $y=y_0$.

</StudentAsks>

## How a line sits relative to a plane

Let the line $L$ have direction vector $\vec{v}$ and the plane $E$ have normal vector $\vec{n}$:

| Case | Test | Common points |
|---|---|---|
| They meet | $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\ne 0$ | exactly one |
| Parallel | $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}=0$, and a point of $L$ is not on $E$ | none |
| $L$ lies in $E$ | $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}=0$, and a point of $L$ is on $E$ | infinitely many |

When $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{n}$ are parallel, $L$ is perpendicular to $E$. To find the point where they meet, substitute the parametric form into the plane and solve for $t$.

**Example.** Find where the line $x=1+2t$, $y=-1+t$, $z=2-t$ meets the plane $x+y+z=6$.

Substitute: $(1+2t)+(-1+t)+(2-t)=2+2t=6$, so $t=2$ and the point is $(5,1,0)$. Check: $5+1+0=6$.

The line x=1+2t, y=−1+t, z=2−t is drawn as the segment from the point (−1, −2, 3) at t=−1 to the point (7, 2, −1) at t=3, and it passes through the plane at the point (5, 1, 0) where t=2. A(1, −1, 2), the point at t=0, is on one side of the plane." range={5}

<Perspective title="Substitution as a linear function: its slope is v·n">

Let $g(t)=x+y+z-6$, evaluated at the point of the line with parameter $t$. Every coordinate is linear in $t$, so $g(t)$ is a linear function of $t$: here $g(t)=2t-4$. The meeting point is the root $t=2$ of $g(t)=0$, where the graph crosses the horizontal axis.

In general the slope of $g(t)$ is $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}$, and $g(0)$ is the value you get by substituting the starting point into the plane. The three cases of the table can be read straight off the graph: nonzero slope, and the graph crosses the axis exactly once (they meet); slope $0$ with $g(0)\ne0$, a horizontal line that never touches the axis (parallel); slope and $g(0)$ both $0$, and the graph is the axis itself (the line lies in the plane).

<Perspective title="Three planes meeting in one point: solve by elimination">

The earlier "The symmetric form is really two planes" showed that this line is where the planes $x-2y=3$ and $y+z=1$ meet. So the point where the line meets the plane $x+y+z=6$ is the common point of three planes, that is, the solution of the system of three linear equations

$$
x-2y=3,\qquad y+z=1,\qquad x+y+z=6
$$

Subtracting the second equation from the third removes $y$ and $z$ together and gives $x=5$ at once; then the first equation gives $5-2y=3$, so $y=1$; and the second gives $z=0$. The point is $(5,1,0)$, the same as by substituting the parametric form. In algebra we eliminate unknowns; in geometry three planes meet in a point.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Use a determinant to tell whether three planes meet in exactly one point" needs="Cramer's rule for three equations in three unknowns, and using a determinant to classify three planes (the Matrices chapter teaches Cramer's rule only for two unknowns; 分科, not on the 學測)">

Arrange the three planes' coefficients as a 3×3 determinant. It is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three normals $(1,-2,0)$, $(0,1,1)$ and $(1,1,1)$:

$$
\Delta=\begin{vmatrix}1&-2&0\\0&1&1\\1&1&1\end{vmatrix}=-2\ne0
$$

$\Delta\ne0$ means the three normals do not lie in one plane, so the three planes meet in exactly one point. Cramer's rule then gives $\Delta_x=-10$, $\Delta_y=-2$ and $\Delta_z=0$, so $x=\frac{-10}{-2}=5$, $y=\frac{-2}{-2}=1$ and $z=\frac{0}{-2}=0$.

</Perspective>

**Example.** Find the projection (foot of the perpendicular) of $P(4,-1,3)$ on the plane $E:2x-y+2z+3=0$.

The line through $P$ perpendicular to $E$ has the normal $(2,-1,2)$ as its direction vector: $x=4+2t$, $y=-1-t$, $z=3+2t$. Substitute into $E$:

$$
2(4+2t)-(-1-t)+2(3+2t)+3=18+9t=0
$$

So $t=-2$ and the foot is $(0,1,-1)$. The distance from $P$ to the foot is $\lvert t\rvert\cdot\lvert\vec{n}\rvert=2\cdot 3=6$, the same distance the formula gave earlier.

<Perspective title="Write down the foot in one step with orthogonal projection">

Substituting $P$ into the plane gives $2\cdot4-(-1)+2\cdot3+3=18$, and this equals $\vec{n}\cdot\overrightarrow{HP}$, where $H$ is the foot (for the same reason as in the derivation of the distance formula). $\overrightarrow{HP}$ is parallel to $\vec{n}$, so $\overrightarrow{HP}=\frac{18}{\lvert\vec{n}\rvert^2}\vec{n}=\frac{18}{9}(2,-1,2)=(4,-2,4)$, and therefore

$$
H=P-\overrightarrow{HP}=(4,-1,3)-(4,-2,4)=(0,1,-1)
$$

No parametric form and no solving for $t$. The factor $\frac{18}{9}=2$ here is exactly the $\lvert t\rvert$ found above.

</Perspective>

## Distances from a point to a line, between parallel lines and between skew lines

The curriculum notes that these three distances are usually found with the cross product.

**Point to line**: take a point $A$ on the line $L$ and its direction vector $\vec{v}$. The parallelogram spanned by $\overrightarrow{AP}$ and $\vec{v}$ has area $\lvert\overrightarrow{AP}\times\vec{v}\rvert$ and base $\lvert\vec{v}\rvert$, and its height is the distance:

$$
d(P,L)=\frac{\lvert\overrightarrow{AP}\times\vec{v}\rvert}{\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}
$$

**Two parallel lines**: take any point on one line and find its distance to the other line.

**Skew lines**: two lines that are neither parallel nor intersecting are <Term t="math.skew-lines">skew lines</Term>. If $L_1$ passes through $A$ with direction $\vec{v}_1$ and $L_2$ passes through $B$ with direction $\vec{v}_2$, then $\vec{n}=\vec{v}_1\times\vec{v}_2$ is perpendicular to both lines, and the distance is the length of the projection of $\overrightarrow{AB}$ onto $\vec{n}$:

$$
d(L_1,L_2)=\frac{\lvert\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\vec{n}\rvert}{\lvert\vec{n}\rvert}
$$

**Example.** Find the distance from $P(2,0,1)$ to the line $L$: $x=t$, $y=t$, $z=0$.

Take $A(0,0,0)$ and $\vec{v}=(1,1,0)$. Then $\overrightarrow{AP}=(2,0,1)$ and $\overrightarrow{AP}\times\vec{v}=(-1,1,2)$, of length $\sqrt{6}$. The distance is $\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{3}$.

<Perspective title="Find the foot directly, or write the distance as a quadratic">

Write a point of the line as $Q(t,t,0)$; then $\overrightarrow{QP}=(2-t,-t,1)$.

**Find the foot**: $\overrightarrow{QP}\perp\vec{v}$, so $(2-t)+(-t)=0$ and $t=1$. Then $Q(1,1,0)$, $\overrightarrow{QP}=(1,-1,1)$, of length $\sqrt{3}$.

**Complete the square**: $\lvert\overrightarrow{QP}\rvert^2=(2-t)^2+t^2+1=2t^2-4t+5=2(t-1)^2+3$, whose minimum $3$ is reached at $t=1$, so the distance is $\sqrt{3}$.

Neither method needs the cross product, and both also give the nearest point $Q$; the cross-product formula gives only the distance, not the foot.

**Example.** Find the distance between the $x$-axis and the line $L_2$: $(x,y,z)=(0,1,2)+s(0,1,1)$.

The $x$-axis passes through $A(0,0,0)$ with direction $(1,0,0)$. Every point of $L_2$ has $x$-coordinate $0$, so to reach the $x$-axis it would need $y=1+s=0$ and $z=2+s=0$, which is impossible; the directions are not parallel either, so the lines are skew. $\vec{n}=(1,0,0)\times(0,1,1)=(0,-1,1)$ and $\overrightarrow{AB}=(0,1,2)$, so the distance is

$$
\frac{\lvert 0-1+2\rvert}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

Every point of $L_2$ has $x$-coordinate $0$, so all of $L_2$ lies in the $yz$-plane, and the $x$-axis is perpendicular to the $yz$-plane, meeting it at the origin. Looking along the $x$-axis, the axis shrinks to the point $O$, and the problem becomes the distance from the origin to a line in the $yz$-plane.

On $L_2$, $y=1+s$ and $z=2+s$; eliminating $s$ gives $z=y+1$, that is, $y-z+1=0$. The distance from the origin to it is $\frac{\lvert 0-0+1\rvert}{\sqrt{1+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, the same as with the cross product.

<Perspective title="Write the squared distance as a function of two parameters">

The squared distance between the point $(p,0,0)$ of the $x$-axis and the point $(0,1+s,2+s)$ of $L_2$ is

$$
p^2+(1+s)^2+(2+s)^2=p^2+2\left(s+\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^2+\tfrac{1}{2}
$$

Each square is at least $0$, so the minimum comes at $p=0$, $s=-\frac{3}{2}$: the squared distance is $\frac{1}{2}$ and the distance is $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The two nearest points are $O(0,0,0)$ and $M(0,-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})$.
