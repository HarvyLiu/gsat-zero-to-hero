The curriculum marks this topic ＃ and notes that it has no unit of its own, so here it gets a short toolbox review. The curriculum asks only for these four kinds of symmetry <Zh>對稱</Zh>: about the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis, the line $y=x$ and the origin <Zh>原點</Zh>. General line symmetry and point symmetry are not required.

## Symmetric points: four rules

The points symmetric to $(a,b)$ about each of these are:

| Symmetric about | Point symmetric to $(a,b)$ | How to remember it |
|---|---|---|
| the $x$-axis | $(a,-b)$ | flip up and down: the $y$-coordinate changes sign |
| the $y$-axis | $(-a,b)$ | flip left and right: the $x$-coordinate changes sign |
| the origin | $(-a,-b)$ | both coordinates change sign |
| the line $y=x$ | $(b,a)$ | the two coordinates swap |

**Example.** Write down the points symmetric to $P(3,-2)$ about the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis, the origin and the line $y=x$.

From the table: about the $x$-axis it is $(3,2)$, about the $y$-axis $(-3,-2)$, about the origin $(-3,2)$, and about the line $y=x$ $(-2,3)$.

Note that for the line $y=x$ the coordinates only swap places; each keeps its own sign.

Drag $a$ and $b$ in the figure below and watch the four symmetric points of $P(a,b)$ move with it. At the start $P$ is the example's $(3,-2)$.

<Perspective title="Through distances: why the line y=x swaps the coordinates">

The rules for the two axes are obvious at a glance, but the "swap" for the line $y=x$ needs a reason. Let $P(a,b)$ and $Q(b,a)$, and take any point $R(t,t)$ on the line $y=x$:

$$
\overline{PR}^{2}=(a-t)^{2}+(b-t)^{2},\qquad \overline{QR}^{2}=(b-t)^{2}+(a-t)^{2}
$$

The two sums differ only in the order of the terms, so $\overline{PR}=\overline{QR}$. In other words, **every** point of the line $y=x$ is the same distance from $P$ as from $Q$. The points equidistant from $P$ and $Q$ make up exactly the perpendicular bisector of segment $PQ$ (when $P\ne Q$), and the whole line $y=x$ lies on that bisector, so the two lines coincide: $y=x$ is the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{PQ}$, and $Q$ is exactly the point symmetric to $P$ about $y=x$. Also, the midpoint of $PQ$, $\left(\frac{a+b}{2},\frac{a+b}{2}\right)$, has equal coordinates, so it does lie on $y=x$.

Drag $t$ to move $R$ along $y=x$; the two segments always have the same length.

## Telling a graph's symmetry from its equation

A graph is symmetric about the $x$-axis when, for every point $(x,y)$ on it, the mirror point $(x,-y)$ is on it too. So substitute the mirror point into the equation and see whether the equation changes:

| Graph symmetric about | Substitution | Condition for symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| the $x$-axis | replace $y$ by $-y$ | the equation is unchanged |
| the $y$-axis | replace $x$ by $-x$ | the equation is unchanged |
| the origin | replace $x$ by $-x$ and $y$ by $-y$ together | the equation is unchanged |
| the line $y=x$ | swap $x$ and $y$ | the equation is unchanged |

"Unchanged" means the result can be tidied back into the original equation. For example, substituting $-x$ and $-y$ into $y=x^3$ gives $-y=-x^3$; multiplying both sides by $-1$ gives the original, so the graph of $y=x^3$ is symmetric about the origin. In the same way, replacing $x$ by $-x$ in $y=x^2$ changes nothing, so its graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

**Example.** Decide which symmetries the graph of $x^2+xy+y^2=7$ has.

- Replace $y$ by $-y$: $x^2-xy+y^2=7$, a different equation. For example $(1,2)$ is on the graph ($1+2+4=7$), but its mirror point $(1,-2)$ gives $1-2+4=3\ne 7$, so the graph is not symmetric about the $x$-axis.
- Replace $x$ by $-x$: $x^2-xy+y^2=7$; in the same way the graph is not symmetric about the $y$-axis ($(1,2)$ is on the graph, but its mirror point $(-1,2)$ is not).
- Replace $x$ by $-x$ and $y$ by $-y$ together: $(-x)^2+(-x)(-y)+(-y)^2=x^2+xy+y^2=7$, unchanged, so the graph is symmetric about the origin.
- Swap $x$ and $y$: $y^2+yx+x^2=7$, unchanged, so the graph is symmetric about the line $y=x$.

<Perspective later title="Turn the axes by 45°: see every symmetry at once" needs="rotating the coordinate axes and the standard form of an ellipse (elective trick)">

Let $x=\frac{u+v}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $y=\frac{u-v}{\sqrt{2}}$; this turns the coordinate axes by $45^\circ$. The new $u$-axis ($v=0$) is the line $y=x$, and the new $v$-axis ($u=0$) is the line $y=-x$. Substituting:

$$
x^{2}=\frac{u^{2}+2uv+v^{2}}{2},\quad y^{2}=\frac{u^{2}-2uv+v^{2}}{2},\quad xy=\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}
$$

Adding gives $x^2+xy+y^2=\frac{3u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}$, so the equation becomes $3u^{2}+v^{2}=14$, an ellipse. In the new equation $u$ and $v$ appear only squared, so the graph is symmetric about the $u$-axis, the $v$-axis and the origin. Back in the original coordinates: symmetry about the $u$-axis is symmetry about the line $y=x$, symmetry about the origin stays as it is, and we see one more: the graph is also symmetric about the line $y=-x$. The major axis runs in the $v$ direction (the line $y=-x$) with semi-length $\sqrt{14}$; the minor axis lies on the line $y=x$ with semi-length $\sqrt{\frac{14}{3}}$.

<Perspective later title="Exponential and log graphs: swapping coordinates in action" needs="graphs of exponential functions and the common logarithm" href="/en/math/s116-growth-models/">

A point $(p,q)$ on the graph of $y=10^{x}$ satisfies $q=10^{p}$, that is $p=\log q$, so the swapped point $(q,p)$ is on the graph of $y=\log x$, and the other way round too. In this chapter's language: swapping $x$ and $y$ in $y=10^{x}$ gives $x=10^{y}$, that is $y=\log x$, so the graphs of $y=10^{x}$ and $y=\log x$ are symmetric about the line $y=x$. For example, $(0,1)$ pairs with $(1,0)$, and $\left(\frac{1}{2},\sqrt{10}\right)$ with $\left(\sqrt{10},\frac{1}{2}\right)$. In the same way, replacing $x$ by $-x$ turns $y=2^{x}$ into $y=2^{-x}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x}$, so those two graphs are symmetric about the $y$-axis.

<StudentAsks q="If the equation looks different after substituting, does that always mean no symmetry?">

Not always; tidy it up first. $-y=-x^3$ looks different from $y=x^3$, but after multiplying by $-1$ it is the same equation. The safest way to show a graph is not symmetric is a counterexample: find a point on the graph whose mirror point is not on it. For example, $y=x^2+x$ passes through $(1,2)$, but for $(-1,-2)$ the right-hand side is $1-1=0\ne -2$, so the graph of $y=x^2+x$ is not symmetric about the origin.

</StudentAsks>

### Two symmetries bring a third

Of the three symmetries about the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis and the origin, a graph with any two of them must have the third. For example, if a graph is symmetric about both axes and $(a,b)$ is on it, then $(a,-b)$ is on it; flipping across the $y$-axis once more puts $(-a,-b)$ on it, so the graph is symmetric about the origin too.

<Perspective title="Through rotation: two flips make a half turn">

Flip $(a,b)$ across the $x$-axis to $(a,-b)$, then across the $y$-axis to $(-a,-b)$: the result is the same as turning $180^\circ$ about the origin. The midpoint of $(a,b)$ and $(-a,-b)$ is $(0,0)$, so the two points lie on opposite sides of the origin on one line through it, equally far from it, which is exactly where a half turn takes $(a,b)$. So "symmetric about the origin" can be read as "the graph lands on itself after a half turn".

This is not the only pair: any two of the flips done one after the other give the third.

| First | Then | Where $(a,b)$ goes | Together they make |
|---|---|---|---|
| about the $x$-axis | about the $y$-axis | $(a,-b)\to(-a,-b)$ | about the origin |
| about the $x$-axis | about the origin | $(a,-b)\to(-a,b)$ | about the $y$-axis |
| about the $y$-axis | about the origin | $(-a,b)\to(a,-b)$ | about the $x$-axis |

If a graph has two of the symmetries, doing those two flips one after the other keeps every point on the graph, so it has the third symmetry as well.
