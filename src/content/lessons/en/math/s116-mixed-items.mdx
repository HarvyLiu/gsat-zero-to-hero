## The mixed item set and the marking principles

On the 111–115 papers (the 116 layout is not confirmed), Part 2 of both Math A and Math B was one mixed item set <Zh>混合題組</Zh> worth $15$ points, split into $3$ sub-items, with the points and item types varying from year to year. On the 115 Math A paper, for example, Q$18$ was single choice worth $3$, Q$19$ written worth $4$ and Q$20$ written worth $8$. So a mixed set can contain choice items too; this chapter is about the written sub-items, where you must show your working.

CEEC's marking principles for the 115 Math A written items <Zh>非選擇題評分原則</Zh> put it like this:

| Your answer | Result |
|---|---|
| Correct reasoning, a detailed process and the right answer | Full marks |
| Correct reasoning, but no final answer | Partial credit |
| No process, a wrong inference, or data that contradicts the problem | Zero |

The Math B description likewise asks for your working or reasons, marks the reasoning, and gives zero for no process or a wrong inference. In other words, a correct answer on its own is not enough, and one wrong inference can cost you the whole item.

## A complete answer: premises, reasons, conclusion

When you write a written answer, check it against these four habits:

1. **State your premises**: what you let the unknowns be, and which condition of the problem you use (for example $x>0$).
2. **Give a reason for each step**: write down the theorem, formula or property you use, such as "by the AM–GM inequality" or "because $y=2^x$ is strictly increasing".
3. **Make every step checkable**: before dividing by an expression, say why it is not $0$; after squaring both sides, check the roots.
4. **Write a conclusion**: answer what the problem asked, together with when equality holds or the range that applies.

**Example.** Given $x>0$, find the minimum of $x+\frac{9}{x}$.

First, an incomplete answer:

> When $x=3$, $3+\frac{9}{3}=6$, so the minimum is $6$.

The number is right, but this only tries one value. It never explains why no other $x$ gives something smaller, so the reasoning does not hold. A complete answer reads:

> Since $x>0$, both $x$ and $\frac{9}{x}$ are positive. By the <Term t="math.am-gm-inequality">AM–GM inequality</Term>,
> $x+\frac{9}{x}\ge 2\sqrt{x\cdot\frac{9}{x}}=2\sqrt{9}=6$.
> Equality holds when $x=\frac{9}{x}$, that is $x^2=9$, and with $x>0$ this gives $x=3$.
> So the minimum of $x+\frac{9}{x}$ is $6$, attained at $x=3$.

This answer has all four habits: the premise ($x>0$, so both terms are positive), the reason (AM–GM), a checkable equality condition, and the conclusion.

"The minimum is $6$" can also be seen on a graph: for the horizontal line $y=c$ to reach the curve $y=x+\frac{9}{x}$ ($x>0$), $c$ must be at least $6$. Drag $c$ and see.

<Graph desc="The curve is y=x+9/x (x>0); its lowest point is (3,6). The dashed line is the horizontal line y=c, which moves with the slider c: at the start c=8 and it meets the curve at two points; at c=6 it just touches the curve at (3,6); for c<6 it misses the curve entirely. So the smallest value x+9/x can take is 6." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 14]}

  functions={[{ expr: 'x+9/x', domain: [0.6, 10], label: 'y=x+9/x' }, { expr: 'c', color: 'amber', dashed: true, label: 'y=c' }]}
  points={[{ at: [3, 6], label: '(3, 6)' }]} />

<Perspective title="Without AM–GM: make a perfect square">

Since $x>0$, the "sum minus $6$" can be written as one fraction:

$$
x+\frac{9}{x}-6=\frac{x^2-6x+9}{x}=\frac{(x-3)^2}{x}
$$

The numerator $(x-3)^2\ge 0$ and the denominator $x>0$, so the difference is not negative, that is $x+\frac{9}{x}\ge 6$; equality holds if and only if $(x-3)^2=0$, that is $x=3$. So the minimum is $6$, attained at $x=3$.

This answer has the same premise ($x>0$, which makes the denominator positive), a reason (a square is never negative), the equality condition and the conclusion, only without citing AM–GM.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Using the discriminant: when does the horizontal line reach the curve?">

Turn the picture into an equation: does $x+\frac{9}{x}=c$ have a positive solution? Since $x>0$, we have $x\ne 0$, so multiplying both sides by $x$ does not change the solutions, giving $x^2-cx+9=0$.

- For a real solution, the discriminant must satisfy $c^2-36\ge 0$, that is $c\ge 6$ or $c\le -6$.
- When $x>0$, $c=x+\frac{9}{x}>0$, so $c\le -6$ is impossible and only $c\ge 6$ remains.
- Conversely, when $c\ge 6$ the product of the roots is $9>0$ and their sum is $c>0$, so both roots are positive and a positive solution really exists.

So the values $x+\frac{9}{x}$ can take are exactly $c\ge 6$, and the minimum is $6$; at $c=6$, $x^2-6x+9=(x-3)^2=0$ gives $x=3$, the point where the dashed line touches the curve. Notice that before multiplying by $x$ in the first step we wrote $x\ne 0$: that is "make every step checkable".

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="How much detail do I need? Do I write out every addition?">

You don't need every addition. What matters is that the key steps of the reasoning are visible: which conditions you used, which properties you cited, and why each step is allowed. The marking principles ask for "correct reasoning and a detailed process", so write one sentence too many rather than skip a key step.

</StudentAsks>

## The steps where points are most often lost

Many lost points are not arithmetic slips. A step is missing a premise, so the inference fails:

| Step | What can go wrong | What to add |
|---|---|---|
| Dividing both sides by an expression | The expression might be $0$ | Say why it is not $0$, or treat the case where it is $0$ separately |
| Squaring both sides | Solutions the original equation does not have can appear | Check every candidate in the original equation |
| Cancelling | The denominator might be $0$ | Write down the condition that the denominator is not $0$ first |
| Comparing two function values | Only the result, no reason | Cite whether the function increases or decreases on that range |

Back to $x^2=3x$ from the opening: the real solutions of the equation are the $x$-coordinates of the points where the parabola $y=x^2$ meets the line $y=3x$. The graph has two intersection points, so there are two solutions; after dividing both sides by $x$ only one is left, and the one that disappears is exactly the point at $x=0$.

<Graph desc="The parabola y=x² and the line y=3x meet at two points, (0,0) and (3,9), so x²=3x has two real solutions, x=0 and x=3. Dividing both sides by x keeps only (3,9); the intersection at the origin is lost." x={[-2, 5]} y={[-2, 12]}
  functions={[{ expr: 'x^2', label: 'y=x²' }, { expr: '3*x', color: 'amber', label: 'y=3x' }]}
  points={[{ at: [0, 0], label: '(0, 0)' }, { at: [3, 9], label: '(3, 9)' }]} />

**Example.** Solve $\sqrt{x+2}=x$.

Squaring gives $x+2=x^2$, that is $x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1)=0$, so the candidates are $x=2$ and $x=-1$. Squaring can create false solutions, so check them in the original equation:

- $x=2$: the left side is $\sqrt{4}=2$ and the right side is $2$, so it holds.
- $x=-1$: the left side is $\sqrt{1}=1$ and the right side is $-1$. They are not equal, so it is rejected.

So the solution is $x=2$. When you write "$x=-1$ is rejected", also write why.

The graph shows where $x=-1$ comes from: after squaring, $x+2=x^2$ cannot tell whether the original was $\sqrt{x+2}=x$ or $-\sqrt{x+2}=x$, so the intersection with the lower half of the curve sneaks in too.

<Graph desc="The solid curve y=√(x+2) is the half-curve rising to the right from (−2,0); it meets the line y=x only at (2,2). The dashed curve y=−√(x+2) is the lower half, and it meets y=x at (−1,−1). The squared equation x+2=x² counts both halves, which is where the extra x=−1 comes from; the original equation is only the solid half, so its only solution is x=2." x={[-3, 5]} y={[-3, 4]}
  functions={[{ expr: 'sqrt(x+2)', domain: [-2, 5], label: 'y=√(x+2)' }, { expr: '-sqrt(x+2)', domain: [-2, 5], color: 'amber', dashed: true, label: 'y=−√(x+2)' }, { expr: 'x', color: 'ink', label: 'y=x' }]}
  points={[{ at: [2, 2], label: '(2, 2)' }, { at: [-1, -1], label: '(−1, −1)', color: 'amber' }]} />

<Perspective title="Check the sign first, and no checking afterwards is needed">

The right side $x$ equals a square root, and a square root is never negative, so first we get the condition $x\ge 0$. Under $x\ge 0$, neither side is negative; two non-negative numbers are equal if and only if their squares are equal, so squaring adds no solutions:

$$
\sqrt{x+2}=x \iff x+2=x^2\ \text{and}\ x\ge 0
$$

($x+2=x^2\ge 0$ also guarantees that the square root is defined.) Solving $x^2-x-2=0$ gives $x=2$ or $x=-1$, and only $x=2$ lies in the range $x\ge 0$.

<NumberLine desc="The part x≥0 of the number line is shaded, with a filled dot at 0. The candidate −1 lies outside the shaded range, so it is rejected; 2 lies inside, so it is the solution." from={-3} to={4} intervals={[{ a: 0, b: 'inf', open: [false, false] }]} points={[{ x: -1, label: '−1', open: true }, { x: 2, label: '2' }]} />

This way of writing moves the check to before the squaring: first state the condition $x\ge 0$ and its reason (a square root is non-negative), then square. On the answer sheet, checking afterwards and stating the condition first are both complete answers; pick one and write it clearly.

</Perspective>

Comparing sizes works the same way: writing only "$\sin 20^\circ<\sin 40^\circ$" without saying "sine is strictly increasing from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$" leaves out the key step.

That reason can be seen on the unit circle: the terminal side of the angle $\theta$ meets the unit circle at $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, and $\sin\theta$ is the height of that point. Drag $\theta$: as the angle turns from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$, the point climbs all the way up.

The larger θ is, the higher P is: that is sine strictly increasing from 0° to 90°." x={[-0.2, 1.3]} y={[-0.2, 1.2]}

<Perspective title="As a function graph: the rising part of y=sin x">

In radians, $20^\circ=\frac{\pi}{9}$ and $40^\circ=\frac{2\pi}{9}$. The graph of $y=\sin x$ rises all the way on $0\le x\le\frac{\pi}{2}$, from $(0,0)$ up to $\left(\frac{\pi}{2},1\right)$; $\frac{\pi}{9}$ and $\frac{2\pi}{9}$ both lie on this part and $\frac{\pi}{9}<\frac{2\pi}{9}$, so $\sin\frac{\pi}{9}<\sin\frac{2\pi}{9}$.

<Graph desc="The graph of y=sin x for 0≤x≤π: it rises from (0,0) to the highest point (π/2,1), then falls to (π,0). The graph also shows why the reason must come with an interval: $\frac{2\pi}{3}>\frac{\pi}{3}$, yet $\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}=\sin\frac{\pi}{3}$, because $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ is already on the falling part.

</Perspective>

## Draw the diagram right before writing equations

The marking principles give zero for using data that contradicts the problem. In a context problem, if the first sketch does not match the stated conditions, the equations written from it go wrong too, and however neat the working, it cannot rescue the answer.

## Can't finish? Write down the reasoning anyway

Even if you cannot finish a sub-item, the correct setup and reasoning are worth writing down, because the marking principles give partial credit for correct reasoning that does not reach the final answer.

**Example (our own item set).** A rectangular flower bed is fenced with $40$ metres of fencing against a long wall; the side along the wall needs no fence. Let the sides perpendicular to the wall be $x$ metres.

First draw the diagram right: the wall is along the bottom and the fence covers only the other three sides, two perpendicular to the wall and one parallel to it. Drag $x$ and watch the two side lengths change together.

<Graph desc="The rectangular flower bed against the wall: the horizontal line on the x-axis is the wall, and the other three sides are fence. The slider x is the side perpendicular to the wall; the side parallel to the wall is 40−2x, and the three pieces of fence always add up to x+(40−2x)+x=40. At the start x=6, the side parallel to the wall is 28 and the area is 168; as x gets close to 0 or 20 the rectangle flattens and the area gets close to 0." x={[-4, 43]} y={[-4, 23]} height={260}
  params={[{ name: 's', min: 1, max: 19, step: 0.5, value: 6, label: 'x' }]}
  polygons={[{ points: [[0, 0], ['40-2*s', 0], ['40-2*s', 's'], [0, 's']] }]}
  segments={[{ from: [-4, 0], to: [43, 0], color: 'ink' }, { from: [0, 0], to: [0, 's'], color: 'red' }, { from: [0, 's'], to: ['40-2*s', 's'], color: 'red' }, { from: ['40-2*s', 's'], to: ['40-2*s', 0], color: 'red' }]}
  labels={[{ at: [18, -3.5], text: 'wall' }, { at: [-3.5, 's/2'], text: 'x' }, { at: ['20-s-3', 's+0.5'], text: '40−2x' }]} />

1. The side parallel to the wall is $40-2x$ metres, so the area is $A=x(40-2x)$ square metres.
2. Both side lengths must be positive: $x>0$ and $40-2x>0$, so $0<x<20$.
3. Find the largest possible area, and explain why.

A complete answer to part $3$: $A=-2x^2+40x$ is a <Term t="math.quadratic-function">quadratic function</Term> whose graph opens downwards. By <Term t="math.completing-the-square">completing the square</Term>,

$$
A=-2(x^2-20x)=-2(x-10)^2+200
$$

so $A\le 200$, with equality at $x=10$. Since $x=10$ lies in the range $0<x<20$, the largest area is $200$ square metres, with sides of $10$ metres perpendicular to the wall and $20$ metres parallel to it.

Plot $A$ against $x$, and the result of completing the square is the highest point of the graph:

<Graph desc="The horizontal axis is x (0<x<20) and the vertical axis is the area A. The curve A=x(40−2x)=−2(x−10)²+200 is a parabola opening downwards; it meets the horizontal axis at x=0 and x=20, and its highest point is (10,200). The slider x starts at 6, with the point at (6,168); drag it to 10 and the point rises to the highest point, area 200." x={[-2, 22]} y={[-20, 230]}
  params={[{ name: 's', min: 0.5, max: 19.5, step: 0.5, value: 6, label: 'x' }]}
  functions={[{ expr: 'x*(40-2*x)', domain: [0, 20], label: 'A=x(40−2x)' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [10, 0], to: [10, 200], color: 'amber', dashed: true }]}
  points={[{ at: ['s', 's*(40-2*s)'], label: '(x, A)' }, { at: [10, 200], label: '(10, 200)', color: 'amber' }]} />

<Perspective title="By symmetry: halfway between the two zeros">

$A=x(40-2x)$ is $0$ at $x=0$ and at $x=20$, the two points where the graph meets the horizontal axis. A parabola is symmetric about its axis, and these two points are at the same height, so the axis is exactly halfway between them: $x=\frac{0+20}{2}=10$. The parabola opens downwards, so the vertex is the highest point, $A(10)=10\times 20=200$.

On the answer sheet, "$x=0$ and $x=20$ are the roots of $A=0$; the parabola is symmetric about their midpoint $x=10$ and opens downwards, so $A$ is largest at $x=10$" is just as complete a reason, with no completing the square; and again you must check that $x=10$ lies in $0<x<20$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="By AM–GM: two parts with a fixed sum">

Since $0<x<20$, both $2x$ and $40-2x$ are positive, and their sum is fixed: $2x+(40-2x)=40$. By AM–GM,

$$
\sqrt{2x(40-2x)}\le\frac{2x+(40-2x)}{2}=20
$$

so $2x(40-2x)\le 400$ and $A=x(40-2x)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2x(40-2x)\le 200$. Equality holds when $2x=40-2x$, that is $x=10$, which lies in the range, so the largest area is $200$ square metres.

This is the same tool as the chapter's first example: for two positive numbers, a fixed product gives the sum a minimum, and a fixed sum gives the product a maximum. Why split into $2x$ and $40-2x$ rather than $x$ and $40-2x$? Because $x+(40-2x)=40-x$ is not constant, so the bound AM–GM gives would change with $x$ and could not give a maximum.

</Perspective>

If completing the square gets stuck, you can still write the expression and range from parts $1$ and $2$, plus correct reasoning such as "$A$ is a quadratic function opening downwards, so its maximum is at the vertex". Finally, don't forget to check that the vertex's $x$ lies in the range: that is part of "make every step checkable".
