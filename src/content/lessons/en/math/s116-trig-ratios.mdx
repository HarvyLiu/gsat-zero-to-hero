## Ratios of an acute angle

In right triangle $ABC$ with $\angle C=90^\circ$, it is usual to name the side opposite each angle: $a=\overline{BC}$, $b=\overline{CA}$, $c=\overline{AB}$, where $c$ is the hypotenuse. For the acute angle $A$, $a$ is the **opposite** side and $b$ is the **adjacent** side:

$$
\sin A=\frac{a}{c},\qquad \cos A=\frac{b}{c},\qquad \tan A=\frac{a}{b}
$$

In words: <Term t="math.sine">sine</Term> is opposite over hypotenuse, <Term t="math.cosine">cosine</Term> is adjacent over hypotenuse, and <Term t="math.tangent">tangent</Term> is opposite over adjacent. Together they are the **trigonometric ratios** <Zh>三角比</Zh>.

Why do the ratios depend only on the angle? Two right triangles with one equal acute angle are similar (AA similarity), so their corresponding sides have the same ratios. However large you draw the triangle, $\sin A$ has the same value.

**Example.** In right triangle $ABC$, $\angle C=90^\circ$, $\overline{AB}=17$ and $\overline{BC}=8$. Find the three trigonometric ratios of $\angle A$.

First find the adjacent side with the Pythagorean theorem: $\overline{AC}=\sqrt{17^2-8^2}=\sqrt{225}=15$. So

$$
\sin A=\frac{8}{17},\qquad \cos A=\frac{15}{17},\qquad \tan A=\frac{8}{15}
$$

Look at $\angle B$ as well: its opposite side is $\overline{AC}=15$, so $\sin B=\frac{15}{17}=\cos A$. That is no coincidence; the complementary-angle identities below explain it.

<Perspective title="Make the hypotenuse 1: the ratios become lengths">

The ratios don't change with the size of the triangle, so we may scale the right triangle until its hypotenuse is $1$. Then $\sin A$ is the opposite side divided by $1$, which is just the length of the opposite side; $\cos A$ is just the length of the adjacent side. Put $A$ at the origin with the adjacent side along the positive $x$-axis: the vertex $B$ then lies on the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin (the **unit circle** <Zh>單位圓</Zh>), at $(\cos A,\sin A)$, and the Pythagorean theorem gives $\cos^2A+\sin^2A=1$ at once.

The tangent can be seen as a length too. Extend the hypotenuse $\overline{AB}$ to meet the line $x=1$ at $T$. The right triangle with vertices $A$, $(1,0)$ and $T$ has adjacent side $1$ and is similar to the original one, so the height of $T$ is $\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}=\tan A$; that is, $T=(1,\tan A)$.

For $A=60^\circ$: $B=\left(\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$ and $T=(1,\sqrt{3})$, matching the special-angle table. Dragging the angle in the figure below also shows directly that as the angle grows, $\sin A$ grows, $\cos A$ shrinks, and $\tan A$ grows faster and faster. Following this trend, as the angle approaches $90^\circ$, $T$ keeps climbing and $\tan A$ has no upper bound.

### Special-angle values

You should be able to write down the ratios of $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ straight away. They come from two special right triangles:

- The isosceles right triangle, with sides in the ratio $1:1:\sqrt{2}$ and both acute angles $45^\circ$.
- Half of an equilateral triangle, cut from a vertex: a right triangle with sides in the ratio $1:\sqrt{3}:2$, where the side opposite the $30^\circ$ angle is $1$ and the side opposite the $60^\circ$ angle is $\sqrt{3}$.

<StudentAsks q="I can never memorise this table. Is there an easy way?">

The cosine row is the same list reversed. You don't need to memorise tangent: divide, using $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$. If you really forget, sketch the $1:\sqrt{3}:2$ triangle in the margin and read off the side ratios.

## Ratios of a generalised angle

A right triangle only has acute angles. To talk about the ratios of $150^\circ$ or $-60^\circ$, put the angle in the coordinate plane with its vertex at the origin and its initial side along the positive $x$-axis (this is **standard position** <Zh>標準位置</Zh>). Pick any point $P(x,y)$ other than the origin on the <Term t="math.terminal-side">terminal side</Term>, let $r=\overline{OP}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$, and define

$$
\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\ (x\ne 0)
$$

When $\theta$ is acute, $x$, $y$ and $r$ are exactly the adjacent side, the opposite side and the hypotenuse, so this agrees with the right-triangle definition. Read the other way, the point on the terminal side at distance $r$ from the origin is $(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)$. This is the conversion formula last chapter promised: the point with <Term t="math.polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</Term> $[r,\theta]$ has rectangular coordinates $(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)$, with the signs built in, so it works even when the angle is not special.

As last chapter mentioned, the curriculum first limits <Term t="math.generalized-angle">generalised angles</Term> to the range $-180^\circ$ to $360^\circ$, and the examples in this chapter stay in that range.

**Example.** The point $P(-1,2)$ lies on the terminal side of an angle $\theta$ in standard position. Find the three trigonometric ratios of $\theta$.

$r=\sqrt{(-1)^2+2^2}=\sqrt{5}$, so

$$
\sin\theta=\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}=\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}}=-\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{2}{-1}=-2
$$

<StudentAsks q="The terminal side has infinitely many points. Do different points give different answers?">

No. If you take $Q(-2,4)$ instead, then $r=2\sqrt{5}$ and $\sin\theta=\dfrac{4}{2\sqrt{5}}=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}}$, the same as with $P$. When a point on the terminal side moves to some multiple of its distance from the origin, $x$ and $y$ are multiplied by the same factor, so the ratios stay the same. This is the same fact as "side ratios of similar triangles don't change".

</StudentAsks>

$r$ is always positive, so the sign of each ratio depends only on $x$ and $y$:

| Terminal side in | Quadrant I | Quadrant II | Quadrant III | Quadrant IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin\theta$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |

The point with $r=1$ is the handiest: where the terminal side meets the unit circle (the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin) is exactly $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. When the terminal side lies on an axis, read the values from the point with $r=1$:

| $\theta$ | $0^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $180^\circ$ | $270^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Point on the terminal side | $(1,0)$ | $(0,1)$ | $(-1,0)$ | $(0,-1)$ |
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | $-1$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $0$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $0$ | undefined | $0$ | undefined |

On the terminal sides of $90^\circ$ and $270^\circ$, $x=0$, so $\dfrac{y}{x}$ has a zero denominator and the tangent is undefined.

### The ratios of any angle

You don't have to find a point every time. When the terminal side is not on an axis (if it is, use the table above), do this: find the acute angle between the terminal side and the $x$-axis, work out that acute angle's ratio, then give it the sign of the terminal side's quadrant.

**Example.** Find $\sin 150^\circ$, $\cos 150^\circ$ and $\tan 150^\circ$.

The terminal side of $150^\circ$ is in the second quadrant, $180^\circ-150^\circ=30^\circ$ from the negative $x$-axis. In the second quadrant only sine is positive, so

$$
\sin 150^\circ=\sin 30^\circ=\frac{1}{2},\qquad \cos 150^\circ=-\cos 30^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\qquad \tan 150^\circ=-\tan 30^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$

Check with a point: $P(-\sqrt{3},1)$ is on the terminal side with $r=2$, and it gives exactly the same values.

<Perspective title="By symmetry: the points in the four quadrants are mirror images">

On the unit circle, the terminal side of $\theta$ meets the circle at $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. The point for $30^\circ$ is $\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$. Reflect it in the $y$-axis to get $\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$; its terminal side makes $30^\circ$ with the negative $x$-axis, which is exactly the terminal side of $150^\circ$. So just read the coordinates:

$$
\cos 150^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\qquad \sin 150^\circ=\frac{1}{2},\qquad \tan 150^\circ=\frac{1/2}{-\sqrt{3}/2}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
$$

In the same way, reflecting in the $x$-axis gives the point for $-30^\circ$, $\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$, and reflecting through the origin gives the point for $210^\circ$, $\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$. The four points form a rectangle; their coordinates have the same absolute values and differ only in sign. That is why "work out the acute angle, then give it the quadrant's sign" works: the reference acute angle decides the size, the quadrant decides the sign.

## Basic identities between the ratios

From the definitions $\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}$, $\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}$ and $x^2+y^2=r^2$, every angle $\theta$ satisfies:

- **Pythagorean identity** <Zh>平方關係</Zh>: $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$. (Divide both sides of $x^2+y^2=r^2$ by $r^2$.)
- **Quotient identity** <Zh>商數關係</Zh>: $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ ($\cos\theta\ne 0$). ($\dfrac{y/r}{x/r}=\dfrac{y}{x}$.)

For an acute angle $\theta$, the two acute angles of a right triangle are complementary, and the side opposite one is adjacent to the other, so we also have

- **Complementary-angle identities** <Zh>餘角關係</Zh>: $\sin(90^\circ-\theta)=\cos\theta$, $\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta$, $\tan(90^\circ-\theta)=\dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}$.

For example, $\sin^2 20^\circ+\sin^2 70^\circ=\sin^2 20^\circ+\cos^2 20^\circ=1$, with no calculator needed.

<Perspective title="See the complementary-angle identities as a reflection">

On the unit circle, the terminal side of $\theta$ makes the angle $\theta$ with the positive $x$-axis, and the terminal side of $90^\circ-\theta$ makes the angle $\theta$ with the positive $y$-axis, so the two terminal sides are symmetric about the line $y=x$. Reflecting in $y=x$ swaps the coordinates: if the point for $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, the point for $90^\circ-\theta$ is $(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$. Reading the coordinates gives

$$
\cos(90^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta,\qquad \sin(90^\circ-\theta)=\cos\theta,\qquad \tan(90^\circ-\theta)=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=\frac{1}{\tan\theta}
$$

For example, the points for $20^\circ$ and $70^\circ$ have swapped coordinates, so $\sin 70^\circ=\cos 20^\circ$. This view needs no right triangle, and the reflection works for every angle, so the complementary-angle identities in fact hold for all $\theta$ (the tangent one needs both sides to be defined).

**Example.** Let $90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ with $\sin\theta=\dfrac{1}{3}$. Find $\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.

By the Pythagorean identity, $\cos^2\theta=1-\dfrac{1}{9}=\dfrac{8}{9}$. $\theta$ is in the second quadrant, so $\cos\theta<0$ and

$$
\cos\theta=-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
$$

A square root has two possible signs; always let the quadrant choose.

<Perspective title="Graphically: the line y=1/3 cuts the unit circle">

The terminal side of $\theta$ meets the unit circle at $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. $\sin\theta=\dfrac{1}{3}$ says this point lies on the horizontal line $y=\dfrac{1}{3}$. That line meets the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ at two points: $x^2=1-\dfrac{1}{9}=\dfrac{8}{9}$, so $x=\pm\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}$. The right-hand point's terminal side is in the first quadrant, the left-hand one's in the second. We need $90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$, so take the left point $\left(-\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{3},\dfrac{1}{3}\right)$ and read off

$$
\cos\theta=-\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}=\frac{1/3}{-2\sqrt{2}/3}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
$$

The "$\pm$" of the square root in the algebra is the pair of intersection points in the picture; the quadrant decides which point to take.

**Example.** Given $\sin\theta+\cos\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}$, find $\sin\theta\cos\theta$.

Square both sides: $\sin^2\theta+2\sin\theta\cos\theta+\cos^2\theta=\dfrac{1}{4}$. The Pythagorean identity turns $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta$ into $1$:

$$
1+2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\frac{1}{4}\iff \sin\theta\cos\theta=-\frac{3}{8}
$$

<Perspective title="Graphically: the line x+y=1/2 cuts the unit circle">

Write $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ as $(x,y)$. The question then asks for $xy$ at the intersections of the line $x+y=\dfrac{1}{2}$ and the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$. Solve the system directly: substitute $y=\dfrac{1}{2}-x$ into the circle's equation,

$$
x^2+\left(\frac{1}{2}-x\right)^2=1\iff 8x^2-4x-3=0\iff x=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{7}}{4}
$$

The matching $y=\dfrac{1}{2}-x=\dfrac{1\mp\sqrt{7}}{4}$, so the two intersections are $\left(\dfrac{1+\sqrt{7}}{4},\dfrac{1-\sqrt{7}}{4}\right)$ and $\left(\dfrac{1-\sqrt{7}}{4},\dfrac{1+\sqrt{7}}{4}\right)$. Whichever point you take,

$$
xy=\frac{(1+\sqrt{7})(1-\sqrt{7})}{16}=\frac{1-7}{16}=-\frac{3}{8}
$$

the same answer as squaring. The picture tells us two more things: there are two angles $\theta$ that fit, one in the fourth quadrant and one in the second, but they have the same $\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so the answer is unique; and $\sin\theta\cos\theta<0$ matches the fact that at both points one coordinate is positive and the other negative. To find $\sin\theta\cos\theta$, squaring is faster; to know where $\theta$ is, solve the system.

<StudentAsks q="What does sin²θ mean? Is it sin(θ²)?">

No. $\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$: find $\sin\theta$, then square it. It is not $\sin(\theta^2)$. Also, $\sin\theta$ is one thing, not "$\sin$ times $\theta$", so you cannot cancel the $\theta$ in $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta}$.
