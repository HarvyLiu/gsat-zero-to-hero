## The division algorithm

For integers, dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder, with $0\le$ remainder $<$ divisor. Polynomials work the same way, except that "the remainder is smaller" becomes "the remainder has lower degree":

Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be polynomials, with $g(x)$ not the zero polynomial. Then there is exactly one pair of polynomials $q(x)$, $r(x)$ with

$$
f(x)=g(x)\,q(x)+r(x)
$$

where $r(x)=0$ or $\deg r(x)<\deg g(x)$. $q(x)$ is the **quotient** <Zh>商式</Zh> and $r(x)$ is the **remainder** <Zh>餘式</Zh>. This is the **division algorithm** <Zh>除法原理</Zh>.

So when the divisor is quadratic, the remainder is at most linear and you can set it up as $ax+b$; when the divisor is linear, the remainder is always a constant.

**Example.** Find the quotient and remainder when $2x^3+x^2+3x-1$ is divided by $x^2+x-1$.

Use long division, removing the highest-degree term at each step:

- The first term of the quotient is $2x$: $2x^3+x^2+3x-1-2x(x^2+x-1)=-x^2+5x-1$.
- The next term of the quotient is $-1$: $-x^2+5x-1-(-1)(x^2+x-1)=6x-2$.

$6x-2$ has degree one, lower than the divisor's degree two, so we stop. The quotient is $2x-1$ and the remainder is $6x-2$:

$$
2x^3+x^2+3x-1=(x^2+x-1)(2x-1)+(6x-2)
$$

A picture of why the quotient can only be $2x-1$: guess a linear quotient $mx+n$ and graph what is left over, $y=f(x)-(x^2+x-1)(mx+n)$. If $m$ is wrong, the leftover still has an $x^3$ term and its graph is a cubic curve. With $m=2$, $n=0$ the leftover is $-x^2+5x-1$, a parabola: exactly the first step of the long division. Only with $m=2$, $n=-1$ does the leftover become the straight line $y=6x-2$. The remainder must have lower degree than the divisor, so this is the only guess where you can stop. That is what "exactly one pair" means.

Move m and n: when m is not 2 the solid curve is a cubic, when m = 2 and n = 0 it is the parabola y = −x² + 5x − 1, and only when m = 2 and n = −1 does it become a straight line lying on the dashed one. <Perspective title="Substitute x = 10: polynomial division becomes integer division">

The division algorithm's identity holds for every $x$, so you can check it by substituting a number. Put $x=10$ and the polynomials turn into ordinary base-ten numbers:

$$
f(10)=2129,\quad g(10)=109,\quad q(10)=19,\quad r(10)=58
$$

And $109\times 19+58=2071+58=2129$ with $58<109$: exactly the integer division $2129\div 109$, quotient $19$, remainder $58$. The powers of $x$ in a polynomial play the role of the digit places of an integer, and each step of long division removes the highest-degree term just as each step of long division of numbers deals with the highest place.

This is only a **check**, not a method: coefficients can be negative or larger than $9$, so after substituting $x=10$ the result need not satisfy the integer rule $0\le$ remainder $<$ divisor. What really tells you to stop is still "the remainder has lower degree than the divisor".

</Perspective>

## Synthetic division

When the divisor is $x-a$, long division can be done with the coefficients alone. This is <Term t="math.synthetic-division">synthetic division</Term>. The curriculum notes say that synthetic division uses only divisors of the form $x-a$.

**Example.** Use synthetic division to find the quotient and remainder when $2x^3-5x^2+7$ is divided by $x-2$.

The dividend has no $x$ term, so that coefficient is filled in with $0$: write $2,\,-5,\,0,\,7$, and take $a=2$ from the divisor $x-2$. Copy the first coefficient down; after that, multiply the number you just got by $2$ and add it to the next coefficient:

$$
\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
2 & -5 & 0 & 7 & \ 2 \\
  & 4 & -2 & -4 & \\ \hline
2 & -1 & -2 & 3 &
\end{array}
$$

The first three numbers on the bottom row are the coefficients of the quotient, and the last one is the remainder: the quotient is $2x^2-x-2$ and the remainder is $3$.

<StudentAsks q="Why do I have to fill in 0 for a missing term?">

Synthetic division works by position: each number stands for the coefficient of one particular power. Leave out a $0$ and every coefficient after it slides into the wrong place, just as dropping the middle $0$ from $207$ turns it into $27$. So write the coefficients of $x^3$, $x^2$, $x$ and the constant in order first, with $0$ for any that are missing.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Synthetic division is evaluating layer by layer">

Factor $x$ out of $2x^3-5x^2+7$ layer by layer:

$$
2x^3-5x^2+0x+7=\big((2x-5)x+0\big)x+7
$$

Now substitute $x=2$ and work from the innermost layer outwards:

- Innermost: $2$.
- Times $2$, plus $-5$: $2\cdot 2-5=-1$.
- Times $2$, plus $0$: $(-1)\cdot 2+0=-2$.
- Times $2$, plus $7$: $(-2)\cdot 2+7=3$.

The numbers you get, $2,\,-1,\,-2,\,3$, are exactly the bottom row of the synthetic division. So "multiply by $a$, add the next coefficient" is this nested way of computing $f(a)$, and the last number is both the remainder and $f(2)$. That is a preview of the remainder theorem in the next section.

</Perspective>

## The remainder theorem

Apply the division algorithm with the divisor $x-a$. The remainder is a constant $r$:

$$
f(x)=(x-a)\,q(x)+r
$$

Substitute $x=a$. The first term becomes $0$, which leaves $f(a)=r$. This is the <Term t="math.remainder-theorem">remainder theorem</Term>:

$$
\text{the remainder of } f(x) \text{ divided by } x-a \text{ equals } f(a)
$$

For the example above, $f(2)=16-20+7=3$, the same remainder synthetic division gave. By the same reasoning, when the divisor is $ax-b$ ($a\ne 0$), substitute the value that makes the divisor $0$, $x=\frac{b}{a}$: the remainder is $f\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$.

Graphically, $f(a)$ is the height of the curve $y=f(x)$ at $x=a$, so **the remainder is that height**. Shift the whole curve down by $f(a)$ and you get $y=f(x)-f(a)=(x-a)\,q(x)$, which must pass through $(a,0)$ because $x-a$ is a factor of it.

The dashed curve is y = f(x) − f(a), which always passes through (a, 0). At the start a = 2, the point is (2, 3) and the remainder is 3; drag a to −1 and the point lands on the x-axis, so the remainder is 0." x={[-2, 3.5]} y={[-12, 18]} height={360}

At $a=-1$, $f(-1)=-2-5+7=0$ and the point lands on the $x$-axis: the remainder is $0$, so $x+1$ divides $2x^3-5x^2+7$. That is the factor theorem coming up below.

**Example.** Find the remainder when $x^5-3x^3+2x+1$ is divided by $x+1$.

$x+1=x-(-1)$, so $a=-1$, not $1$. The remainder is

$$
f(-1)=-1+3-2+1=1
$$

**Example.** A polynomial $f(x)$ leaves remainder $3$ when divided by $x-1$ and remainder $5$ when divided by $x-2$. Find the remainder when $f(x)$ is divided by $(x-1)(x-2)$.

The divisor is quadratic, so the remainder is at most linear. Set it up as $ax+b$:

$$
f(x)=(x-1)(x-2)\,q(x)+ax+b
$$

By the remainder theorem, $f(1)=3$ and $f(2)=5$. Substituting $x=1$ and $x=2$ makes the first term $0$ each time:

$$
a+b=3,\qquad 2a+b=5
$$

So $a=2$ and $b=1$, and the remainder is $2x+1$.

<Perspective title="Graphically: the remainder is the line through two points">

$f(x)-(ax+b)=(x-1)(x-2)\,q(x)$ is $0$ at $x=1$ and at $x=2$, so the curve $y=f(x)$ and the line $y=ax+b$ meet at $x=1$ and $x=2$, at the points $(1,f(1))=(1,3)$ and $(2,f(2))=(2,5)$. So the remainder $y=ax+b$ is **the line through $(1,3)$ and $(2,5)$**:

$$
\text{slope}=\frac{5-3}{2-1}=2,\qquad y-3=2(x-1)\ \Rightarrow\ y=2x+1
$$

No simultaneous equations needed: the point-slope form from the chapter on lines gives the same answer. In the figure, every $f(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-c)+2x+1$ meets the conditions of the problem; however you move $c$, the curve passes through both points and the remainder line stays the same.

  points={[{ at: [1, 3], label: '(1, 3)', color: 'amber' }, { at: [2, 5], label: '(2, 5)', color: 'amber' }]} />

<StudentAsks q="When dividing by x+1, why substitute −1 and not 1?">

The $a$ in the remainder theorem is the number that makes the divisor $0$. $x+1=0$ when $x=-1$, so you substitute $-1$. A way to remember: ask yourself "for which $x$ is the divisor $0$?", and substitute that number.

</StudentAsks>

## The factor theorem

A remainder of $0$ means the division is exact. So the remainder theorem gives the <Term t="math.factor-theorem">factor theorem</Term> straight away:

$$
x-a \text{ is a factor of } f(x) \iff f(a)=0
$$

To factor a cubic, first find by trial an $a$ with $f(a)=0$, then divide out $x-a$ with synthetic division and factor the quadratic that is left. If the coefficients are integers and the leading coefficient is $1$, any integer $a$ with $f(a)=0$ must be a divisor of the constant term, so start by trying the positive and negative divisors of the constant term.

**Example.** Factor $x^3-2x^2-5x+6$.

The divisors of the constant term $6$ are $\pm 1$, $\pm 2$, $\pm 3$ and $\pm 6$. Try $x=1$: $f(1)=1-2-5+6=0$, so $x-1$ is a factor. Divide by $x-1$ with synthetic division:

$$
\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
1 & -2 & -5 & 6 & \ 1 \\
  & 1 & -1 & -6 & \\ \hline
1 & -1 & -6 & 0 &
\end{array}
$$

The quotient is $x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)$, so

$$
x^3-2x^2-5x+6=(x-1)(x-3)(x+2)
$$

<Perspective title="Graphically: linear factors are the crossings of the x-axis">

$f(a)=0$ means the point $(a,0)$ lies on the curve $y=f(x)$. So each linear factor $x-a$ of $f(x)$ corresponds to a point where the graph meets the $x$-axis. Plotting a few points narrows down what to try: $f(0)=6$ is above the $x$-axis and $f(2)=8-8-10+6=-4$ is below it. The curve goes from above to below, so it must cross the $x$-axis in between, and the candidate to try first is $1$, between $0$ and $2$. In the same way, $f(-3)=-24$ and $f(-1)=8$ point to $-2$, and $f(2)=-4$, $f(4)=18$ point to $3$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Why an integer root must divide the constant term">

Suppose an integer $a$ has $f(a)=a^3-2a^2-5a+6=0$. Move the terms containing $a$ to one side:

$$
a^3-2a^2-5a=-6\ \Rightarrow\ a\,(a^2-2a-5)=-6
$$

$a$ is an integer and so is $a^2-2a-5$; two integers multiply to $-6$, so $a$ must be a divisor of $6$. The general case is the same: when the coefficients are integers, an integer $a$ with $f(a)=0$ gives $a\times(\text{an integer})=-(\text{constant term})$. That is the reason for "try the positive and negative divisors of the constant term first". Note that when the leading coefficient is not $1$, there may also be fractional roots (from a factor like $2x-1$), and trying only integers would miss them.

</Perspective>

## Writing a polynomial in powers of $x-a$

Sometimes you need to rewrite $f(x)$ in powers of $x-a$, for example $f(x)=c_3(x-a)^3+c_2(x-a)^2+c_1(x-a)+c_0$. The method is repeated synthetic division: divide by $x-a$ again and again. The remainders you get, in order, are $c_0$, $c_1$, $c_2$, and the quotient left at the end is $c_3$.

**Example.** Write $f(x)=x^3-3x^2+5x-2$ in powers of $x-1$.

Divide by $x-1$ repeatedly:

- $1,\,-3,\,5,\,-2$ divided by $x-1$ gives quotient coefficients $1,\,-2,\,3$ and remainder $1$.
- $1,\,-2,\,3$ divided by $x-1$ again gives quotient coefficients $1,\,-1$ and remainder $2$.
- $1,\,-1$ divided by $x-1$ again gives quotient $1$ and remainder $0$.

So

$$
f(x)=(x-1)^3+0\cdot(x-1)^2+2(x-1)+1=(x-1)^3+2(x-1)+1
$$

Expanding $(x-1)^3=x^3-3x^2+3x-1$ and adding $2x-2+1$ brings you back to exactly $x^3-3x^2+5x-2$.

<Perspective title="Change the variable: put t = x − 1 and expand">

You can also do it without division: put $t=x-1$, that is $x=t+1$, substitute and expand:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t+1)&=(t+1)^3-3(t+1)^2+5(t+1)-2\\
&=(t^3+3t^2+3t+1)-(3t^2+6t+3)+(5t+5)-2\\
&=t^3+0\cdot t^2+2t+1
\end{aligned}
$$

Changing back to $t=x-1$ gives the same $(x-1)^3+2(x-1)+1$. For low degrees, substituting and expanding is often quicker than repeated synthetic division; for higher degrees, repeated synthetic division makes it harder to drop a term.

On the graph, $t=x-1$ just moves the starting point for measuring the horizontal coordinate from $x=0$ to $x=1$: the curve $y=t^3+2t+1$ has exactly the same shape as $y=f(x)$, only shifted sideways. $c_0=1$ is $f(1)$, the point where the curve meets the vertical axis in the new coordinate.

<Perspective later title="Zoom in at x = 1: the first two terms are the approximating line" needs="approximating a graph near a point by a straight line (graphs of cubic functions)" href="/en/math/s116-cubic-functions/">

When $x$ is very close to $1$, $(x-1)^3$ is much smaller than $2(x-1)$, so

$$
f(x)=1+2(x-1)+(x-1)^3\approx 1+2(x-1)=2x-1
$$

Near $x=1$ the curve $y=f(x)$ almost coincides with the line $y=2x-1$. For example $f(1.01)\approx 1+2(0.01)=1.02$, and the true value is $1.02+(0.01)^3=1.020001$. When a polynomial is written in powers of $x-a$, the constant term $c_0$ is the height of the point and $c_1$ is the slope of the approximating line.
