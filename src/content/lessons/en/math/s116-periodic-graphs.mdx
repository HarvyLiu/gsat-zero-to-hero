## The graph of the sine function

Put the angle $x$ (in radians) in standard position. Its <Term t="math.terminal-side">terminal side</Term> meets the unit circle at $(\cos x,\sin x)$, so $\sin x$ is that point's $y$-coordinate. As $x$ grows from $0$ to $2\pi$, the point goes once around the unit circle, and its $y$-coordinate changes like this:

| $x$ | $0$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$ | $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ | $2\pi$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin x$ | $0$ | $1$ (maximum) | $0$ | $-1$ (minimum) | $0$ |

Join these points with a smooth wave and you have one stretch of the graph of the <Term t="math.sine-function">sine function</Term>: up from $0$ to $1$, down through $0$ to $-1$, and back to $0$.

- **Domain** <Zh>定義域</Zh>: all real numbers (every angle has a terminal side).
- **Range** <Zh>值域</Zh>: $-1\le \sin x\le 1$ (a point on the unit circle never has a $y$-coordinate beyond the radius $1$).
- **Period**: $2\pi$. When $x$ grows by $2\pi$ the terminal side is back where it started, so $\sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x$.

In general, if some positive $p$ makes $f(x+p)=f(x)$ for every $x$ in the domain, the smallest such $p$ is the <Term t="math.period">period</Term> of $f$. The graph repeats once every period, so once you have drawn the piece for $0\le x\le 2\pi$, copying it again and again to the left and right gives the whole graph of $y=\sin x$.

The figure below draws exactly this. On the left is the unit circle, on the right $y=\sin x$. Drag $x$: the point $P$ on the circle turns through $x$ radians, and the dashed horizontal line "carries" its height (its $y$-coordinate) across to the right, where it is exactly the height of the wave at $x$. Each time the point goes once around, the wave completes one period.

### Increasing and decreasing

The table shows it (the stretch from $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ to $2\pi$ rises from $-1$ to $0$, and by periodicity that is the stretch from $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ to $0$): $\sin x$ is **strictly increasing** <Zh>嚴格遞增</Zh> on $-\frac{\pi}{2}\le x\le\frac{\pi}{2}$ (rising from $-1$ to $1$) and **strictly decreasing** <Zh>嚴格遞減</Zh> on $\frac{\pi}{2}\le x\le\frac{3\pi}{2}$ (falling from $1$ to $-1$). When you compare sine values, this is the reason you cite.

**Example.** Without a calculator, put $\sin 1$, $\sin 2$ and $\sin 3$ in order (all angles in radians).

Both $2$ and $3$ are larger than $\frac{\pi}{2}\approx 1.57$, so first use $\sin x=\sin(\pi-x)$ to move them into $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$
\sin 2=\sin(\pi-2),\qquad \sin 3=\sin(\pi-3)
$$

Since $3.14<\pi<3.15$, we get $0.14<\pi-3<0.15$ and $1.14<\pi-2<1.15$. The three angles $\pi-3$, $1$, $\pi-2$ all lie between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$, in increasing order, and $\sin x$ is strictly increasing on that interval, so

$$
\sin 3<\sin 1<\sin 2
$$

<Perspective title="Use the graph's axis of symmetry: the closer to π/2, the higher">

Instead of moving the angles, look straight at the piece of the graph for $0\le x\le\pi$. Because $\sin(\pi-x)=\sin x$, the points at $x$ and $\pi-x$ have the same height, and those two points are mirror images in the line $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$. So this piece of the graph is symmetric about $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$: the left half rises from $0$ to $1$ and the right half falls back to $0$ in mirror image.

Write each $x$ in $0\le x\le \pi$ in terms of its distance from $\frac{\pi}{2}$, $d=\left|x-\frac{\pi}{2}\right|$. By the symmetry, $\sin x=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-d\right)$, and $\frac{\pi}{2}-d$ lies between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$, where $\sin$ is strictly increasing. So **the smaller $d$ is, the larger $\sin x$ is**.

Using $1.57<\frac{\pi}{2}<1.575$:

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| distance from $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}-1$, between $0.57$ and $0.575$ | $2-\frac{\pi}{2}$, between $0.425$ and $0.43$ | $3-\frac{\pi}{2}$, between $1.425$ and $1.43$ |

From smallest to largest distance the order is $2$, $1$, $3$, so $\sin 2>\sin 1>\sin 3$, the same result as above. This view is especially fast in multiple-choice questions: for angles between $0$ and $\pi$, just compare how far they are from $\frac{\pi}{2}$.

## Amplitude, period and frequency

Using the stretches and shifts of graphs from grade 10 (for example, the graph of $y=f(x-h)$ is the graph of $y=f(x)$ moved $h$ to the right), you can get the graph of $y=a\sin(bx)+k$ ($a\ne 0$, $b>0$) from $y=\sin x$:

| Change | Effect on the graph | What you read off |
|---|---|---|
| multiply by $a$ | stretch vertically by $\lvert a\rvert$; flip upside down too if $a<0$ | <Term t="math.amplitude">amplitude</Term> $\lvert a\rvert$ |
| replace $x$ by $bx$ | stretch horizontally by $\frac{1}{b}$ | period $\frac{2\pi}{b}$ |
| add $k$ | move the whole graph up by $k$ | midline $y=k$ |

So the maximum is $k+|a|$, the minimum is $k-|a|$, and the graph swings above and below the midline $y=k$. The reciprocal of the period, $\frac{b}{2\pi}$, is called the **frequency** <Zh>頻率</Zh>: how many times the graph repeats each time $x$ grows by $1$ (for example, per minute when $x$ is measured in minutes).

Also, $y=a\sin\big(b(x-h)\big)+k$ is the graph of $y=a\sin(bx)+k$ moved $h$ to the right (to the left when $h<0$); the amplitude and period do not change.

<StudentAsks q="Why is the period 2π divided by b, not 2π times b?">

The period is "how far $x$ has to go before the graph repeats". For $\sin(bx)$ to repeat, the inside $bx$ has to cover $2\pi$, so $x$ goes from $0$ to $\frac{2\pi}{b}$. For example, with $\sin 2x$, by the time $x$ reaches $\pi$, $2x$ has already covered $2\pi$, so the period is $\pi$: the graph is squeezed sideways to half its width. The bigger $b$ is, the tighter the waves and the shorter the period.

</StudentAsks>

### Reading amplitude and period from a graph

Given a stretch of a sine curve, the highest and lowest points tell you everything:

- amplitude $=\dfrac{\text{maximum}-\text{minimum}}{2}$, and midline $k=\dfrac{\text{maximum}+\text{minimum}}{2}$;
- two neighbouring highest points are one period apart; a highest point and the next lowest point are **half** a period apart.

**Example.** The graph of $y=a\sin(bx)+k$ ($a>0$, $b>0$) has a highest point $\left(\frac{\pi}{4},5\right)$, and the next lowest point after it is $\left(\frac{3\pi}{4},-1\right)$. Find $a$, $b$ and $k$.

The amplitude is $a=\frac{5-(-1)}{2}=3$ and the midline is $k=\frac{5+(-1)}{2}=2$. From the highest point to the next lowest point is $\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}$, which is half a period, so the period is $\pi$ and $b=\frac{2\pi}{\pi}=2$.

Check: $3\sin\left(2\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+2=3\cdot 1+2=5$ and $3\sin\left(2\cdot\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)+2=3\cdot(-1)+2=-1$.

The teal segment on the midline runs from x=π/4 to x=3π/4, length π/2: half a period, so one period is π." x={[-0.4, 3.6]} y={[-2, 6.3]} height={300}

<Perspective title="See b as a rate: how fast the angle moves">

You can also read $b$ as "how much the angle $bx$ inside the brackets grows each time $x$ grows by $1$". At a highest point the angle inside $\sin$ is $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (plus a whole multiple of $2\pi$); at the next lowest point it is $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ (plus the same multiple). So from the highest point to the next lowest point the angle inside moves $\frac{3\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}=\pi$, while $x$ moves only $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$
b=\frac{\text{change in the angle}}{\text{change in }x}=\frac{\pi}{\ \frac{\pi}{2}\ }=2
$$

You can also get $a$ and $k$ by setting up equations: with $a>0$ the maximum is $k+a$ and the minimum is $k-a$, so $k+a=5$ and $k-a=-1$. Adding gives $2k=4$, $k=2$; subtracting gives $2a=6$, $a=3$. This is where "the average is the midline, half the difference is the amplitude" comes from.

Finally, check that no shift is needed: at the highest point $x=\frac{\pi}{4}$ we have $2x=\frac{\pi}{2}$, exactly the angle where $\sin$ is largest, so $y=3\sin 2x+2$ itself passes through both points.

</Perspective>

## Describing periodic change with the sine function

Changes that repeat, such as the height of a Ferris wheel car or the rise and fall of temperature over a day, are often modelled with $y=a\sin\big(b(x-h)\big)+k$. Building the model is the last section in reverse: find $|a|$ and $k$ from the highest and lowest values, find $b$ from the time one cycle takes, and choose the shift from where the motion starts.

**Example.** A Ferris wheel car is at most $45$ m and at least $5$ m above the ground, and one turn takes $12$ minutes. The car starts at the lowest point; let $h(t)$ be its height in metres after $t$ minutes.

(1) Write down $h(t)$.

The amplitude is $\frac{45-5}{2}=20$, the midline is $\frac{45+5}{2}=25$, and the period is $12$, so $b=\frac{2\pi}{12}=\frac{\pi}{6}$. Starting from the lowest point, the car takes $\frac{1}{4}$ of a period ($3$ minutes) to rise to the midline, while $y=20\sin\frac{\pi t}{6}+25$ is already on the midline and rising at $t=0$. Our car is $3$ minutes behind, so move that graph $3$ to the right:

$$
h(t)=20\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t-3)\right)+25
$$

Check: $h(0)=20\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)+25=5$ and $h(6)=20\sin\frac{\pi}{2}+25=45$.

(2) During the first turn ($0\le t\le 12$), for how many minutes in total is the car more than $35$ m above the ground?

Let $\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}(t-3)$. Then $h(t)>35 \iff 20\sin\theta>10 \iff \sin\theta>\frac{1}{2}$. For $0\le t\le 12$ we have $-\frac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\frac{3\pi}{2}$, and the part with $\sin\theta>\frac{1}{2}$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}<\theta<\frac{5\pi}{6}$. That means $1<t-3<5$, so $4<t<8$: $4$ minutes in all.

Draw $h(t)$ and this question becomes "over how long a time is the curve above the horizontal line $y=35$?"

<Perspective title="Look at the wheel itself: a circle and a chord">

Forget the sine curve and look at the circle. The centre of the wheel is $\frac{45+5}{2}=25$ m above the ground and its radius is $\frac{45-5}{2}=20$ m. It turns $2\pi$ in $12$ minutes, so after $t$ minutes it has turned through $\varphi=\frac{\pi t}{6}$.

**(1) Another formula.** Measure the angle with "from the centre straight down" as the initial side. After turning $\varphi$, the radius from the centre to the car (length $20$) has a component $20\cos\varphi$ in the downward direction, so the car is $20\cos\varphi$ below the centre (when $\varphi>\frac{\pi}{2}$, $\cos\varphi<0$, which means the car is actually above the centre). Therefore

$$
h(t)=25-20\cos\frac{\pi t}{6}
$$

Check by substituting: at $t=0,3,6,9,12$ it gives $5,25,45,25,5$, exactly the same as $20\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t-3)\right)+25$. (For a proof that the two formulas agree everywhere, see the going-further box below.)

**(2) No inequality, just a chord.** Being higher than $35$ m means the car is above the horizontal chord $y=35$. That chord is $35-25=10$ above the centre, exactly half the radius $20$. From the centre to the right end of the chord, the vertical rise is $10$ and the radius is $20$, so the angle $\alpha$ between this radius and "straight up" has $\cos\alpha=\frac{10}{20}=\frac{1}{2}$, $\alpha=\frac{\pi}{3}$. The arc above the chord spans $2\alpha=\frac{2\pi}{3}$, which is $\frac{1}{3}$ of a full turn:

$$
12\times\frac{1}{3}=4\text{ minutes}
$$

You can see the start time too: from the lowest point to the right end of the chord the car turns $\pi-\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{2\pi}{3}$, which takes $t=\frac{2\pi/3}{\pi/6}=4$; another $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ brings it to the left end at $t=8$. The same as the algebraic answer $4<t<8$.

<Perspective later title="Prove the two formulas agree everywhere with the angle-difference formula" needs="the sine angle-sum and difference formulas (Math A)" href="/en/math/s116a-sum-difference/">

The Math A difference formula is $\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta$. Take $\alpha=\frac{\pi t}{6}$ and $\beta=\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$
\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t-3)\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{6}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\sin\frac{\pi t}{6}\cdot 0-\cos\frac{\pi t}{6}\cdot 1=-\cos\frac{\pi t}{6}
$$

So $20\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}(t-3)\right)+25=25-20\cos\frac{\pi t}{6}$ for every $t$. In the same way, $\sin\left(x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\sin x\cdot 0+\cos x\cdot 1=\cos x$, which is why, in the next section, $y=\cos x$ is $y=\sin x$ moved $\frac{\pi}{2}$ to the left.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="When solving sin θ > 1/2, why do I have to write down the range of θ first?">

Because $\sin\theta>\frac{1}{2}$ has infinitely many stretches of solutions, one every $2\pi$. The problem only asks about the first turn, so you must turn the range of $t$ into a range of $\theta$ to know which stretches count. Get the range wrong and you often count one stretch too many or too few.

</StudentAsks>

## Math A: the graphs of cosine and tangent

The Math A curriculum includes the graphs of the $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$ functions; the Math B curriculum lists only the graph of the sine function and states that it does not include the graphs of $\cos$ and $\tan$.

- $y=\cos x$: $\cos x$ is the $x$-coordinate of the point on the unit circle. The domain is all real numbers, the range is $-1\le\cos x\le 1$, and the period is $2\pi$. Because $\cos x=\sin\left(x+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, its graph is $y=\sin x$ moved $\frac{\pi}{2}$ to the left: exactly the same shape, just starting from the highest point $(0,1)$.
- $y=\tan x$: $\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ is undefined where $\cos x=0$, so the domain is $x\ne\frac{\pi}{2}+n\pi$ ($n$ an integer). The range is all real numbers. On $-\frac{\pi}{2}<x<\frac{\pi}{2}$ the graph is strictly increasing from bottom to top, shooting up and down without bound as $x$ approaches $\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$; these two vertical lines are called asymptotes <Zh>漸近線</Zh>. Its period is $\pi$, not $2\pi$: $\tan(x+\pi)=\tan x$.

<Perspective title="Think of slope: why tan x repeats every π">

The point $P(\cos x,\sin x)$ on the unit circle and the origin make the terminal side. $\tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ is exactly the **slope** of the line $OP$.

Turn the angle by another $\pi$ and the point moves to the opposite side, $P'(-\cos x,-\sin x)$. Both $\sin$ and $\cos$ change sign, so they need a full $2\pi$ to repeat; but $P'$ is still on the same line through the origin, and the slope $\frac{-\sin x}{-\cos x}=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ does not change, so $\tan$ repeats after only $\pi$.

You can see the asymptotes too: as $x$ approaches $\frac{\pi}{2}$ the line $OP$ gets steeper and steeper and its slope grows without bound; at $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$ the line is vertical and has no slope, so $\tan\frac{\pi}{2}$ is undefined.

So the period of $y=a\cos(bx)+k$ is also $\frac{2\pi}{b}$, while the period of $y=\tan(bx)$ is $\frac{\pi}{b}$ ($b>0$). The curriculum marks the definitions and graphs of $\cot$, $\sec$ and $\csc$ with ※ (recommended not to be tested), so we do not review them here.
