## Subjective and objective probability

A probability value can come from different places:

- **Objective probability** <Zh>客觀機率</Zh>: obtained from known data. For example, you observe something many times and use the **relative frequency** <Zh>相對次數</Zh> of an event (the number of times it happened divided by the total number of observations) as its probability. Anyone working from the same data gets the same value.
- **Subjective probability** <Zh>主觀機率</Zh>: how confident one person is that something will happen, based on their own experience, information and judgement. Different people can give different values. You may feel your chance of passing is $0.8$, while a friend thinks it is only $0.6$.

Subjective probabilities differ from person to person, but they still have to obey the properties of probability; otherwise they are not reasonable. The points to check are:

- every event's probability is between $0$ and $1$;
- if some events are pairwise mutually exclusive and together cover every possible case, their probabilities add up to $1$;
- $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$;
- if $B$ always happens whenever $A$ does ($A\subset B$), then $P(A)\le P(B)$. In particular, $P(A\cap B)$ is never larger than $P(A)$ or than $P(B)$.

**Example.** Teams X, Y and Z play for a championship, and exactly one of them wins. Hua estimates their chances of winning as $0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.3$. Mei estimates that X wins with probability $0.4$, and that "X or Y wins" has probability $0.35$. Are their estimates reasonable?

Hua: the three events "X wins", "Y wins" and "Z wins" are pairwise mutually exclusive and together cover every case, so their probabilities should add up to $1$. But $0.5+0.3+0.3=1.1\ne 1$, so the estimate is not reasonable. Mei: whenever X wins, "X or Y wins" also happens, so its probability must be at least $0.4$. Since $0.35<0.4$, her estimate is not reasonable either.

**Example.** A crossroads records the morning traffic on $200$ school days: flowing freely on $112$ days, busy on $42$ days and jammed on $46$ days. From these data, what is the objective probability that the traffic is jammed on a school-day morning? That it is not jammed?

Use relative frequency: the objective probability of a jam is $\frac{46}{200}=0.23$, and of no jam is $1-0.23=0.77$ (that is, $\frac{112+42}{200}$).

<StudentAsks q="If a subjective probability is just one person's judgement, how can it ever be &quot;unreasonable&quot;?">

A single value really can differ from person to person: you say $0.8$, someone else says $0.6$, and both are allowed. But when one person gives several probabilities at once, those values have to fit together under the properties of probability, or they contradict each other. On Hua's estimate, "one of the three teams wins", which is certain to happen, would have probability $1.1$, more than $1$. That makes no sense. The curriculum puts it exactly this way: check whether a subjective probability is reasonable using the properties of probability <Zh>根據機率性質檢視主觀機率的合理性</Zh>.

</StudentAsks>

## Conditional probability: shrinking the sample space

Suppose $P(A)>0$. The <Term t="math.conditional-probability">conditional probability</Term> that $B$ happens given that $A$ has happened is written $P(B\mid A)$ and defined by

$$
P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}
$$

If every outcome in the sample space is equally likely, this is $\frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(A)}$: once you know $A$ has happened, the <Term t="math.sample-space">sample space</Term> shrinks from $S$ to $A$, and you look at how many outcomes in $A$ also belong to $B$. In the opening question, $A=\{\text{HH},\text{HT},\text{TH}\}$ and $A\cap B=\{\text{HH}\}$, so $P(B\mid A)=\frac{1}{3}$.

Arrange the four outcomes in a grid and you can see the sample space shrink:

<Graph desc="A 2 by 2 grid shows the 4 equally likely outcomes of tossing two coins: the left column is first coin heads, the right column first coin tails; the top row is second coin heads, the bottom row second coin tails. HH at the top left is shaded blue: it is A∩B. HT at the bottom left and TH at the top right are shaded amber. TT at the bottom right is crossed out, because it is removed once you know there is at least one head. The three cells left are the shrunken sample space A, and only one of them has both heads, so the probability is 1/3." x={[-0.2, 2.2]} y={[-0.2, 2.2]}

<Perspective title="Thinking in counts: why the gut answer is wrong">

Toss the two coins $400$ times. Each of the four outcomes turns up about $100$ times. "Given at least one head" throws away the roughly $100$ TTs and leaves about $300$ tosses; about $100$ of them are both heads, so the proportion is $\frac{100}{300}=\frac{1}{3}$. The definition gives the same: $P(A)=\frac{3}{4}$ and $P(A\cap B)=\frac{1}{4}$, and dividing gives $\frac{1}{3}$.

The gut answer $\frac{1}{2}$ actually answers a different question: "given that the **first** coin is heads". That keeps only the left column of the grid (HH, HT), one cell out of two, and then it really is $\frac{1}{2}$. "At least one head" keeps the L-shape of three cells, and you don't know which coin showed the head, so you can't say "the other coin" is heads or tails half the time. The two conditions sound alike, but they shrink the sample space to different sets, so the answers differ.

</Perspective>

Multiply the denominator of the definition across to get

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)
$$

In words, "both $A$ and $B$ happen" can be seen in two steps: first $A$ happens, then $B$ happens given that $A$ already has. When you draw balls one after another without putting them back, this is how you multiply, step by step.

### Reading conditional probabilities from a contingency table

When the same data are counted under two ways of classifying them at once, the table you get is a <Term t="math.contingency-table">contingency table</Term>.

**Example.** A school asks $200$ students whether they wear glasses, with the results below. One student is chosen at random. (1) Given that the student is a girl, what is the probability that she wears glasses? (2) Given that the student wears glasses, what is the probability that the student is a girl?

|  | Glasses | No glasses | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boys | $72$ | $48$ | $120$ |
| Girls | $40$ | $40$ | $80$ |
| Total | $112$ | $88$ | $200$ |

(1) Given a girl, look only at the girls' row: $\frac{40}{80}=\frac{1}{2}$.

(2) Given glasses, look only at the glasses column: $\frac{40}{112}=\frac{5}{14}$.

A contingency table and a <Term t="math.venn-diagram">Venn diagram</Term> say the same thing. Let $A$ be "girl" and $B$ "wears glasses". The four inner cells of the table are exactly the four regions the two circles cut a Venn diagram into: $A\cap B$ has $40$ students, $A\cap B'$ has $40$, $A'\cap B$ has $72$ and $A'\cap B'$ has $48$. The row and column totals are $n(A)$, $n(B)$ and the sizes of their <Term t="math.complementary-set">complements</Term>.

<StudentAsks q="Aren't P(B|A) and P(A|B) the same thing?">

No. What comes after the bar is the condition you are given, and it decides the denominator. In the example above, $P(\text{glasses}\mid\text{girl})=\frac{40}{80}=\frac{1}{2}$, with the number of girls as the denominator, while $P(\text{girl}\mid\text{glasses})=\frac{40}{112}=\frac{5}{14}$, with the number of glasses-wearers as the denominator. Both have numerator $n(A\cap B)=40$; only the denominators differ. When you read a question, first find which event is the one you are "given".

</StudentAsks>

- Given a girl: look only at the girls' column. The proportion wearing glasses is the **height** of its shading, $0.5$, that is $\frac{1}{2}$.

## Independent events

If

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)
$$

then $A$ and $B$ are <Term t="math.independent-events">independent events</Term>. When $P(A)>0$, this is the same as $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$: knowing that $A$ happened does not change the probability of $B$.

**Example.** Roll a fair die once. Let $A$ be "the number is even", $B$ "the number is at most $2$" and $C$ "the number is at most $3$". Are $A$ and $B$ independent? What about $A$ and $C$?

$A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,2\}$ and $C=\{1,2,3\}$.

- $P(A\cap B)=P(\{2\})=\frac{1}{6}$ and $P(A)P(B)=\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{6}$. They are equal, so $A$ and $B$ are independent. Seen another way: given a number at most $2$, the probability of an even number is $P(A\mid B)=\frac{1}{2}$, the same as $P(A)$.
- $P(A\cap C)=P(\{2\})=\frac{1}{6}$ but $P(A)P(C)=\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}$. They are not equal, so $A$ and $C$ are not independent. Given a number at most $3$, the probability of an even number becomes $P(A\mid C)=\frac{1}{3}$.

<Graph desc="The numbers 1 to 6 on a die are marked on the horizontal axis; the even numbers 2, 4 and 6 are blue dots and the odd numbers 1, 3 and 5 are black dots. An amber box around 1 and 2 is event B; a larger red box around 1, 2 and 3 is event C. Of all 6 dots, 3 are blue, a proportion of 1/2. Inside box B, 1 of the 2 dots is blue, also 1/2, so A and B are independent. Inside box C, only 1 of the 3 dots is blue, 1/3, which is not 1/2, so A and C are not independent." x={[0.2, 6.8]} y={[-1.2, 1.6]}

  labels={[{ at: [2.0, 0.8], text: 'B', color: 'amber' }, { at: [3.05, 1.2], text: 'C', color: 'red' }]} />

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then so are $A$ and $B'$, because

$$
P(A\cap B')=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=P(A)-P(A)P(B)=P(A)\,P(B')
$$

In the same way, $A'$ and $B$, and $A'$ and $B'$, are independent too.

- The shaded area of the $A$ column is $P(A)\,P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)$: that is the multiplication formula. The shaded areas of the two columns add up to $P(B)$, the height of the red dashed line.
- Make $p$ and $q$ equal: the columns are equally tall, the height is $P(B)$, so $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$ and the shaded area of the $A$ column is $P(A)\,P(B)$, which is exactly the definition of independence.
- When the columns are equally tall, the unshaded parts on top ($B'$) are equally tall too, both $1-P(B)$. So $A$ and $B'$ are independent as well, the same result the formula above gave.
- Drag $p$ down to $0$: there is no $B$ inside $A$ at all, so $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive. Then as long as $q>0$, the columns can never be equally tall, so two mutually exclusive events whose probabilities are not $0$ are never independent.

### Using independence: trials that don't affect each other

When two things don't affect each other, treat them as independent events and simply multiply their probabilities.

**Example.** Two players, A and B, each take one basketball shot. They score with probabilities $0.7$ and $0.6$, and neither shot affects the other. What is the probability that at least one of them scores?

Both miss with probability $0.3\times 0.4=0.12$ ($A'$ and $B'$ are independent too), so at least one scores with probability $1-0.12=0.88$.

<Perspective title="Seeing the two shots as areas in a square">

The two shots don't affect each other, so you can draw the probabilities as a square of side $10$ in which each small cell is $0.01$. Across is A: scoring takes $0.7$ (the left $7$ columns) and missing $0.3$. Up is B: scoring takes $0.6$ (the bottom $6$ rows) and missing $0.4$. Because the shots are independent, B's scoring rate is the same $0.6$ in both A's "score" columns and A's "miss" columns, so the two dividing lines cut the square into four rectangles, and the area of each is the product of two probabilities.

<StudentAsks q="Aren't independent and mutually exclusive basically the same? Both mean the two events have &quot;nothing to do with each other&quot;?">

Quite the opposite. Mutually exclusive means $A\cap B=\varnothing$: the two events can never happen together, so $P(A\cap B)=0$. If $P(A)>0$ and $P(B)>0$ and they are mutually exclusive, then $P(A\cap B)=0\ne P(A)P(B)$, so they are **not** independent: once you know $A$ happened, you know for sure that $B$ didn't, and the probability of $B$ drops from $P(B)$ to $0$. For a die, "even" and "odd" are mutually exclusive but not independent at all. Mutually exclusive is about whether the sets overlap; independent is about whether the news changes the probability.

</StudentAsks>

## Asking it backwards: basic uses of Bayes' theorem

Math B's learning content puts the basic applications of <Term t="math.bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</Term> in this unit. Math A lists Bayes' theorem as a separate item of learning content (D-11A-3), and the Math A path reviews it in full later, in the chapter "Bayes' theorem and applications of matrices". Here you only see its most basic form.

If $A_1$ and $A_2$ are mutually exclusive and together make up the whole sample space (for example "made on machine X" and "made on machine Y"), then for any event $B$ (the second formula needs $P(B)>0$):

$$
P(B)=P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1)+P(A_2)P(B\mid A_2)
$$

$$
P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1)}{P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1)+P(A_2)P(B\mid A_2)}
$$

The first formula splits $B$ into two pieces by where it came from and adds them. The second is just the definition of conditional probability: the numerator is $P(A_1\cap B)$ and the denominator is $P(B)$.

**Example.** In a factory, $60\%$ of the products are made on machine X and $40\%$ on machine Y. Machine X has a defect rate of $2\%$ and machine Y of $5\%$. A product picked at random turns out to be defective. What is the probability that it came from machine Y?

First draw a tree diagram: the first level splits by which machine made the product, the second by defective or good, and the probability of each path is the product along it.

$$
P(\text{defective})=0.6\times 0.02+0.4\times 0.05=0.012+0.020=0.032
$$

$$
P(\text{Y}\mid\text{defective})=\frac{0.020}{0.032}=\frac{5}{8}
$$

You can also imagine $1000$ products and count them in a contingency table:

|  | Defective | Good | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Machine X | $12$ | $588$ | $600$ |
| Machine Y | $20$ | $380$ | $400$ |
| Total | $32$ | $968$ | $1000$ |

There are $32$ defective products, $20$ of them from machine Y, and $\frac{20}{32}=\frac{5}{8}$. Machine Y makes only four tenths of the products, yet five eighths of the defective ones come from it, because its defect rate is higher.

<Perspective title="Seeing it as area: each small cell is one defective product">

Draw the defective products from the contingency table as areas. Across, cut by machine, with width $10\times P(\text{machine})$: machine X is $6$ wide and machine Y $4$ wide. Up is the defect rate in per cent: X's column is shaded up to $2$ and Y's up to $5$. Each small cell is $0.1$ wide and $1\%$ tall, so it stands for probability $0.1\times 0.01=0.001$, one product in $1000$. X's defective products are $6\times 2=12$ cells and Y's are $4\times 5=20$ cells, the same $12$ and $20$ as in the table.

Given that the product is defective, look only at the shaded part:

$$
P(\text{Y}\mid\text{defective})=\frac{\text{red area}}{\text{blue area}+\text{red area}}=\frac{4\times 5}{6\times 2+4\times 5}=\frac{20}{32}=\frac{5}{8}
$$

This is exactly the fraction in Bayes' theorem: the two terms of the denominator are the two areas, each "width $P(A_i)$ times height $P(B\mid A_i)$". Try dragging $r_1$ up to $5$ as well, so the two columns are equally tall. Then being defective has nothing to do with which machine made the product, that is, the events are independent, and $P(\text{Y}\mid\text{defective})=\frac{4\times 5}{6\times 5+4\times 5}=0.4$, back to the original $P(\text{Y})$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Getting P(defective) and P(good) in one go with matrix multiplication" needs="matrix multiplication" href="/en/math/s116a-matrices/">

Write the two machines' shares of production as a row, $\begin{bmatrix}0.6 & 0.4\end{bmatrix}$, and each machine's probabilities of "defective, good" as a $2\times 2$ matrix, one row per machine:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0.6 & 0.4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0.02 & 0.98\\ 0.05 & 0.95\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6\times 0.02+0.4\times 0.05 & 0.6\times 0.98+0.4\times 0.95\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.032 & 0.968\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first entry is $P(\text{defective})$ and the second is $P(\text{good})$. Matrix multiplication's "a row times a column, then add" is exactly the tree diagram's "multiply along each path, then add up the paths with the same result". Later on the Math A path, the chapter "Bayes' theorem and applications of matrices" uses matrices for transition matrices, but there the states are written as a column and multiplied on the right of the matrix, so the matrix there is the transpose of this one (each column adds up to $1$, whereas here each row does); when you read a question, check first how it is laid out. This is only another way of writing it; this chapter's method does not need it.

</Perspective>
