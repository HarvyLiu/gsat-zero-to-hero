## Slope: how tilted a line is

Take any two points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ on a non-vertical line. Its <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> is

$$
m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}
$$

that is, how much $y$ goes up when $x$ goes up by $1$. On one line, every pair of points gives the same slope.

- $m>0$: the line rises to the right; $m<0$: it falls to the right; $m=0$: it is horizontal.
- The larger the **absolute value of the slope** $|m|$, the steeper the line. For example, $y=-3x$ is steeper than $y=\frac{1}{2}x$, because $|-3|>\left|\frac{1}{2}\right|$.
- Two points on a vertical line have the same $x$-coordinate, so the denominator is $0$: a vertical line **has no slope**.

<Perspective title="Similar triangles: why any two points give the same slope">

On the line $y=2x$ take two pairs of points: $(1,2)$, $(4,8)$ and $(-2,-4)$, $(-1,-2)$. For each pair draw the right triangle "across, then up":

- the big triangle: across $3$, up $6$;
- the small triangle: across $1$, up $2$.

Both hypotenuses lie on the same line, so they make the same angle with the horizontal, and the two right triangles are **similar**: the ratio of their legs must agree, $\frac{6}{3}=\frac{2}{1}=2$. That is the geometric reason every pair of points on one line gives the same slope. Conversely, if $AB$ and $BC$ have the same slope, the three points $A$, $B$, $C$ are collinear, which is the quickest way to check collinearity.

<Perspective later title="The slope is the tangent of the angle of inclination" needs="trigonometric ratios (tangent) and angles of any size" href="/en/math/s116-trig-ratios/">

The angle $\theta$ ($0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ$) a line makes with the positive $x$-axis is its angle of inclination. In the slope triangle "right $1$, up $m$", $m$ is the opposite side and $1$ the adjacent side, so

$$
m=\tan\theta
$$

For example, $y=x$ is inclined at $45^\circ$ and $\tan 45^\circ=1$; $y=-x$ is inclined at $135^\circ$ and $\tan 135^\circ=-1$. The closer $\theta$ is to $90^\circ$, the larger $|\tan\theta|$ and the steeper the line; at $\theta=90^\circ$, $\tan\theta$ is undefined, which matches "a vertical line has no slope".

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Is the slope of a vertical line infinity?">

Don't write that. The definition divides by $x_2-x_1$, which is always $0$ on a vertical line, and you can't divide by $0$. So we say a vertical line has no slope. Write its equation directly as $x=k$; for example, the vertical line through $(3,5)$ is $x=3$.

</StudentAsks>

## Writing a line's equation from given conditions

The line through $(x_0,y_0)$ with slope $m$ has the <Term t="math.point-slope-form">point slope form</Term>

$$
y-y_0=m(x-x_0)
$$

The other common forms all come from it:

| Given | Equation |
|---|---|
| slope $m$, through $(x_0,y_0)$ | $y-y_0=m(x-x_0)$ |
| slope $m$, $y$-intercept $k$ | $y=mx+k$ |
| two points | find the slope, then use the point slope form |
| general form | $ax+by+c=0$; if $b\ne 0$ the slope is $-\frac{a}{b}$, if $b=0$ the line is vertical |

**Example.** Find the equation of the line through $(-1,5)$ and $(2,-1)$.

The slope is $m=\frac{-1-5}{2-(-1)}=\frac{-6}{3}=-2$. By the point slope form:

$$
y-5=-2(x+1)\iff y=-2x+3\iff 2x+y-3=0
$$

Check with the other point $(2,-1)$: $2\cdot 2+(-1)-3=0$. Correct.

  points={[{ at: [-1, 5], label: '(−1, 5)' }, { at: [2, -1], label: '(2, −1)' }, { at: [0, 3], label: '(0, 3)', color: 'blue' }]}
  labels={[{ at: [0.2, 5.2], text: 'right 3', color: 'amber' }, { at: [2.1, 2], text: 'down 6', color: 'red' }]} />

<Perspective title="Undetermined coefficients: solve for m and k directly">

You don't have to find the slope first. Let the line be $y=mx+k$; both points must satisfy it:

$$
\begin{cases}5=-m+k\\-1=2m+k\end{cases}
$$

Subtract the first equation from the second: $-6=3m$, so $m=-2$; then $5=2+k$ gives $k=3$. So $y=-2x+3$, the same answer as the point slope form.

This turns "find the line" into "solve simultaneous equations": two points give two conditions, exactly enough for the two unknowns $m$ and $k$. Later, finding the equation of a circle or a parabola works the same way: as many conditions as unknown coefficients.

</Perspective>

## Translation: moving points and lines

Moving the point $(x_0,y_0)$ right by $h$ and up by $k$ (a negative $h$ or $k$ means left or down) takes it to $(x_0+h,\,y_0+k)$.

To translate a line, **replace $x$ by $x-h$ and $y$ by $y-k$** in its equation. A translation doesn't turn the line, so the slope stays the same.

**Example.** Move the line $y=2x+1$ right by $3$ and up by $1$.

Replace $x$ by $x-3$ and $y$ by $y-1$:

$$
y-1=2(x-3)+1\iff y=2x-4
$$

Check: the point $(0,1)$ on the original line moves to $(3,2)$, and $2\cdot 3-4=2$, so it does lie on the new line.

Drag h and k: the two lines always stay parallel (both have slope 2); with the defaults h=3, k=1 the new line is y=2x−4, through (3, 2)." x={[-5, 6]} y={[-6, 8]}

  functions={[{ expr: '2x+1', dashed: true }, { expr: '2*(x-h)+1+k' }]}

<Perspective title="Move just one point, then use the point slope form">

A translation keeps the slope, so the new line still has slope $2$. All you need is one point on it: the point $(0,1)$ of the original line moves to $(0+3,\,1+1)=(3,2)$. By the point slope form:

$$
y-2=2(x-3)\iff y=2x-4
$$

This also explains the minus sign in "replace $x$ by $x-h$": a point $(X,Y)$ on the new line came from the point $(X-h,\,Y-k)$ on the original line, so $(X-h,\,Y-k)$ must satisfy the original equation. Moving right means you step back left before substituting.

</Perspective>

## Parallel, perpendicular, and how two lines meet

Let two non-vertical lines have slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$:

- **Parallel**: $m_1=m_2$, and they are not the same line.
- **Perpendicular**: $m_1m_2=-1$.

A vertical line has no slope, so treat it separately: two different vertical lines are parallel, and a horizontal line and a vertical line are perpendicular.

The general form makes this quicker: a line parallel to $ax+by+c=0$ can be written $ax+by+k=0$, and a line perpendicular to it can be written $bx-ay+k=0$; then the given point fixes $k$.

**Example.** Find the line through $(3,1)$ perpendicular to $2x-3y+6=0$.

$2x-3y+6=0$ has slope $\frac{2}{3}$, so the perpendicular has slope $-\frac{3}{2}$. Swap the $x$ and $y$ coefficients and change one sign: the line is $3x+2y+k=0$ for some $k$. Substituting $(3,1)$: $9+2+k=0$, so $k=-11$ and the line is $3x+2y-11=0$. Its slope $-\frac{3}{2}$ times $\frac{2}{3}$ is exactly $-1$.

<Perspective title="Turn the slope triangle by 90°: why the product is −1">

On the line $y=mx$, start at the origin $O$ and go "right $1$, up $m$" to $A(1,m)$. Turn this slope triangle $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about $O$: "right" becomes "up" and "up" becomes "left", so you arrive at $B(-m,1)$. The slope of $OB$ is

$$
\frac{1}{-m}=-\frac{1}{m},\qquad m\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{m}\right)=-1
$$

If you'd rather not trust the turned picture, check $\angle AOB=90^\circ$ with Pythagoras:

$$
\overline{OA}^2+\overline{OB}^2=(1+m^2)+(m^2+1)=2+2m^2,\qquad \overline{AB}^2=(1+m)^2+(m-1)^2=2+2m^2
$$

The two sides agree, so by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem $\angle AOB$ is a right angle. Drag $m$ in the figure below: the two slope triangles stay congruent and the lines stay perpendicular.

The figure's horizontal and vertical scales may stretch a little with the screen width." x={[-4, 4]} y={[-4, 4]}

Where two lines meet is the solution of the simultaneous equations, so there are only three possibilities:

| Two lines | Slopes and equations | Common points |
|---|---|---|
| intersecting | different slopes | exactly one |
| parallel | same slope, not the same line | none |
| coincident | one equation is a multiple of the other | infinitely many |

For example, $2x+y-3=0$ and $x-y=0$ have different slopes; adding them gives $3x-3=0$, so they meet at $(1,1)$.

<Graph desc="The line 2x+y−3=0 (that is, y=−2x+3) is fixed; a second line y=mx+b is set by the sliders. The defaults m=1, b=0 give x−y=0, and the lines meet at (1, 1). Drag m to −2: the slopes are equal, so for b≠3 the lines are parallel with no common point, and for b=3 they coincide with infinitely many common points. For any other m the lines meet in exactly one point." x={[-4, 5]} y={[-5, 7]}
  params={[{ name: 'm', min: -3, max: 3, step: 0.5, value: 1 }, { name: 'b', min: -4, max: 4, step: 0.5, value: 0 }]}
  functions={[{ expr: '-2x+3' }, { expr: 'm*x+b' }]} />

<Perspective title="What elimination does in the picture">

Adding $2x+y-3=0$ and $x-y=0$ gives $3x-3=0$, that is, $x=1$. This step actually **swaps in a new line**: the intersection point satisfies both equations, so it satisfies their sum too, and the vertical line $x=1$ also passes through it. In the same way, the first equation minus $2$ times the second gives $3y-3=0$, so the horizontal line $y=1$ passes through the intersection as well.

Elimination replaces two slanted lines by a vertical and a horizontal line through the same point, and then you can read off the intersection $(1,1)$. If the lines are parallel, elimination ends in an impossible statement such as $0=5$: no common point. If they coincide, it ends in $0=0$.

## Distance: from a point to a line, and between parallel lines

The distance from the point $P(x_0,y_0)$ to the line $L:ax+by+c=0$ is

$$
d(P,L)=\frac{|ax_0+by_0+c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}
$$

The distance between the parallel lines $ax+by+c_1=0$ and $ax+by+c_2=0$ is

$$
\frac{|c_1-c_2|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}
$$

Before using the parallel-line formula, the two lines' $x$ and $y$ coefficients **must be made the same**.

**Example.** Find the distance from the point $(2,-1)$ to the line $3x+4y+8=0$.

$$
\frac{|3\cdot 2+4\cdot(-1)+8|}{\sqrt{3^2+4^2}}=\frac{10}{5}=2
$$

<Perspective title="Walk it once: find the foot of the perpendicular">

Skip the formula and follow the definition of distance. The line through $P(2,-1)$ perpendicular to $3x+4y+8=0$ can be written $4x-3y+k=0$; substituting $P$ gives $8+3+k=0$, so $k=-11$. Solve

$$
\begin{cases}3x+4y=-8\\4x-3y=11\end{cases}
$$

Multiply the first equation by $3$, the second by $4$, and add: $25x=20$, so $x=\frac{4}{5}$; substituting back gives $y=-\frac{13}{5}$. The foot is $H\left(\frac{4}{5},-\frac{13}{5}\right)$, so

$$
\overline{PH}=\sqrt{\left(2-\tfrac{4}{5}\right)^2+\left(-1+\tfrac{13}{5}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\tfrac{36}{25}+\tfrac{64}{25}}=2
$$

the same answer as the formula. The formula just does this whole computation in one go; when a question asks for the coordinates of the foot, this is the method to use.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Compute a right triangle's area twice">

From $P(2,-1)$ go horizontally to $L$: set $y=-1$, so $3x-4+8=0$ and $x=-\frac{4}{3}$, giving $A\left(-\frac{4}{3},-1\right)$ and $\overline{PA}=\frac{10}{3}$. Go vertically from $P$ to $L$: set $x=2$, so $6+4y+8=0$ and $y=-\frac{7}{2}$, giving $B\left(2,-\frac{7}{2}\right)$ and $\overline{PB}=\frac{5}{2}$.

$\triangle PAB$ is a right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{AB}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{10}{3}\right)^2+\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^2}=\sqrt{\frac{625}{36}}=\frac{25}{6}$, and the distance $d$ from $P$ to $L$ is the height to the hypotenuse. Compute the area twice:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{10}{3}\cdot\frac{5}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{25}{6}\cdot d\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d=\frac{25/3}{25/6}=2
$$

This uses only the junior-high fact "product of the legs = hypotenuse × height", and gives the same answer again.

<Perspective later title="With a normal vector: the distance is a projection's length" needs="the dot product and orthogonal projection of vectors" href="/en/math/s116-plane-vectors/">

The vector $\vec n=(a,b)$ is perpendicular to the line $ax+by+c=0$ (a normal vector). Take a point $A(0,-2)$ on $L:3x+4y+8=0$; then $\overrightarrow{AP}=(2,1)$. The distance from $P$ to $L$ is the length of the orthogonal projection of $\overrightarrow{AP}$ onto the direction of $\vec n=(3,4)$:

$$
d=\frac{|\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\vec n|}{|\vec n|}=\frac{|2\cdot 3+1\cdot 4|}{5}=2
$$

In general, replace $A$ by any point $(x_1,y_1)$ on $L$; since $ax_1+by_1=-c$, the numerator simplifies to $|ax_0+by_0+c|$, which is exactly the distance formula.

</Perspective>

**Example.** Find the distance between the parallel lines $3x+4y-2=0$ and $6x+8y+11=0$.

Divide the second line by $2$ to get $3x+4y+\frac{11}{2}=0$; now the coefficients match the first line. The distance is

$$
\frac{\left|-2-\frac{11}{2}\right|}{\sqrt{3^2+4^2}}=\frac{\frac{15}{2}}{5}=\frac{3}{2}
$$

<Perspective title="Pick a point on one line and use point-to-line distance">

Parallel lines are the same distance apart everywhere, so take any point on the first line $3x+4y-2=0$, say $(2,-1)$ (since $6-4-2=0$), and find its distance to the second line:

$$
\frac{|6\cdot 2+8\cdot(-1)+11|}{\sqrt{6^2+8^2}}=\frac{15}{10}=\frac{3}{2}
$$

This way you **don't have to match the coefficients first**: the point-to-line formula handles the multiple in $6x+8y+11=0$ by itself.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Where does the point-to-line distance formula come from?">

The distance means the shortest distance, measured along the perpendicular. Draw the line through $P$ perpendicular to $L$, find where it meets $L$ (the foot of the perpendicular) $H$, and work out $\overline{PH}$ with the two-point distance; simplifying gives the formula. When solving problems, just use the formula, but remember it measures the perpendicular distance, which is why the top has an absolute value: a distance is never negative.

</StudentAsks>

## Linear inequalities in two variables: a line cuts the plane in two

The line $ax+by+c=0$ splits the coordinate plane into two half-planes: on one side every point makes $ax+by+c>0$, on the other every point makes $ax+by+c<0$. So to draw the region of a linear inequality in two variables <Zh>二元一次不等式</Zh>:

1. draw the line $ax+by+c=0$ (solid if the inequality includes equality, dashed if not);
2. substitute a point that is not on the line (usually the origin): if the inequality holds, shade that side; if not, shade the other side.

**Example.** Draw the region $2x-y+4>0$.

Draw the dashed line $2x-y+4=0$. The origin gives $4>0$, which holds, so the region is **the origin's side** of the line, not including the line. You can also write it as $y<2x+4$: once you solve for $y$, "$<$" means below the line $y=2x+4$.

The origin gives 4>0, so it is in the region; the point (−4, 0) gives −4<0, so it is on the other side." x={[-5, 5]} y={[-5, 7]}

<Perspective title="Slice the plane into horizontal lines">

Cut the plane into horizontal lines. On the line at height $y=t$, the inequality becomes a linear inequality in $x$ alone:

$$
2x-t+4>0\iff x>\frac{t-4}{2}
$$

For example, at $t=0$ (the $x$-axis itself) it is $x>-2$: on a number line, the ray to the right of $-2$, not including $-2$. On every horizontal line the region is "the ray to the right of a crossing point", and the crossing point $\left(\frac{t-4}{2},\,t\right)$ lies exactly on the line $2x-y+4=0$. Stack all these rays and you get the half-plane to the right of the line, which is why one side is all $>0$ and the other all $<0$, never mixed.

## A triangle's area from coordinates

The curriculum's note for this unit mentions finding the area of $\triangle OPQ$ ($O$ the origin) from the coordinates of $P$ and $Q$. If $P(x_1,y_1)$ and $Q(x_2,y_2)$, then

$$
\text{area of } \triangle OPQ=\frac{1}{2}\,|x_1y_2-x_2y_1|
$$

The absolute value is there because swapping $P$ and $Q$ changes the sign of $x_1y_2-x_2y_1$, but not the area.

**Example.** $P(4,1)$ and $Q(2,5)$. Find the area of $\triangle OPQ$.

$$
\frac{1}{2}\,|4\cdot 5-2\cdot 1|=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 18=9
$$

You can check this with the distance formula from this chapter: the line $OP$ is $x-4y=0$ and $\overline{OP}=\sqrt{17}$; the distance from $Q$ to $OP$ is $\frac{|2-20|}{\sqrt{17}}=\frac{18}{\sqrt{17}}$. The area is $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{17}\cdot\frac{18}{\sqrt{17}}=9$, the same answer.

You can do it without the formula. Put $\triangle OPQ$ inside the rectangle $0\le x\le 4$, $0\le y\le 5$, of area $20$; what's left over is three right triangles:

- $O$, $(4,0)$, $P$: legs $4$ and $1$, area $2$;
- $P$, $(4,5)$, $Q$: legs $4$ and $2$, area $4$;
- $Q$, $(0,5)$, $O$: legs $2$ and $5$, area $5$.

So $\triangle OPQ=20-2-4-5=9$. Doing this boxing with general $x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2$ (with $P$ at the lower right and $Q$ at the upper left) gives $x_1y_2-\frac{1}{2}x_1y_1-\frac{1}{2}(x_1-x_2)(y_2-y_1)-\frac{1}{2}x_2y_2=\frac{1}{2}(x_1y_2-x_2y_1)$, which is exactly the area formula.

<Perspective later title="Seen as a 2×2 determinant" needs="plane vectors and 2×2 determinants (Math A)" href="/en/math/s116a-vector-operations/">

$x_1y_2-x_2y_1$ is the $2\times 2$ determinant $\begin{vmatrix}x_1&y_1\\x_2&y_2\end{vmatrix}$. Its absolute value is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\overrightarrow{OP}$ and $\overrightarrow{OQ}$, and the triangle is half of it. In the example, $\begin{vmatrix}4&1\\2&5\end{vmatrix}=18$: the parallelogram has area $18$ and the triangle $9$. The sign of the determinant also tells you the orientation: $18>0$ means turning from $\overrightarrow{OP}$ to $\overrightarrow{OQ}$ is counterclockwise.

</Perspective>
