## Laws of logarithms for any base

The common logarithms chapter used only base $10$. Now let the base be any positive number: for $a>0$, $a\ne 1$ and $b>0$,

$$
a^{x}=b \iff x=\log_a b
$$

that is, "$a$ to what power gives $b$". With $a=10$, $\log_{10}b$ is the <Term t="math.common-logarithm">common logarithm</Term> $\log b$. The curriculum wants you to know logarithms to other bases, but also cautions against over-practising them.

The laws of logarithms come from the <Term t="math.law-of-exponent">laws of exponents</Term>. Since $MN=a^{\log_a M}\cdot a^{\log_a N}=a^{\log_a M+\log_a N}$, we get $\log_a(MN)=\log_a M+\log_a N$: multiplying has turned into adding exponents. For $M,N>0$ and a real number $r$:

| Law of exponents | <Term t="math.law-of-logarithm">Law of logarithms</Term> |
|---|---|
| $a^{p}\cdot a^{q}=a^{p+q}$ | $\log_a(MN)=\log_a M+\log_a N$ |
| $\dfrac{a^{p}}{a^{q}}=a^{p-q}$ | $\log_a\dfrac{M}{N}=\log_a M-\log_a N$ |
| $\left(a^{p}\right)^{r}=a^{pr}$ | $\log_a M^{r}=r\log_a M$ |

### Change of base: turn any base into common logarithms

The curriculum notes that a logarithm to any base can be turned into common logarithms, and that different bases are not deliberately mixed in one expression. To do it, take common logarithms of both sides of $a^{x}=b$: $x\log a=\log b$, so

$$
\log_a b=\frac{\log b}{\log a}
$$

This is the change-of-base formula <Zh>換底公式</Zh>.

**Example.** Find $\log_2 24-\log_2 3$ and $\log_4 8$.

$\log_2 24-\log_2 3=\log_2\frac{24}{3}=\log_2 8=3$.

$\log_4 8=\dfrac{\log 8}{\log 4}=\dfrac{3\log 2}{2\log 2}=\dfrac{3}{2}$. You can also see it directly: $4^{\frac{3}{2}}=\left(\sqrt{4}\right)^{3}=8$.

<Perspective title="Count the factors of 2: switch to base 2">

$\log_2 b$ is "how many factors of $2$ are multiplied into $b$". Keep dividing $24$ by $2$: $24\to 12\to 6\to 3$. It takes $3$ halvings to get down to $3$, so $24=3\times 2^{3}$ and

$$
\log_2 24-\log_2 3=\log_2\frac{24}{3}=\log_2 2^{3}=3
$$

Mark each number $k$ at position $\log_2 k$ and you get a base-$2$ ruler: $3$ sits at $\log_2 3\approx 1.585$ and $24$ at $\log_2 24\approx 4.585$. The two marks are exactly $3$ units apart, that is, $3$ factors of $2$.

<NumberLine desc="A base-2 logarithmic ruler: each number k is marked at log₂k. The integers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 are the positions of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. The point 3 is at about 1.585 and the point 24 at about 4.585; the orange stretch from 3 to 24 has length exactly 3, showing 24 = 3 × 2³." from={0} to={5} intervals={[{ a: 1.585, b: 4.585, color: 'amber' }]} points={[{ x: 1.585, label: '3' }, { x: 3, label: '8' }, { x: 4.585, label: '24' }]} />

$\log_4 8$ can be counted the same way: $8$ is $3$ factors of $2$ and $4$ is $2$ factors of $2$. Asking "how many $4$s make $8$" is asking "how many groups of two $2$s are in three $2$s", and the answer is $\frac{3}{2}$. This is really a change of base using $2$ as the middle base: $\log_4 8=\dfrac{\log_2 8}{\log_2 4}=\dfrac{3}{2}$. The $10$ in the change-of-base formula can be replaced by any convenient base with the same result, because multiplying numerator and denominator by the same constant ($\log_2 10$) does not change the ratio.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can log(M + N) be split into log M + log N?">

No. The laws of logarithms turn **multiplication** into addition; they do not split up a sum. Try $M=N=1$: $\log(1+1)=\log 2\approx 0.3010$, but $\log 1+\log 1=0$. What $\log M+\log N$ equals is $\log(MN)$.

</StudentAsks>

## Solving exponential equations

An exponential equation has the unknown in the exponent. There are two common methods:

**Same base.** Solve $4^{x}=8^{x-1}$. Since $4=2^{2}$ and $8=2^{3}$, we get $2^{2x}=2^{3x-3}$. With the same base, the exponents must match: $2x=3x-3$, so $x=3$.

**Take logarithms of both sides.** Solve $2^{x}=5$. There is no common base, so take common logarithms of both sides: $x\log 2=\log 5$, and

$$
x=\frac{\log 5}{\log 2}\approx\frac{0.6990}{0.3010}\approx 2.32
$$

The picture lets you estimate first: $c=5$ lies between $4=2^{2}$ and $8=2^{3}$, so the solution is between $2$ and $3$, and closer to $2$. That is a check on $2.32$. </Perspective>

This also explains another line in the curriculum: any $a^{x}$ can be rewritten as $10^{kx}$. Since $a=10^{\log a}$, we have $a^{x}=10^{x\log a}$ with $k=\log a$. For example $2^{x}\approx 10^{0.3010x}$.

## Graphs of exponential and common logarithmic functions

For $a>0$ and $a\ne 1$, $y=a^{x}$ is called an <Term t="math.exponential-function">exponential function</Term>. Here are some values:

| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2^{x}$ | $\frac{1}{4}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $1$ | $2$ | $4$ |
| $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x}$ | $4$ | $2$ | $1$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $\frac{1}{4}$ |

The table shows the features of an exponential graph:

- Every graph passes through $(0,1)$, because $a^{0}=1$.
- $a^{x}$ is always positive, so the graph lies entirely above the $x$-axis; on one side it gets closer and closer to the $x$-axis without ever touching it.
- When $a>1$ (for example $2^{x}$), $y$ grows as $x$ grows: the function is increasing. When $0<a<1$ (for example $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x}$), it is decreasing.
- $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x}=2^{-x}$, so these two graphs are mirror images in the $y$-axis.

## Proportional growth and decay

When a quantity becomes the same multiple of the previous period's value every period, we call it proportional growth <Zh>按比例成長</Zh> (decay when the multiple is less than $1$). If it starts at $P_0$ and changes at rate $r$ per period, after $n$ periods it is

$$
P_n=P_0(1+r)^{n}
$$

$r>0$ is growth and $-1<r<0$ is decay. <Term t="math.compound-interest">Compound interest</Term> is the most familiar example: with principal $P_0$ and interest rate $r$ per period, the interest is added to the principal every period and earns interest itself.

To ask "at least how many periods?", take common logarithms of both sides to bring $n$ down from the exponent.

**Example (growth).** A website's membership becomes $1.5$ times the previous year's every year. After at least how many years is it more than $10$ times the original?

We need $1.5^{n}>10$. Taking $\log$ of both sides: $n\log 1.5>1$. And $\log 1.5=\log 3-\log 2\approx 0.4771-0.3010=0.1761$, so $n>\frac{1}{0.1761}\approx 5.68$: at least $6$ years. Check: $1.5^{5}\approx 7.59$ and $1.5^{6}\approx 11.39$.

Plot each year's membership (taking the original as $1$) as a point, and the question becomes "which is the first point above the horizontal line $y=10$?":

<Graph desc="The horizontal axis is the number of years n and the vertical axis is the membership as a multiple of the original. **Example (decay).** Each filtering removes $20\%$ of the impurities in the water. At least how many filterings leave less than $1\%$ of the original impurities?

Each time $80\%$ remains, so we need $0.8^{n}<0.01$. $\log 0.8=\log 8-1=3\log 2-1\approx -0.0970$, so $-0.0970n<-2$ and $n>\frac{2}{0.0970}\approx 20.6$: at least $21$ times. Note that dividing both sides by the negative number $-0.0970$ reverses the inequality.

### Average growth rate

If the growth rates over two years are $10\%$ and $30\%$, what is the average growth per year <Zh>平均成長率</Zh>? Over the two years the total becomes $1.1\times 1.3=1.43$ times as much. The average growth rate $r$ means "growing at $r$ every year gives the same result after two years":

$$
(1+r)^{2}=1.1\times 1.3=1.43,\qquad 1+r=\sqrt{1.43}\approx 1.196
$$

So the average growth rate is about $19.6\%$, not $\frac{10\%+30\%}{2}=20\%$. $1+r$ is the geometric mean of $1.1$ and $1.3$; by the <Term t="math.am-gm-inequality">AM–GM inequality</Term> it is never larger than the arithmetic mean $1.2$. An average rate of decay is found the same way.

<Perspective title="See it as area: trade the rectangle for a square of the same size">

Take the two yearly factors $1.1$ and $1.3$ as the sides of a rectangle. Its area $1.1\times 1.3=1.43$ is the total factor over the two years. "The same every year" means both sides equal, so we want the square with the same area $1.43$; its side $\sqrt{1.43}\approx 1.196$ is $1+r$.

<Graph desc="On the left, the orange rectangle has sides p and q (the two yearly factors); its area pq is the total factor over two years. On the right, the blue square has side √(pq), the same area as the rectangle, and its side is the average yearly factor. The black dashed square has side (p + q) ÷ 2, the factor you would get by simply averaging the percentages. For p = 1.1 and q = 1.3 the blue square's side is about 1.196 and the dashed square's side is 1.2, slightly larger; drag p and q to the same value and all three become the same square." x={[-0.2, 3.9]} y={[-0.2, 1.8]} height={260}
  params={[{ name: 'p', min: 0.5, max: 1.6, step: 0.05, value: 1.1 }, { name: 'q', min: 0.5, max: 1.6, step: 0.05, value: 1.3 }]}
  polygons={[{ points: [[0, 0], ['p', 0], ['p', 'q'], [0, 'q']], color: 'amber' }, { points: [[2, 0], ['2+sqrt(p*q)', 0], ['2+sqrt(p*q)', 'sqrt(p*q)'], [2, 'sqrt(p*q)']], color: 'blue' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [2, '(p+q)/2'], to: ['2+(p+q)/2', '(p+q)/2'], color: 'ink', dashed: true }, { from: ['2+(p+q)/2', 0], to: ['2+(p+q)/2', '(p+q)/2'], color: 'ink', dashed: true }]}
  labels={[{ at: [0.05, 1.65], text: 'area pq' }, { at: [2.05, 1.65], text: 'side √(pq)', color: 'blue' }]} />

Why can the plain average ($1.2$) never come out smaller? A square of side $1.2$ has area $1.44$, which is more than $1.43$ by

$$
1.2^{2}-1.1\times 1.3=\left(\frac{1.1+1.3}{2}\right)^{2}-1.1\times 1.3=\left(\frac{1.3-1.1}{2}\right)^{2}=0.1^{2}=0.01
$$

In general $\left(\frac{p+q}{2}\right)^{2}-pq=\left(\frac{p-q}{2}\right)^{2}\ge 0$, which is the AM–GM inequality. The more the two years' rates differ, the more the plain average overestimates; only when they are equal do the two agree.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Take logarithms: the average growth rate is the average of the logarithms">

Taking $\log$ of both sides of $(1+r)^{2}=1.1\times 1.3$ gives $2\log(1+r)=\log 1.1+\log 1.3$, that is,

$$
\log(1+r)=\frac{\log 1.1+\log 1.3}{2}\approx\frac{0.0414+0.1139}{2}\approx 0.0777
$$

In the world of logarithms, the average growth rate is an ordinary "add and divide by $2$". $10^{0.0777}\approx 1.196$, the same as the square root.

<NumberLine desc="A number line from 0 to 0.12 marking log 1.1 ≈ 0.0414 and log 1.3 ≈ 0.1139, joined by an orange stretch; the point exactly in the middle, about 0.0777, is log(1 + r), and converting back gives 1 + r = 10^0.0777 ≈ 1.196." from={0} to={0.12} step={0.01} labelEvery={2} intervals={[{ a: 0.0414, b: 0.1139, color: 'amber' }]} points={[{ x: 0.0414, label: 'log 1.1' }, { x: 0.0777, label: 'log(1+r)' }, { x: 0.1139, label: 'log 1.3' }]} />

So the "average for multiplying" (the geometric mean) becomes, after taking logarithms, the "average for adding" (the arithmetic mean). Three or four years work the same way: average the logarithms of the yearly factors, then convert back.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Why can't I just average the two percentages?">

Because growth builds up by **multiplying**, layer on layer, not by adding. The second year's $30\%$ is taken on the amount after the first year's growth. So what you want is the factor that gives the same result when applied twice, which means a square root, not adding and dividing by $2$.

</StudentAsks>

## A logarithmic scale: earthquake magnitude

Quantities that range over a huge span are often measured with logarithms. In a simplified model, the earthquake magnitude <Zh>地震規模</Zh> $M$ is defined from the measured amplitude $A$ and a standard amplitude $A_0$ as

$$
M=\log\frac{A}{A_0}
$$

So each extra $1$ of magnitude means $10$ times the amplitude. **Example.** How many times larger is the amplitude of a magnitude-$6$ earthquake than a magnitude-$4$ one? $6-4=\log\frac{A_6}{A_0}-\log\frac{A_4}{A_0}=\log\frac{A_6}{A_4}$, so $\frac{A_6}{A_4}=10^{2}=100$ times. The real-life applications the curriculum mentions include earthquake magnitude like this and personal finance.

<NumberLine desc="A number line of earthquake magnitude from 0 to 8; each step means one more unit of magnitude and 10 times the amplitude. <Perspective title="Convert back to exponential form, then divide">

You can do without the laws of logarithms. By definition, $M=\log\frac{A}{A_0}$ means $\frac{A}{A_0}=10^{M}$, that is, $A=10^{M}A_0$. So

$$
A_4=10^{4}A_0,\qquad A_6=10^{6}A_0,\qquad \frac{A_6}{A_4}=\frac{10^{6}}{10^{4}}=10^{2}=100
$$

This also explains what "one step" on the number line means: going from magnitude $4$ to $5$ to $6$, the amplitude goes $10^{4}A_0\to 10^{5}A_0\to 10^{6}A_0$, multiplying by $10$ each step. It works for differences that are not whole numbers too: a difference of $0.3$ means $10^{0.3}\approx 2$ times the amplitude.

### Math B: $e$ and continuous compounding

Take an annual interest rate of $100\%$ on $1$ dollar. Paid once a year, you have $2$ dollars at year end; if the year is split into $n$ interest periods at rate $\frac{1}{n}$ each, you have $\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}$ dollars at year end:

| $n$ | $1$ | $2$ | $4$ | $12$ | $365$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\approx$ | $2$ | $2.25$ | $2.4414$ | $2.6130$ | $2.7146$ |

The more interest periods, the more money, but it does not grow without limit: it gets closer and closer to a fixed number, written $e$, with $e=2.71828\cdots$, an irrational number. Splitting the interest infinitely finely is called continuous compounding <Zh>連續複利</Zh>: with principal $P_0$ and annual rate $r$, continuous compounding gives $P_0e^{rt}$ after $t$ years.

The logarithm to base $e$ is called the <Term t="math.natural-logarithm">natural logarithm</Term>, written $\ln x=\log_e x$; for example $\ln e=1$ and $\ln 1=0$.

<Perspective later title="Another face of e: the tangent at (0, 1) has slope exactly 1" needs="derivatives and tangent slopes (elective trick)">

Every $y=a^{x}$ passes through $(0,1)$, but they are not equally steep there. Look at the slope $\dfrac{a^{h}-1}{h}$ of the line through two very close points $(0,1)$ and $(h,a^{h})$, with $h=0.001$:

- $a=2$: $\dfrac{2^{0.001}-1}{0.001}\approx 0.693$
- $a=3$: $\dfrac{3^{0.001}-1}{0.001}\approx 1.099$

One is less than $1$ and the other more, so some base between $2$ and $3$ makes this slope (its limit as $h$ shrinks, that is, the slope of the tangent) exactly $1$, and that base is $e$. Connect it with continuous compounding: $e\approx\left(1+h\right)^{\frac{1}{h}}$ (with $h=\frac{1}{n}$ small), so $e^{h}\approx 1+h$ and $\dfrac{e^{h}-1}{h}\approx 1$. For a general $a$, the slope of the tangent at $(0,1)$ is $\ln a$.
