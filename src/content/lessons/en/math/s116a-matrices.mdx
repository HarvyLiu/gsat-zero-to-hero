## A matrix times a vector: a linear combination of the columns

Numbers arranged in horizontal **rows** <Zh>列</Zh> and vertical **columns** <Zh>行</Zh> form a <Term t="math.matrix">matrix</Term>. One with $m$ rows and $n$ columns is an $m\times n$ matrix; one with as many rows as columns is a **square matrix** <Zh>方陣</Zh>, such as a $2\times2$ matrix.

A 2×2 matrix times a vector is defined by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}ax+by\\cx+dy\end{bmatrix}
=x\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}+y\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}
$$

The middle form is "row times column": multiply the first row $(a,b)$ by $(x,y)$ entry by entry and add. The right-hand form matters more: the result is **$x$ of the first column plus $y$ of the second column**, a <Term t="math.linear-combination">linear combination</Term> of the two column vectors.

So the system $\begin{cases}ax+by=e\\cx+dy=f\end{cases}$ can be written

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e\\f\end{bmatrix}
$$

It asks: how much of each column vector do you need to make $\begin{bmatrix}e\\f\end{bmatrix}$ on the right?

**Example.** Write $\begin{cases}3x+2y=8\\x-y=1\end{cases}$ as a matrix times a vector, and say what it means as a linear combination.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8\\1\end{bmatrix}
\iff
x\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+y\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

We want $x$ and $y$ so that $x$ times the vector $(3,1)$ plus $y$ times $(2,-1)$ equals $(8,1)$. The next section finds $x=2$, $y=1$: $2(3,1)+1(2,-1)=(8,1)$.

When a matrix multiplies a vector, entry $i$ of the result is "row $i$" times the vector, entry by entry and added; the whole result is a linear combination of the columns. ## Cramer's formula and the area of a parallelogram

The absolute value of the 2×2 <Term t="math.determinant">determinant</Term> $\begin{vmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{vmatrix}=ad-bc$ is the area of the parallelogram whose adjacent sides are the column vectors $(a,c)$ and $(b,d)$. It lets you write down the solution of a system directly.

Let the coefficient determinant be $\Delta=\begin{vmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{vmatrix}$. Replacing the first column by the constants gives $\Delta_x=\begin{vmatrix}e&b\\f&d\end{vmatrix}$, and replacing the second column gives $\Delta_y=\begin{vmatrix}a&e\\c&f\end{vmatrix}$. <Term t="math.cramers-formula">Cramer's formula</Term> says that when $\Delta\ne0$ the system has exactly one solution

$$
x=\frac{\Delta_x}{\Delta},\qquad y=\frac{\Delta_y}{\Delta}
$$

Why? Call the columns $\vec u=(a,c)$ and $\vec v=(b,d)$; then the constant vector is $(e,f)=x\vec u+y\vec v$. $\Delta_x$ is the (signed) area of the parallelogram on $(e,f)$ and $\vec v$. The part $y\vec v$ is parallel to $\vec v$ and doesn't change that area, so it is the area on $x\vec u$ and $\vec v$, which is $x$ times the area on $\vec u$ and $\vec v$: $\Delta_x=x\Delta$. The same argument works for $y$.

<Graph desc="Using the example below: u=(3, 1), v=(2, −1), and the solution is x=2, y=1. The red parallelogram has sides u and v, vertices (0, 0), (3, 1), (5, 0), (2, −1), and area |Δ|=5. The orange parallelogram has sides v and 2u+s·v, vertices (0, 0), (6+2s, 2−s), (8+2s, 1−s), (2, −1). **Example.** Solve $\begin{cases}3x+2y=8\\x-y=1\end{cases}$ with Cramer's formula.

$$
\Delta=\begin{vmatrix}3&2\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-5,\quad
\Delta_x=\begin{vmatrix}8&2\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-10,\quad
\Delta_y=\begin{vmatrix}3&8\\1&1\end{vmatrix}=-5
$$

So $x=\frac{-10}{-5}=2$ and $y=\frac{-5}{-5}=1$. Along the way: the parallelogram on $(3,1)$ and $(2,-1)$ has area $|\Delta|=5$.

<Perspective title="The same formula from elimination">

You don't need area; just eliminate. Multiply $ax+by=e$ by $d$ and $cx+dy=f$ by $b$, and subtract to remove $y$:

$$
(ad-bc)x=ed-bf\iff \Delta\cdot x=\Delta_x
$$

In the same way, multiply the second equation by $a$ and the first by $c$ and subtract to remove $x$: $(ad-bc)y=af-ce$, that is, $\Delta\cdot y=\Delta_y$.

Check with the example: $d=-1$ and $b=2$, so multiply the first equation by $-1$, the second by $2$, and subtract:

$$
-(3x+2y)-2(x-y)=-8-2\iff -5x=-10
$$

The $-5$ on the left is exactly $\Delta$ and the $-10$ on the right is exactly $\Delta_x$, so $x=2$.

This view also explains the three cases below at once: when $\Delta=0$, the equation $\Delta\cdot x=\Delta_x$ becomes $0=\Delta_x$, which is a contradiction if $\Delta_x\ne0$ (no solution) and puts no restriction on $x$ if $\Delta_x=0$.

</Perspective>

### One solution, no solution, infinitely many

Each linear equation in two unknowns is a line, and a solution is a point where the two lines meet:

- $\Delta\ne0$: the lines meet in one point, so there is **exactly one solution**.
- $\Delta=0$ but at least one of $\Delta_x$, $\Delta_y$ is not $0$: the lines are parallel, so there is **no solution**.
- $\Delta=\Delta_x=\Delta_y=0$: when both equations are lines (in each, the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ are not both $0$), the lines coincide and there are **infinitely many solutions**.

The default a=3, c=8 gives 3x+2y=8, and the lines meet at (2, 1): exactly one solution. Drag a to −2: Δ=0 and both lines have slope 1; for c≠−2 the lines are parallel and there is no solution; for c=−2 they coincide and there are infinitely many solutions." x={[-5, 6]} y={[-6, 8]}

<StudentAsks q="Why can't I just use the formula when Δ = 0?">

Because the formula divides by $\Delta$. $\Delta=0$ means the two columns are parallel, so their linear combinations all lie on one line: if the vector on the right is off that line you can't make it (no solution), and if it is on the line there are infinitely many ways. Then go back to the equations and check whether the two lines are parallel or the same line.

</StudentAsks>

## Three-variable systems: augmented matrices and Gaussian elimination

A system of three linear equations is solved by elimination: remove one unknown to get two, then one. Write the coefficients and constants as an <Term t="math.augmented-matrix">augmented matrix</Term> and elimination becomes work on rows; this is <Term t="math.gaussian-elimination">Gaussian elimination</Term>. There are three **row operations** <Zh>列運算</Zh>, and none of them changes the solutions:

1. swap two rows;
2. multiply a row by a number that is not $0$;
3. add a multiple of one row to another.

The goal is an upper-triangular matrix, with only $0$s in the lower-left corner; then substitute back from the bottom row up.

**Example.** Solve $\begin{cases}x+y+z=6\\2x-y+z=3\\x+2y-z=2\end{cases}$.

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\2&-1&1&3\\1&2&-1&2\end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow[\text{row 3}-\text{row 1}]{\text{row 2}-2\times\text{row 1}}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\0&-3&-1&-9\\0&1&-2&-4\end{array}\right]
$$

Swap rows 2 and 3, then add $3$ times the new row 2 to the new row 3:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\0&1&-2&-4\\0&-3&-1&-9\end{array}\right]
\longrightarrow
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&1&1&6\\0&1&-2&-4\\0&0&-7&-21\end{array}\right]
$$

From the bottom up: $-7z=-21$, so $z=3$; $y-2z=-4$, so $y=2$; $x+y+z=6$, so $x=1$. Check the second equation: $2-2+3=3$.

If elimination leaves a row $\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}0&0&0&k\end{array}\right]$: for $k\ne0$ it says $0=k$, which can never hold, so there is **no solution**; for $k=0$ the row sets no condition, and as long as no other row gives a contradiction $0=k$ with $k\ne0$, there are **infinitely many solutions**, written with a parameter (such as $t$). The curriculum notes that Gaussian elimination does not extend to rank, so all you need is to read these three cases off the reduced matrix.

Each linear equation in three unknowns is a plane in space, and exactly one solution means the three planes meet in one point. The curriculum marks "deciding algebraically how three planes sit" <Zh>三平面幾何關係的代數判定</Zh> with ★, so it is not a direct exam target; here it is only a picture to help you imagine.

The first two equations, $x+y+z=6$ and $2x-y+z=3$, are two planes that are not parallel, so they meet in a line. Subtracting them gives $x-2y=-3$; let $y=t$:

$$
x=2t-3,\quad y=t,\quad z=6-x-y=9-3t
$$

This is a parametric form, as in the chapter on planes and lines in space. Put it into the third plane $x+2y-z=2$:

$$
(2t-3)+2t-(9-3t)=2\iff 7t-12=2\iff t=2
$$

So the meeting point is $(2\cdot2-3,\ 2,\ 9-3\cdot2)=(1,2,3)$, the same answer as elimination.

Row operations mean something in the picture too: "row 2 $-2\times$ row 1" gives $-3y-z=-9$, a different plane that also contains this line (substituting gives $-3t-(9-3t)=-9$ for every $t$). Adding a multiple of one row to another swaps one plane for another plane through the same intersection, and swapping two rows or scaling a row by a nonzero number doesn't change any plane at all. That is why the solutions don't change.

You can eliminate directly; the arithmetic is exactly the same. The curriculum also mentions the idea of solving linear systems by computer, and fixed steps like these suit a computer well; still, you need to be able to carry them out step by step yourself.

### One use of systems: a quadratic through three points

To find $y=ax^2+bx+c$ through three given points, substitute the points and you get three linear equations in $a$, $b$, $c$. The curriculum notes a link to Newton's interpolation polynomial <Zh>牛頓插值多項式</Zh>: written as $y=p+q(x-x_1)+r(x-x_1)(x-x_2)$, substituting $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ gives a system that is already triangular (the first equation has only $p$, the second only $p$ and $q$), so you can solve for $p$, $q$, $r$ one at a time.

## Matrix operations

**Equality, addition, subtraction and scalar multiples.** Two matrices can be compared, added or subtracted only when they have the same size, and all of these work **entry by entry**. A real number $k$ times a matrix (a scalar multiple <Zh>係數積</Zh>) multiplies every entry by $k$.

**Multiplication.** When $A$ is $m\times n$ and $B$ is $n\times p$ (the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$), $AB$ is an $m\times p$ matrix whose entry in row $i$, column $j$ is row $i$ of $A$ times column $j$ of $B$, entry by entry and added. A matrix times a vector is the case $p=1$.

Matrix multiplication is **in general not commutative**: $AB$ and $BA$ are usually different, and one of them may not even exist.

**Example.** Shop 1 sells $3$ of product A and $2$ of product B; shop 2 sells $1$ of A and $4$ of B. A costs $50$ dollars each and B costs $30$. Use matrices to find each shop's takings.

Treat the data table as a matrix: each row is a shop, each column a product.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}50\\30\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}3\times50+2\times30\\1\times50+4\times30\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}210\\170\end{bmatrix}
$$

Shop 1 takes $210$ dollars and shop 2 takes $170$. Given a second day's sales table, adding the two tables gives the total sold over both days.

<Perspective title="By columns: how much each product brings in">

Above we went "row times column", one shop at a time. You can also use the column picture from the first section and go one product at a time:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}50\\30\end{bmatrix}
=50\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+30\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}150\\50\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}60\\120\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}210\\170\end{bmatrix}
$$

$\begin{bmatrix}150\\50\end{bmatrix}$ is what product A brings the two shops, and $\begin{bmatrix}60\\120\end{bmatrix}$ is what product B brings. This view is especially quick for "if one product's price goes up, how much more does each shop take?": if A goes up by $10$ dollars each, the takings rise by $10\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}30\\10\end{bmatrix}$, that is, $30$ dollars more for shop 1 and $10$ more for shop 2.

### 2×2 inverse matrices

A square matrix with $1$s on the main diagonal and $0$s elsewhere is the **identity matrix** <Zh>單位方陣</Zh> $I$, for example $I=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$. For any 2×2 matrix $A$, $AI=IA=A$, just like $1$ in multiplying numbers.

If a square matrix $B$ satisfies $AB=BA=I$, then $B$ is the <Term t="math.inverse-matrix">inverse matrix</Term> of $A$, written $A^{-1}$. The curriculum says inverses can be discussed conceptually for any size, but exact computation is limited to 2×2:

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix},\ ad-bc\ne0
\implies
A^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}
$$

To remember it: swap the diagonal entries, change the sign of the other two, and divide by the determinant. When $ad-bc=0$, $A$ has no inverse; this is the same fact as "no unique solution when $\Delta=0$" in Cramer's formula.

With an inverse, multiply both sides of $AX=B$ on the left by $A^{-1}$ to get $X=A^{-1}B$.

**Example.** Solve $\begin{cases}2x+y=4\\5x+3y=11\end{cases}$ with an inverse matrix.

$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\5&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $ad-bc=6-5=1$, so $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{bmatrix}$.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}4\\11\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}3\times4-1\times11\\-5\times4+2\times11\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}
$$

<Perspective title="The inverse formula is Cramer's formula">

Multiply the formula out:

$$
A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}e\\f\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}de-bf\\-ce+af\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_x/\Delta\\ \Delta_y/\Delta\end{bmatrix}
$$

because $de-bf=\begin{vmatrix}e&b\\f&d\end{vmatrix}=\Delta_x$ and $af-ce=\begin{vmatrix}a&e\\c&f\end{vmatrix}=\Delta_y$. So solving with the inverse and solving with Cramer's formula are two ways of writing the same calculation.

Check with the example: $\Delta=1$, $\Delta_x=\begin{vmatrix}4&1\\11&3\end{vmatrix}=12-11=1$ and $\Delta_y=\begin{vmatrix}2&4\\5&11\end{vmatrix}=22-20=2$, giving $x=1$, $y=2$ as above. The column picture checks it as well: $1\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\11\end{bmatrix}$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="A matrix as a transformation of the plane" needs="matrices as transformations of the plane" href="/en/math/s116a-bayes-matrix-applications/">

Send each point $(x,y)$ to $A\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}$, and the matrix becomes a transformation of the plane. $A$ sends $(1,0)$ to its first column $(2,5)$ and $(0,1)$ to its second column $(1,3)$, so the square of side $1$ goes to the parallelogram with the two columns as sides, and areas are multiplied by $|ad-bc|$.

Solving $AX=B$ asks "which point is sent to $B$?", and $A^{-1}$ runs the transformation backwards. In the example, $A$ sends $(1,2)$ to $(4,11)$, so $A^{-1}$ sends $(4,11)$ back to $(1,2)$.

When $ad-bc=0$, the square is squashed into a segment and the whole plane onto a line: different points land on the same point, there is no way back, and so there is no inverse.

<StudentAsks q="Can I write X = A⁻¹B as X = BA⁻¹?">

No. Matrix multiplication doesn't commute, and $A^{-1}$ must go on the **left** of $B$, because we multiplied both sides of $AX=B$ on the left by $A^{-1}$: $A^{-1}AX=IX=X$. Besides, $B$ is $2\times1$ and $A^{-1}$ is $2\times2$, so the sizes of $BA^{-1}$ don't even match and the product can't be formed.
