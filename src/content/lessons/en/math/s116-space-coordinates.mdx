## Coordinates in space: three perpendicular number lines

Pick a point $O$ in space as the **origin** and draw three number lines through $O$, each perpendicular to the other two: the $x$-axis, the $y$-axis and the $z$-axis. We arrange them as a **right-handed system** <Zh>右手系</Zh>: when the fingers of your right hand curl from the positive $x$-axis towards the positive $y$-axis, your thumb points along the positive $z$-axis.

Each pair of axes determines a **coordinate plane** <Zh>坐標平面</Zh>:

| Coordinate plane | Axes it contains | Its points satisfy |
|---|---|---|
| $xy$-plane | $x$-axis, $y$-axis | $z=0$ |
| $yz$-plane | $y$-axis, $z$-axis | $x=0$ |
| $xz$-plane | $x$-axis, $z$-axis | $y=0$ |

The three coordinate planes cut space into eight regions, the eight **octants** <Zh>卦限</Zh>.

For a point $P$ in space, draw through $P$ the three planes perpendicular to the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-axes. If they meet the axes at the coordinates $a$, $b$ and $c$, the coordinates of $P$ are $P(a,b,c)$. Put the other way round: starting from the origin, walk $a$ in the $x$-direction, then $b$ in the $y$-direction, then $c$ in the $z$-direction, and you arrive at $P$.

**Example.** Plot the point $P(2,3,4)$.

The safest way is to draw a rectangular box with $O$ and $P$ as opposite corners and edges along the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-axes of lengths $2$, $3$ and $4$. Its eight corners are

$$
O(0,0,0),\ (2,0,0),\ (0,3,0),\ (0,0,4),\ (2,3,0),\ (0,3,4),\ (2,0,4),\ P(2,3,4)
$$

Find $(2,3,0)$ in the $xy$-plane first, then go $4$ "up" in the positive $z$-direction to reach $P$.

Look at "the $x$-coordinate of $P$ is $2$" another way: all the points whose $x$-coordinate is $2$ form a plane perpendicular to the $x$-axis through $(2,0,0)$, written $x=2$. In the same way, $y=3$ is the plane perpendicular to the $y$-axis through $(0,3,0)$, and $z=4$ is the plane perpendicular to the $z$-axis through $(0,0,4)$.

So "write down $P(2,3,4)$" is the same as solving the simplest possible system of three linear equations in three unknowns

$$
\begin{cases} x=2\\ y=3\\ z=4 \end{cases}
$$

and geometrically it says that three planes meet in one point. Step by step:

- The planes $x=2$ and $y=3$ meet in a line: $x=2$, $y=3$, $z$ anything, that is, the line through $(2,3,0)$ parallel to the $z$-axis.
- That line meets the plane $z=4$ in one point, namely $(2,3,4)$.

This is exactly "find $(2,3,0)$ in the $xy$-plane, then go up $4$": the first two equations fix a vertical line, and the third fixes the height on it. The three faces of the box through $P$ are pieces of these three planes. The coordinate planes are the same idea: the $xy$-plane is $z=0$, and the three coordinate planes meet at the origin $(0,0,0)$.

<StudentAsks q="Can the three axes point any way I like?">

No. If you reverse the $z$-axis of a right-handed system you get a different arrangement (a left-handed system), and the same coordinates land on the mirror-image position. By convention we always use a right-handed system. On paper the $x$-axis is often drawn slanting down to the left (towards you), the $y$-axis to the right and the $z$-axis upwards.

## Projecting a point on the coordinate axes and planes

Drop a perpendicular from $P$ to a coordinate plane (or coordinate axis). Its foot is the **projection** <Zh>投影</Zh> of $P$ on that plane (or axis). It is the same move as the <Term t="math.orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</Term> in the chapter on the laws of sines and cosines: draw the perpendicular and take its foot.

In coordinates, projections are very easy: **keep only the coordinates the target has, and replace the others by $0$**. For $P(a,b,c)$:

| Project on | Projection | Distance from $P$ |
|---|---|---|
| $xy$-plane | $(a,b,0)$ | $\lvert c\rvert$ |
| $yz$-plane | $(0,b,c)$ | $\lvert a\rvert$ |
| $xz$-plane | $(a,0,c)$ | $\lvert b\rvert$ |
| $x$-axis | $(a,0,0)$ | $\sqrt{b^2+c^2}$ |
| $y$-axis | $(0,b,0)$ | $\sqrt{a^2+c^2}$ |
| $z$-axis | $(0,0,c)$ | $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ |

Compare the two kinds of distance: the distance to a **coordinate plane** depends on **one** coordinate (the one set to $0$); the distance to a **coordinate axis** depends on the **other two** coordinates.

**Example.** Let $P(3,-4,12)$. Find the projections of $P$ on the $xy$-plane and on the $z$-axis, and the distances from $P$ to the $xy$-plane, the $z$-axis and the origin.

The projection on the $xy$-plane is $(3,-4,0)$ and the projection on the $z$-axis is $(0,0,12)$. The distance from $P$ to the $xy$-plane is $\lvert 12\rvert=12$; the distance to the $z$-axis is the distance from $P$ to $(0,0,12)$, which is $\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2}=5$. The distance from $P$ to the origin is $\sqrt{9+16+144}=\sqrt{169}=13$.

<Perspective title="Looking straight down: distance to the z-axis becomes a distance in the plane">

Stand high up on the positive $z$-axis and look down. The whole $z$-axis shrinks to one point (the origin), and $P(a,b,c)$ appears to sit on its projection $(a,b)$; the height $c$ cannot be seen at all.

The perpendicular from $P$ to the $z$-axis runs from $P$ to $(0,0,c)$. It is horizontal (both ends have $z$-coordinate $c$), so seen from straight above it is not shortened: it looks exactly like the segment in the plane from $(a,b)$ to the origin. Therefore

$$
\text{distance from }P\text{ to the }z\text{-axis}=\text{distance in the plane from }(a,b)\text{ to the origin}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
$$

This is the $r$ in the polar coordinates of $(a,b)$. The example's $P(3,-4,12)$ seen from above is $(3,-4)$, at distance $5$ from the origin, just as computed before; whatever the height $12$ is changed to, the answer stays the same. Looking along the positive $x$-axis instead, the $x$-axis shrinks to a point and $P$ appears at $(b,c)=(-4,12)$ in the $yz$-plane, so the distance from $P$ to the $x$-axis is $\sqrt{16+144}=4\sqrt{10}$.

Drag the sliders in the figure: the points at a fixed distance $r$ from the $z$-axis look like a circle of radius $r$ from above; in space they form a cylinder around the $z$-axis.

<StudentAsks q="Why isn't a point's distance to the x-axis just the absolute value of its x-coordinate?">

Because $\lvert a\rvert$ measures how far $P$ is from the $yz$-plane in the $x$-direction: that is the distance to the $yz$-plane. To reach the $x$-axis you walk from $P$ perpendicularly to $(a,0,0)$ on the $x$-axis. That walk never changes the $x$-coordinate, only $y$ and $z$, so its length is $\sqrt{b^2+c^2}$. A way to remember it: **for the distance to something, drop the coordinates that something has, and take the square root of the sum of squares of the rest.**

</StudentAsks>

## The distance between two points in space

Start with the distance from $P(a,b,c)$ to the origin. Let $Q(a,b,0)$ be the projection of $P$ on the $xy$-plane:

- In the $xy$-plane, $\overline{OQ}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$: the Pythagorean theorem in the plane.
- $\overline{PQ}$ is perpendicular to the $xy$-plane, so $\overline{PQ}\perp\overline{OQ}$, and $\overline{PQ}=\lvert c\rvert$. Use the Pythagorean theorem once more:

$$
\overline{OP}^2=\overline{OQ}^2+\overline{PQ}^2=a^2+b^2+c^2,\qquad \overline{OP}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}
$$

Take the opening's $P(2,3,6)$: $Q(2,3,0)$, $\overline{OQ}=\sqrt{4+9}=\sqrt{13}$, $\overline{PQ}=6$, so $\overline{OP}=\sqrt{13+36}=7$.

The orange segment QP goes straight up, has length 6 and is perpendicular to OQ. This is also the length of the diagonal of a box with edges $a$, $b$ and $c$. For two general points $A(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $B(x_2,y_2,z_2)$, just put in the three coordinate differences:

$$
\overline{AB}=\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2+(z_1-z_2)^2}
$$

**Example.** Find the distance between $A(1,2,-1)$ and $B(3,-4,8)$.

The coordinate differences are $2$, $-6$ and $9$, so $\overline{AB}=\sqrt{4+36+81}=\sqrt{121}=11$.

<Perspective title="Put the two points in a box: first across, then up">

Two general points don't have to be moved to the origin first. Draw the box with $A$ and $B$ as opposite corners and edges parallel to the axes, and take $C(3,-4,-1)$: it is at the same height as $A$ ($z=-1$) and straight below $B$ (same $x$ and $y$ as $B$).

- $\overline{AC}$ is horizontal, in the plane $z=-1$. Seen from straight above it is the segment in the plane from $(1,2)$ to $(3,-4)$, so $\overline{AC}=\sqrt{2^2+6^2}=\sqrt{40}$.
- $\overline{CB}$ is vertical; its length is the height difference $\lvert 8-(-1)\rvert=9$, and $\overline{CB}\perp\overline{AC}$.
- In the right triangle $ACB$, $\overline{AB}=\sqrt{40+81}=\sqrt{121}=11$.

So a distance in space splits into "the distance between the two projections in the $xy$-plane" and "the height difference", put back together with one more Pythagorean theorem. The edges of this box are exactly the absolute coordinate differences $2$, $6$ and $9$, and $\overline{AB}$ is its diagonal. If the two points in a problem share a coordinate (say they are at the same height), this step leaves only a distance in the plane.

The distance formula is also handy for setting up equations, for example to find a point equally far from two points: square both distances and set them equal, and the squared terms usually cancel.

<StudentAsks q="What if I subtract in the wrong order?">

Nothing goes wrong. Every term is squared and $(x_1-x_2)^2=(x_2-x_1)^2$, so the order doesn't matter. What you do need to watch is the minus signs: the difference between $-1$ and $8$ is $9$, not $7$.

</StudentAsks>

## Coordinates from latitude and longitude

In the Math B version of this learning content <Zh>學習內容</Zh>, the curriculum also lists computing coordinates in space from latitude and longitude on a sphere centred at the origin, using generalised angles and trigonometric ratios. The Math A version does not list this, but the tools it uses (trigonometric ratios, the distance to the $z$-axis) are exactly the ones above.

A problem usually tells you how the axes are placed. Here is a common set-up: the sphere has centre $O$ and radius $R$; the equator lies in the $xy$-plane and the North Pole on the positive $z$-axis; the meridian of longitude $0^\circ$ passes through the positive $x$-axis.

- The <Term t="math.latitude">latitude</Term> $\theta$ is the angle between $\overline{OP}$ and the equatorial plane, positive for north and negative for south.
- The <Term t="math.longitude">longitude</Term> $\phi$ is the angle turned about the $z$-axis, positive for east and negative for west: a <Term t="math.generalized-angle">generalised angle</Term>.

Let $P$ be at latitude $\theta$ N and let $Q$ be the projection of $P$ on the $xy$-plane. In the right triangle $OQP$, $\angle POQ=\theta$ and the hypotenuse is $\overline{OP}=R$, so

$$
z=\overline{PQ}=R\sin\theta,\qquad \overline{OQ}=R\cos\theta
$$

$\overline{OQ}=R\cos\theta$ is exactly the distance from $P$ to the $z$-axis: the radius of the parallel (worked out in the Math B part of the last chapter). In the $xy$-plane, $Q$ has <Term t="math.polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</Term> $[R\cos\theta,\phi]$, so

$$
P=(R\cos\theta\cos\phi,\ R\cos\theta\sin\phi,\ R\sin\theta)
$$

**Example.** On a sphere of radius $6$, find the coordinates of the point at latitude $30^\circ$ N, longitude $60^\circ$ E.

$z=6\sin 30^\circ=3$, and the parallel has radius $6\cos 30^\circ=3\sqrt{3}$. So $x=3\sqrt{3}\cos 60^\circ=\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $y=3\sqrt{3}\sin 60^\circ=\frac{9}{2}$: the point is $\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{9}{2},3\right)$. Check: $\frac{27}{4}+\frac{81}{4}+9=36=6^2$, so it really is on the sphere.

<Perspective title="The other way round: read latitude and longitude off the coordinates">

The same figures also work backwards: given the coordinates of a point on the sphere, use the height to fix the latitude, then the shadow seen from above to fix the longitude.

**Example.** The point $P(3,3,3\sqrt{2})$ is on the sphere of radius $6$. Find its latitude and longitude.

First check that it is on the sphere: $9+9+18=36=6^2$.

- From the cross-section: $z=6\sin\theta=3\sqrt{2}$, so $\sin\theta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Latitude lies between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, so $\theta=45^\circ$: latitude $45^\circ$ N. Check: the distance to the $z$-axis is $\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt{2}=6\cos 45^\circ$, matching the radius of the parallel.
- From the view from above: the projection $(3,3)$ is in the first quadrant with equal legs, so its polar coordinates are $[3\sqrt{2},45^\circ]$ and the longitude is $45^\circ$ E.

The answer is latitude $45^\circ$ N, longitude $45^\circ$ E. Set the sliders in both figures above to $45$ to see this point. Be careful: the latitude is decided by the single coordinate $z$, but the longitude needs the signs of both $x$ and $y$ to fix the quadrant. For example, $(-3,-3,3\sqrt{2})$ is also at latitude $45^\circ$ N, but its longitude is $-135^\circ$, that is, $135^\circ$ W.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="How do I put west longitude and south latitude into the formula?">

Both go in as negative generalised angles. For $45^\circ$ W use $\phi=-45^\circ$: $\cos(-45^\circ)=\cos 45^\circ$ and $\sin(-45^\circ)=-\sin 45^\circ$, so $y$ changes sign and $x$ does not. For $30^\circ$ S use $\theta=-30^\circ$: $\cos\theta$ is unchanged, so the parallel has the same radius as at $30^\circ$ N; $\sin\theta<0$, so $z$ automatically comes out negative and the point is in the southern hemisphere.

</StudentAsks>
