## Triangle area: two sides and the angle between them

In $\triangle ABC$ we usually write $a$, $b$, $c$ for the sides opposite $\angle A$, $\angle B$, $\angle C$, and $A$, $B$, $C$ for the sizes of the three angles.

Take $\overline{BC}=a$ as the base and drop the height $h$ from $A$. If $\angle C$ is acute, $h=b\sin C$; if $\angle C$ is obtuse, the height lands on the extension of $\overline{BC}$ and $h=b\sin(180^\circ-C)$. From the trig ratios of general angles, the terminal sides of $180^\circ-\theta$ and $\theta$ are mirror images in the $y$-axis, so their points have the same $y$-coordinate and $\sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta$. So in both cases $h=b\sin C$ and the area is $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$. Any other pair of sides works the same way:

$$
\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C=\frac{1}{2}bc\sin A=\frac{1}{2}ca\sin B
$$

To remember it: **multiply two sides, multiply by the sine of the angle between those two sides, then halve**.

**Example.** In $\triangle ABC$, $\overline{AB}=5$, $\overline{AC}=4$ and $\angle A=120^\circ$. Find the area of $\triangle ABC$.

$\angle A$ is exactly the angle between $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, so the area is

$$
\frac{1}{2}\times 5\times 4\times\sin 120^\circ=10\times\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}=5\sqrt{3}
$$

## The law of sines: sides in proportion to the sines of opposite angles

Divide the three area expressions above by $\frac{1}{2}abc$ and you get $\frac{\sin A}{a}=\frac{\sin B}{b}=\frac{\sin C}{c}$. Turn it upside down and add the link to the circumcircle, and you have the <Term t="math.law-of-sines">law of sines</Term>:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R
$$

Here $R$ is the radius of the circumcircle <Zh>外接圓</Zh> of $\triangle ABC$. You can also write it as $a:b:c=\sin A:\sin B:\sin C$. Also remember that in a triangle **the longer side faces the larger angle**: comparing sides tells you how the angles compare.

The law of sines fits best when you **know two angles and one side**: get the third angle from the angle sum $180^\circ$, then find the other sides by proportion.

**Example.** In $\triangle ABC$, $\angle A=45^\circ$, $\angle B=60^\circ$ and $a=4\sqrt{2}$. Find $b$ and the circumradius $R$.

$$
b=\frac{a\sin B}{\sin A}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}\times\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}=4\sqrt{3}
$$

Also $2R=\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}=8$, so $R=4$.

<Perspective title="Work out one height twice">

You can see the law of sines directly, without going through the area. Drop the perpendicular from $C$ to $\overline{AB}$ (or its extension): the height $h$ is a leg of two right triangles at once,

$$
h=b\sin A,\qquad h=a\sin B
$$

(If $\angle A$ or $\angle B$ is obtuse, the height lies outside $\triangle ABC$ and its right triangle has the angle $180^\circ-A$ or $180^\circ-B$, which has the same sine.) So $a\sin B=b\sin A$, that is, $\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}$. Another height gives the equality with $\frac{c}{\sin C}$.

In the example above, $b=\frac{a\sin B}{\sin A}$ is exactly "two ways of working out the same height".

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Why is the ratio exactly the diameter of the circumcircle?">

Start with $\angle A$ acute. Draw the diameter $\overline{BD}$ of the circumcircle from $B$ and join $\overline{CD}$. $\angle BDC$ and $\angle A$ are inscribed angles on the same arc $BC$, so $\angle D=\angle A$; an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle, so $\angle BCD=90^\circ$. In the right triangle $BCD$, $a=\overline{BD}\sin D=2R\sin A$.

If $\angle A$ is obtuse, $D$ and $A$ are on opposite sides of $\overline{BC}$ and $\angle D=180^\circ-A$, which has the same sine. If $\angle A$ is a right angle, $\overline{BC}$ is itself a diameter, so $a=2R$ while $\sin A=1$. All three cases give $\frac{a}{\sin A}=2R$.

</StudentAsks>

## The law of cosines: the Pythagorean theorem extended

Put $A$ at the origin with $\overline{AB}$ along the positive $x$-axis, so $B(c,0)$; from polar coordinates, $C(b\cos A,\,b\sin A)$. The distance formula gives $a^2=\overline{BC}^2$:

$$
a^2=(b\cos A-c)^2+(b\sin A)^2=b^2(\cos^2 A+\sin^2 A)-2bc\cos A+c^2
$$

Since $\sin^2 A+\cos^2 A=1$, this is the <Term t="math.law-of-cosines">law of cosines</Term>:

$$
a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A
$$

Rearranged, it finds an angle:

$$
\cos A=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}
$$

The other two angles work the same way, for example $c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$. When $\angle A=90^\circ$, $\cos A=0$ and the formula turns back into the Pythagorean theorem $a^2=b^2+c^2$.

<Perspective later title="The law of cosines with vectors" needs="the dot product of plane vectors" href="/en/math/s116-plane-vectors/">

$\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{AC}-\overrightarrow{AB}$; take the dot product of each side with itself:

$$
a^2=|\overrightarrow{AC}-\overrightarrow{AB}|^2=|\overrightarrow{AC}|^2+|\overrightarrow{AB}|^2-2\,\overrightarrow{AC}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A
$$

because $\overrightarrow{AC}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}=bc\cos A$. The $-2bc\cos A$ in the law of cosines is the cross term you get when you expand "the square of a difference".

</Perspective>

| Given | Use first | You can find |
|---|---|---|
| two angles and a side | law of sines | the other two sides, the circumradius |
| two sides and the angle between them | law of cosines | the third side (for the area, use $\frac{1}{2}bc\sin A$ directly) |
| three sides | law of cosines | any angle |
| two sides and the angle opposite one of them | law of sines | another angle; there may be two, one or no solutions |

<Perspective title="Two sides and an opposite angle: solve a quadratic with the law of cosines">

The circle above decided how many triangles have $\angle A=30^\circ$ and $b=4$; you can also do it entirely with algebra. Call the side we want $c=\overline{AB}$. The law of cosines gives

$$
a^2=16+c^2-2\times 4\times c\cos 30^\circ\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c^2-4\sqrt{3}\,c+(16-a^2)=0
$$

so $c=2\sqrt{3}\pm\sqrt{a^2-4}$. **The number of positive roots is the number of triangles**:

- $a<2$: the discriminant $4a^2-16<0$, no real roots, no triangle;
- $a=2$: the double root $c=2\sqrt{3}$, one right triangle;
- $2<a<4$: the roots have sum $4\sqrt{3}>0$ and product $16-a^2>0$, so both are positive: two triangles;
- $a\ge 4$: the product $16-a^2\le 0$, only one positive root: one triangle.

For example, $a=3$: $c=2\sqrt{3}\pm\sqrt{5}$, and since $2\sqrt{3}=\sqrt{12}>\sqrt{5}$ both are positive, so there are two triangles. These two roots are exactly the $x$-coordinates of $B_1$ and $B_2$ in the earlier circle figure: the picture and the equation say the same thing.

</Perspective>

**Example (two sides and the angle between them).** In $\triangle ABC$, $b=5$, $c=8$ and $\angle A=60^\circ$. Find $a$.

$$
a^2=25+64-2\times 5\times 8\times\frac{1}{2}=49
$$

So $a=7$.

**Example (three sides).** A triangle has sides $3$, $5$ and $7$. Find its largest angle.

The longest side faces the largest angle, so we want the angle $\theta$ facing the side of length $7$:

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{3^2+5^2-7^2}{2\times 3\times 5}=\frac{-15}{30}=-\frac{1}{2}
$$

So $\theta=120^\circ$.

### Acute, right or obtuse?

The denominator $2bc$ of $\cos A$ is positive, so $\cos A$ has the same sign as $b^2+c^2-a^2$. With $a$ the longest side, just compare $a^2$ with $b^2+c^2$:

- $a^2<b^2+c^2$: the largest angle is acute, so the triangle is acute;
- $a^2=b^2+c^2$: a right triangle;
- $a^2>b^2+c^2$: an obtuse triangle.

For example, sides $2$, $3$, $4$: $4^2=16>2^2+3^2=13$, so the triangle is obtuse.

<Perspective title="With a circle: the circle on BC as diameter">

Fix $\overline{BC}$, draw the circle with $\overline{BC}$ as diameter, and see where $A$ is:

- $A$ on the circle: an angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle, so $\angle A=90^\circ$;
- $A$ inside the circle: extend $\overline{BA}$ to meet the circle at $A'$. Then $\angle BA'C=90^\circ$, and $\angle BAC$ is an exterior angle of $\triangle AA'C$, equal to $90^\circ+\angle ACA'$, so $\angle A$ is obtuse;
- $A$ outside the circle: by the same argument, $\angle A$ is acute.

The algebra agrees: take $B(-2,0)$, $C(2,0)$ and $A(x,y)$, so $a=4$ and

$$
b^2+c^2-a^2=\big[(x-2)^2+y^2\big]+\big[(x+2)^2+y^2\big]-16=2(x^2+y^2-4)
$$

The sign of $\cos A$ is the sign of $x^2+y^2-4$, which says whether $A$ is outside, on or inside the circle. For the triangle with sides $2$, $3$, $4$, take $\overline{AB}=2$ and $\overline{AC}=3$; this gives $A_0\big(-\frac{5}{8},\frac{3\sqrt{15}}{8}\big)$ with $x^2+y^2=\frac{25+135}{64}=\frac{5}{2}<4$, inside the circle, so the largest angle is obtuse, as found above. Note that the circle only judges $\angle A$; to classify the whole triangle, $\overline{BC}$ must be the longest side.

### Orthogonal projection

Drop perpendiculars from the two ends of a segment $\overline{PQ}$ to a line $L$; the segment between the two feet is the <Term t="math.orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</Term> of $\overline{PQ}$ on $L$. If $\overline{PQ}$ makes the angle $\theta$ with $L$ ($0^\circ\le\theta\le 90^\circ$), the projection has length $\overline{PQ}\cos\theta$. For example, a segment of length $6$ at $60^\circ$ to a line projects to length $6\cos 60^\circ=3$.

In $\triangle ABC$, drop the perpendicular from $A$ to $\overline{BC}$: the projections of $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$ on $\overline{BC}$ together make up $\overline{BC}$:

$$
a=b\cos C+c\cos B
$$

This still holds when $\angle B$ or $\angle C$ is obtuse: the cosine of an obtuse angle is negative, which subtracts exactly the piece that sticks out past $\overline{BC}$. Check it on the $3$, $5$, $7$ triangle above ($a=7$, $b=3$, $c=5$): $\cos B=\frac{49+25-9}{70}=\frac{13}{14}$ and $\cos C=\frac{49+9-25}{42}=\frac{11}{14}$, and $3\times\frac{11}{14}+5\times\frac{13}{14}=\frac{98}{14}=7$.

<Perspective title="The law of cosines from the projection formula">

Write the projection formula once for each side:

$$
a=b\cos C+c\cos B,\qquad b=c\cos A+a\cos C,\qquad c=a\cos B+b\cos A
$$

Multiply them by $a$, $b$ and $c$ respectively:

$$
a^2=ab\cos C+ca\cos B,\qquad b^2=bc\cos A+ab\cos C,\qquad c^2=ca\cos B+bc\cos A
$$

Add the last two and subtract the first: $ab\cos C$ and $ca\cos B$ both cancel, leaving

$$
b^2+c^2-a^2=2bc\cos A
$$

That is the law of cosines, with no coordinates at all: only "each side is the sum of the projections of the other two sides on it".

</Perspective>

## Slope and the angle between two lines

The **angle of inclination** <Zh>斜角</Zh> $\theta$ of a non-vertical line is the angle you turn anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis to the line, with $0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ$. The line's <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> is the tangent of that angle:

$$
m=\tan\theta
$$

For example, a line of slope $1$ has angle of inclination $45^\circ$, and a line of slope $-\sqrt{3}$ has $120^\circ$ (because $\tan 120^\circ=-\sqrt{3}$).

Two intersecting lines make two angles, $\alpha$ and $180^\circ-\alpha$. If the lines have angles of inclination $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, one of those angles is the difference between them.

**Example.** Find the angle between the lines $L_1:y=\sqrt{3}x+1$ and $L_2:y=-\sqrt{3}x$.

$L_1$ has slope $\sqrt{3}$, so its angle of inclination is $60^\circ$; $L_2$ has slope $-\sqrt{3}$, so its angle is $120^\circ$. They differ by $60^\circ$, so the lines meet at angles $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$.

<Perspective title="With an exterior angle: why the angle is the difference of inclinations">

The two lines and the $x$-axis enclose a triangle ($\triangle PQO$ in the figure). For the line with the larger inclination, $L_2$, the angle of inclination $\theta_2$ is the triangle's exterior angle at $O$. An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles not next to it; one of them is $L_1$'s inclination $\theta_1$ and the other is the angle $\alpha$ between the lines:

$$
\theta_2=\theta_1+\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha=\theta_2-\theta_1
$$

In the example, $120^\circ=60^\circ+60^\circ$. So "the angle is the difference of the inclinations" is no coincidence: it is the exterior angle theorem.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="The angle straight from the slopes" needs="the difference formula for tangent" href="/en/math/s116a-sum-difference/">

From $\alpha=\theta_2-\theta_1$ and the difference formula for tangent,

$$
\tan\alpha=\frac{\tan\theta_2-\tan\theta_1}{1+\tan\theta_1\tan\theta_2}=\frac{m_2-m_1}{1+m_1m_2}
$$

The example: $\tan\alpha=\frac{-\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{3}}{1+\sqrt{3}\times(-\sqrt{3})}=\frac{-2\sqrt{3}}{-2}=\sqrt{3}$, so $\alpha=60^\circ$ without finding the inclinations first. When the denominator $1+m_1m_2=0$ the angle is $90^\circ$: the familiar fact that two lines whose slopes multiply to $-1$ are perpendicular.

</Perspective>

## Triangulation on a cross-section of a cuboid

The curriculum marks triangulation <Zh>三角測量</Zh> with ＃: it is woven into teaching rather than given a unit of its own, demonstrated on cross-sections of a cuboid, and may be extended to regular pyramids. The method is always the same: find a flat triangle (a cross-section <Zh>截面</Zh>) inside the solid, work out the lengths you need with the Pythagorean theorem, then find angles or lengths with trig ratios, the law of sines or the law of cosines.

**Example.** The cuboid $ABCD\text{-}EFGH$ has base $ABCD$, with $E$, $F$, $G$, $H$ directly above $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, and $\overline{AB}=2$, $\overline{AD}=1$, $\overline{AE}=2$. In the cross-section $\triangle ACF$, find $\cos\angle ACF$.

The red cross-section ACF has as its sides the diagonals of the base, the right face BCGF and the front face ABFE. Each of the three sides is a diagonal of one face of the cuboid, so use the Pythagorean theorem once for each:

$$
\overline{AC}^2=2^2+1^2=5,\qquad \overline{CF}^2=\overline{BC}^2+\overline{BF}^2=1^2+2^2=5,\qquad \overline{AF}^2=2^2+2^2=8
$$

Then the law of cosines:

$$
\cos\angle ACF=\frac{\overline{AC}^2+\overline{CF}^2-\overline{AF}^2}{2\cdot\overline{AC}\cdot\overline{CF}}=\frac{5+5-8}{2\times\sqrt{5}\times\sqrt{5}}=\frac{2}{10}=\frac{1}{5}
$$

<Perspective title="With the isosceles triangle and its area">

$\overline{AC}=\overline{CF}=\sqrt{5}$, so the cross-section is isosceles. Take the midpoint $M$ of $\overline{AF}$: then $\overline{CM}\perp\overline{AF}$, $\overline{AM}=\sqrt{2}$ and $\overline{CM}=\sqrt{5-2}=\sqrt{3}$. Work out the area twice:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\times 2\sqrt{2}\times\sqrt{3}=\sqrt{6}=\frac{1}{2}\times\sqrt{5}\times\sqrt{5}\times\sin\angle ACF\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\angle ACF=\frac{2\sqrt{6}}{5}
$$

So $\cos^2\angle ACF=1-\frac{24}{25}=\frac{1}{25}$. Also $\overline{AF}^2=8<\overline{AC}^2+\overline{CF}^2=10$, so $\angle ACF$ is acute and $\cos\angle ACF=\frac{1}{5}$, the same answer as the law of cosines.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="With space coordinates and the dot product" needs="space coordinates and the dot product of space vectors" href="/en/math/s116a-vector-operations/">

Put coordinates on the figure as above: $A(0,0,0)$, $C(2,1,0)$, $F(2,0,2)$. Then $\overrightarrow{CA}=(-2,-1,0)$ and $\overrightarrow{CF}=(0,-1,2)$, and

$$
\cos\angle ACF=\frac{\overrightarrow{CA}\cdot\overrightarrow{CF}}{|\overrightarrow{CA}|\,|\overrightarrow{CF}|}=\frac{0+1+0}{\sqrt{5}\times\sqrt{5}}=\frac{1}{5}
$$

The angle comes straight from the coordinates, without finding side lengths on the cross-section.

</Perspective>
