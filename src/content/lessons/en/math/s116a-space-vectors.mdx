## Space vectors in coordinates

In coordinate space, if $A=(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $B=(x_2,y_2,z_2)$, then

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=(x_2-x_1,\;y_2-y_1,\;z_2-z_1)
$$

The three numbers are the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-components of the <Term t="math.vector">vector</Term> $\overrightarrow{AB}$. A vector only records **size and direction**, not where it sits: two vectors are equal exactly when all three components are equal. The vector $\overrightarrow{OP}$ starting at the origin $O$ is the **position vector** <Zh>位置向量</Zh> of the point $P$, and its components are the coordinates of $P$.

<Perspective title="Split a vector into three moves along the axes">

$\overrightarrow{AB}=(3,-2,2)$ can also be read as a route: start at $A=(1,2,3)$, move $3$ in the $x$-direction to $(4,2,3)$, then $-2$ in the $y$-direction (that is, $2$ the negative way) to $(4,0,3)$, and finally $2$ in the $z$-direction, which lands exactly on $B=(4,0,5)$. The three components are three moves, and taking them in any order reaches the same point, so "end minus start" really asks "how far did we go in each direction?".

The length also follows from this route by using Pythagoras twice: the first two moves lie in the horizontal plane $z=3$, and together they make a horizontal displacement of length $\sqrt{3^2+2^2}=\sqrt{13}$; that is perpendicular to the vertical third move (length $2$), so $|\overrightarrow{AB}|=\sqrt{13+2^2}=\sqrt{17}$, the same as at the start.

<Space3D desc="The blue arrow from A(1, 2, 3) to B(4, 0, 5) is the vector AB=(3, −2, 2). The three orange segments are the same displacement made along the three axes: 3 in the x-direction to (4, 2, 3), −2 in the y-direction to (4, 0, 3), then 2 in the z-direction to B. The dashed segment is the horizontal displacement of the first two moves together, of length √13; it is perpendicular to the vertical move of length 2, so AB has length √(13+4)=√17." range={5}

Addition, subtraction and scalar multiples all work **component by component**, just as for plane vectors, with a third component added:

| | Plane | Space |
|---|---|---|
| Vector | $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2)$ | $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ |
| Sum / difference | $\vec{a}\pm\vec{b}=(a_1\pm b_1,\,a_2\pm b_2)$ | $\vec{a}\pm\vec{b}=(a_1\pm b_1,\,a_2\pm b_2,\,a_3\pm b_3)$ |
| Scalar multiple | $t\vec{a}=(ta_1,\,ta_2)$ | $t\vec{a}=(ta_1,\,ta_2,\,ta_3)$ |
| Length | $\lvert\vec{a}\rvert=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}$ | $\lvert\vec{a}\rvert=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}$ |

A scalar multiple $t\vec{a}$ <Zh>係數積</Zh> means the same as in the plane: for $t>0$ it points the same way as $\vec{a}$, for $t<0$ the opposite way, and its length is $|t|$ times as long.

**Example.** Let $\vec{a}=(1,-2,2)$ and $\vec{b}=(3,0,-1)$. Find $2\vec{a}-\vec{b}$.

$$
2\vec{a}-\vec{b}=(2,-4,4)-(3,0,-1)=(-1,-4,5)
$$

<Perspective title="Draw it with arrows, head to tail">

The same calculation can be drawn with arrows: $2\vec{a}=(2,-4,4)$ stretches $\vec{a}$ to twice its length, and $-\vec{b}=(-3,0,1)$ turns $\vec{b}$ around. From the origin, draw $2\vec{a}$ first, reaching $(2,-4,4)$; from there, carry on with $-\vec{b}$, reaching $(2-3,\,-4+0,\,4+1)=(-1,-4,5)$. The arrow from the origin straight to this end point is $2\vec{a}-\vec{b}$. Adding components is exactly a record of the total displacement of the two arrows placed head to tail.

<Space3D desc="The red arrow from the origin is 2a=(2, −4, 4); the orange arrow starting at its tip is −b=(−3, 0, 1), ending at (−1, −4, 5); the blue arrow goes from the origin straight to (−1, −4, 5) and is 2a−b. The three arrows form a triangle." range={5}
  vectors={[{ tip: [2,-4,4], label: '2a', color: 'red' }, { tail: [2,-4,4], tip: [-1,-4,5], label: '−b', color: 'amber' }, { tip: [-1,-4,5], label: '2a−b', color: 'blue' }]} />

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Points and vectors are both written as three numbers in brackets. How do I tell them apart?">

Ask what it stands for. The point $P=(1,2,3)$ is a **position**; the vector $(1,2,3)$ is a **displacement**: move $1$ in the $x$-direction, $2$ in the $y$-direction and $3$ in the $z$-direction, starting from anywhere. The position vector links the two: starting at the origin and making this displacement lands exactly on the point $(1,2,3)$. So "coordinates of $B$ = coordinates of $A$ + $\overrightarrow{AB}$", which is "end minus start" written the other way round.

</StudentAsks>

## Linear combinations

Multiplying several vectors by numbers and adding them, as in $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}$, gives a <Term t="math.linear-combination">linear combination</Term> of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. To decide whether a vector is a linear combination, and with which coefficients, write it in components and compare them one by one.

**Example.** Let $\vec{a}=(1,2,0)$ and $\vec{b}=(0,1,3)$. Write $\vec{c}=(2,7,9)$ as $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}$.

$x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}=(x,\,2x+y,\,3y)$. The first and third components give $x=2$ and $y=3$; then check the second: $2\times 2+3=7$, which works. So $\vec{c}=2\vec{a}+3\vec{b}$.

<Space3D desc="From the origin, the red arrow is 2a=(2, 4, 0) and the orange arrow is 3b=(0, 3, 9). Completing the parallelogram on these two sides (the dashed segments are the other two sides), the diagonal is the blue arrow c=(2, 7, 9), that is c=2a+3b. The shaded parallelogram lies in the plane formed by the end points of all linear combinations of a and b." range={9}
  planes={[{ corners: [[0,0,0],[2,4,0],[2,7,9],[0,3,9]], color: 'blue' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [2,4,0], to: [2,7,9], dashed: true }, { from: [0,3,9], to: [2,7,9], dashed: true }]}
  vectors={[{ tip: [2,4,0], label: '2a', color: 'red' }, { tip: [0,3,9], label: '3b', color: 'amber' }, { tip: [2,7,9], label: 'c', color: 'blue' }]} />

Space adds one thing that the plane doesn't have: the two coefficients must satisfy **three** equations at once. If the check fails, $\vec{c}$ **cannot** be written as a linear combination of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. In space, if you draw every linear combination of two non-parallel vectors from the origin, the end points all lie in one plane; to reach every vector in space you need three vectors that are not coplanar.

<Perspective title="Draw the three equations as three lines">

Written out, $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}=\vec{c}$ is three equations: $x=2$, $2x+y=7$ and $3y=9$. Treat the unknown coefficients $(x,y)$ as a point in the coordinate plane; then each equation is a line, and the $(x,y)$ satisfying all three equations is the **common point** of the three lines. The first two lines meet at $(2,3)$, and the third line, $y=3$, happens to pass through that point too, so there is a solution, and only one.

Change the third component of $\vec{c}$ to $k$, and the third line becomes $y=\frac{k}{3}$: only for $k=9$ do the three lines meet in one point; for $k\ne 9$ the lines cross in pairs but share no common point, and $\vec{c}=(2,7,k)$ **cannot** be written as a linear combination of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. Two lines in the plane usually meet in one point, and a third line usually misses it; that is the picture behind "in space there is one more equation to check".

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Decide at a glance with a plane equation" needs="equations of planes in space and normal vectors (later chapter: Planes and lines in space)" href="/en/math/s116a-planes-lines/">

Call the end point of the linear combination $(x,\,2x+y,\,3y)$ of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ the point $(X,Y,Z)$. Then $X=x$ and $Z=3y$, so $Y=2X+\frac{Z}{3}$, that is

$$
6X-3Y+Z=0
$$

This is the plane through the origin with normal vector $(6,-3,1)$, and $(6,-3,1)$ is perpendicular to both $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$: $6-6+0=0$ and $0-3+3=0$. To decide, just substitute: $\vec{c}=(2,7,9)$ gives $12-21+9=0$, so it is in the plane and is a linear combination; $(2,7,8)$ gives $12-21+8=-1\ne 0$, so it is not in the plane and is not a linear combination. This turns "solve three equations" into "substitute into one"; but to find the coefficients $x$ and $y$ you still go back to the components.

</Perspective>

### Dividing a segment, and the centroid

Linear combinations are used most with position vectors. If $P$ lies on segment $\overline{AB}$ with $\overline{AP}:\overline{PB}=m:n$, then $\overrightarrow{AP}=\frac{m}{m+n}\overrightarrow{AB}$, which rearranges to the section formula <Zh>分點公式</Zh>:

$$
\overrightarrow{OP}=\frac{n}{m+n}\overrightarrow{OA}+\frac{m}{m+n}\overrightarrow{OB}
$$

Notice the weights are **crossed**: for a point near $A$, the coefficient of $\overrightarrow{OA}$ is the larger one, and the two coefficients add up to $1$. With $m=n$ you get the midpoint $\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB})$. The centroid <Zh>重心</Zh> $G$ of triangle $ABC$ is $\overrightarrow{OG}=\frac{1}{3}(\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{OC})$.

Drag m/(m+n), call it t: the point P=(1−t)A+tB lies on AB with AP:PB=m:n; the orange segment is AP, the blue segment is PB, and the red arrow is the position vector OP. It starts at t=0.25 (for example m:n=1:3), where P=(−2, 0) is nearer A, and the coefficient 3/4 of OA is larger than the coefficient 1/4 of OB; at t=0.5, P is the midpoint (0, 1)." x={[-5, 5]} y={[-2, 4]}
  params={[{ name: 't', min: 0, max: 1, step: 0.05, value: 0.25, label: 'm/(m+n)' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [-4, -1], to: ['-4+8*t', '-1+4*t'], color: 'amber' }, { from: ['-4+8*t', '-1+4*t'], to: [4, 3], color: 'blue' }]}
  vectors={[{ tip: ['-4+8*t', '-1+4*t'], label: 'OP', color: 'red' }]}
  points={[{ at: [-4, -1], label: 'A' }, { at: [4, 3], label: 'B' }, { at: ['-4+8*t', '-1+4*t'], label: 'P' }]} />

<Perspective title="Physical intuition: the coefficients are weights hung at the ends">

Put a very light rod between $A$ and $B$, hang a weight $n$ at $A$ and a weight $m$ at $B$. For the lever to balance, "weight × lever arm" must be equal on the two sides of the pivot $P$: $n\cdot\overline{AP}=m\cdot\overline{PB}$, that is $\overline{AP}:\overline{PB}=m:n$. So the coefficient $\frac{n}{m+n}$ of $\overrightarrow{OA}$ in the section formula is the share of the total weight hung at $A$: the balance point is nearer whichever end is heavier, and that is why the weights are "crossed". The centroid of a triangle works the same way: hang equal weights at the three vertices, and the balance point is the average of the three position vectors.

</Perspective>

**Example.** In space, $A=(2,0,1)$, $B=(0,4,-1)$ and $C=(1,-1,3)$. Find the centroid $G$ of $\triangle ABC$.

$$
G=\left(\frac{2+0+1}{3},\;\frac{0+4-1}{3},\;\frac{1-1+3}{3}\right)=(1,1,1)
$$

<Perspective title="The centroid is a trisection point of a median">

You can also just use the section formula twice. First find the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$, $M=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},1\right)$. The centroid lies on the median $\overline{AM}$ with $\overline{AG}:\overline{GM}=2:1$, so

$$
\overrightarrow{OG}=\frac{1}{3}\overrightarrow{OA}+\frac{2}{3}\overrightarrow{OM}=\left(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3},\;0+1,\;\frac{1}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\right)=(1,1,1)
$$

the same answer as the example. In general $\frac{1}{3}\overrightarrow{OA}+\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}(\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{OC})=\frac{1}{3}(\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{OC})$, so the two methods are really the same formula.

<Space3D desc="The vertices of △ABC are A(2, 0, 1), B(0, 4, −1) and C(1, −1, 3). M(1/2, 3/2, 1) is the midpoint of BC, and the dashed segment is the median AM; the centroid G(1, 1, 1) lies on AM with AG:GM=2:1, that is, two thirds of the way from A to M." range={4}

</Perspective>

## The length of a vector

By the distance formula for two points in space, the length of $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ is

$$
|\vec{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}
$$

and $|\overrightarrow{AB}|$ is the distance between the points $A$ and $B$. A scalar multiple has length $|t\vec{a}|=|t|\,|\vec{a}|$. Dividing a non-zero vector by its own length, $\frac{\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}|}$ is the unit vector <Zh>單位向量</Zh> of length $1$ pointing the same way as $\vec{a}$.

**Example.** Find the length of $\vec{a}=(2,-1,2)$ and the unit vector pointing the same way as $\vec{a}$.

$|\vec{a}|=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3$, so the unit vector is $\frac{1}{3}\vec{a}=\left(\frac{2}{3},-\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right)$.

<Perspective title="The length formula is Pythagoras used twice">

$\vec{a}=(2,-1,2)$ starts at the origin and ends at $P=(2,-1,2)$. First look at its shadow on the $xy$-plane, $(2,-1,0)$: from the origin to the shadow is the hypotenuse of a right triangle on the floor with legs $2$ and $1$, of length $\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt{5}$. Then go up $2$ from the shadow to $P$; this move is vertical, perpendicular to the floor, so $|\vec{a}|=\sqrt{(\sqrt{5})^2+2^2}=\sqrt{9}=3$. The square root of the sum of the three squared components is these two steps in one. The unit vector $\frac{1}{3}\vec{a}$ runs along the same arrow, taking only the first third of it from the origin.

<Space3D desc="The blue arrow is a=(2, −1, 2), ending at P(2, −1, 2). The orange path goes from the origin to P along the three axes: 2 in the x-direction, −1 in the y-direction, 2 in the z-direction. The dashed segment on the floor runs from the origin to the shadow (2, −1, 0) and has length √5; it is perpendicular to the vertical move of length 2, so |a|=√(5+4)=3. The short red arrow is the unit vector (2/3, −1/3, 2/3), lying along a with one third of its length." range={3}
  points={[{ at: [2,-1,2], label: 'P(2,−1,2)' }, { at: [2,-1,0], label: '(2,−1,0)' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [0,0,0], to: [2,0,0], color: 'amber' }, { from: [2,0,0], to: [2,-1,0], color: 'amber' }, { from: [2,-1,0], to: [2,-1,2], color: 'amber' }, { from: [0,0,0], to: [2,-1,0], dashed: true }]}
  vectors={[{ tip: [2,-1,2], color: 'blue' }, { tip: [2/3,-1/3,2/3], label: 'unit vector', color: 'red' }]} />

</Perspective>

Be careful: lengths **cannot** be added or subtracted the way components can. In general $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|\ne|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|$, and how the two compare is exactly the triangle inequality in the next section.

## The triangle inequality for vectors

Put $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ head to tail: go along $\vec{a}$ from $A$ to $B$, then along $\vec{b}$ from $B$ to $C$, so that $\overrightarrow{AC}=\vec{a}+\vec{b}$. Two sides of a triangle add up to more than the third, so

$$
|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|\le|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|
$$

This is the <Term t="math.triangle-inequality">triangle inequality</Term> for vectors. Equality holds exactly when $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ point the same way (or one of them is the zero vector): then $A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on one line with $B$ between $A$ and $C$, and the triangle has been "squashed flat".

Any side of a triangle is also longer than the difference of the other two, which gives the other half:

$$
\bigl||\vec{a}|-|\vec{b}|\bigr|\le|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|\le|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|
$$

The left-hand equality holds when $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ point opposite ways (or one of them is the zero vector). Replacing $\vec{b}$ with $-\vec{b}$ shows that the same range holds for $|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|$.

**Example.** $|\vec{a}|=3$ and $|\vec{b}|=5$. Find the range of $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|$.

$|5-3|\le|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|\le 3+5$, that is $2\le|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|\le 8$. It is $8$ when the vectors point the same way and $2$ when they point opposite ways; as the angle between them turns smoothly from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$, the length takes every value between $2$ and $8$.

<Graph desc="The orange arrow a has length 3 and runs from the origin along the x-axis to (3, 0); the blue arrow b has length 5, starts at the tip of a, and makes an angle θ with a; the red arrow is a+b. Drag θ (in degrees): the tip of b moves on the circle with centre (3, 0) and radius 5, and |a+b|=√(34+30cos θ). At θ=0° the vectors point the same way, a+b reaches (8, 0), and its length is 8; as θ grows, a+b gets shorter; at θ=180° they point opposite ways, a+b reaches (−2, 0), and its length is 2." x={[-3, 9]} y={[-6, 6]} height={340}
  params={[{ name: 'th', min: 0, max: 180, step: 1, value: 60, label: 'θ (degrees)' }]}
  circles={[{ center: [3, 0], r: 5, color: 'ink' }]}
  vectors={[{ tip: [3, 0], label: 'a', color: 'amber' }, { tail: [3, 0], tip: ['3+5*cos(th*pi/180)', '5*sin(th*pi/180)'], label: 'b', color: 'blue' }, { tip: ['3+5*cos(th*pi/180)', '5*sin(th*pi/180)'], label: 'a+b', color: 'red' }]} />

<Perspective title="Use the law of cosines to get the length at every angle">

Work out the third side of the triangle directly, and you can even see "every value from $2$ to $8$". Let the angle between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ be $\theta$. In the head-to-tail triangle, the interior angle at $B$ between the sides $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ is $180^\circ-\theta$, so by the law of cosines

$$
|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2=3^2+5^2-2\cdot 3\cdot 5\cos(180^\circ-\theta)=34+30\cos\theta
$$

As $\theta$ goes from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$, $\cos\theta$ decreases continuously from $1$ to $-1$, so $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2$ decreases continuously from $64$ to $4$, the length decreases from $8$ to $2$, and every value in between is reached. For example, at $\theta=90^\circ$, $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|=\sqrt{34}$, exactly the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $3$ and $5$; at $\theta=120^\circ$, $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2=34-15=19$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Where can the tip land? On a sphere">

Fix $\vec{a}=\overrightarrow{AB}$ and let $\vec{b}$, of length $5$, point in any direction in space. The tip $C$ of $\vec{a}+\vec{b}$ is always at distance $5$ from $B$, so $C$ lies on the sphere with centre $B$ and radius $5$; the starting point $A$ is at distance $3$ from the centre $B$, inside the sphere. The point of the sphere nearest $A$ is reached by going $5$ from the centre $B$ towards $A$, at distance $5-3=2$; the farthest point is in the opposite direction, at distance $5+3=8$. So $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|=\overline{AC}$ ranges from $2$ to $8$. The circle in the figure above is the cross-section of this sphere by the plane containing $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. If $|\vec{a}|>|\vec{b}|$, $A$ lies outside the sphere and the nearest distance becomes $|\vec{a}|-|\vec{b}|$, which is why the lower bound is written with an absolute value, $\bigl||\vec{a}|-|\vec{b}|\bigr|$.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can I prove this inequality by calculation instead of drawing a triangle?">

Yes, but it needs the dot product, and the Cauchy inequality that the next chapter reviews: expand $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2$ and use the Cauchy inequality to bound the cross term, which gives $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2\le(|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|)^2$. For this chapter, remember the geometric meaning: **a detour is never shorter than the straight path**.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Use the dot product: expand the squared length" needs="the dot product of space vectors and the Cauchy inequality (next chapter: Vector operations and 3×3 determinants)" href="/en/math/s116a-vector-operations/">

$$
|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2=(\vec{a}+\vec{b})\cdot(\vec{a}+\vec{b})=|\vec{a}|^2+2\,\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}+|\vec{b}|^2
$$

Since $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta$ and $-1\le\cos\theta\le 1$, we have $-|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\le\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}\le|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|$ (this is the Cauchy inequality). Substituting gives

$$
(|\vec{a}|-|\vec{b}|)^2\le|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2\le(|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|)^2
$$

and taking square roots gives the whole triangle inequality; the right-hand equality holds when $\cos\theta=1$ (same direction) and the left-hand one when $\cos\theta=-1$ (opposite directions). With $|\vec{a}|=3$ and $|\vec{b}|=5$ this again gives $|\vec{a}+\vec{b}|^2=34+30\cos\theta$, matching the law of cosines.

</Perspective>

### The triangle inequality for real numbers is a special case

A real number can be seen as a vector on the number line, and its <Term t="math.absolute-value">absolute value</Term> is its length. So the triangle inequality for vectors, on the number line, reads

$$
|a+b|\le|a|+|b|
$$

with equality exactly when $a$ and $b$ have the same sign or one of them is $0$, that is when $ab\ge 0$.

**Example.** Find the minimum of $f(x)=|x-1|+|x-5|$ and the values of $x$ where it occurs.

$f(x)=|x-1|+|5-x|\ge|(x-1)+(5-x)|=4$, with equality exactly when $(x-1)(5-x)\ge 0$, that is when $1\le x\le 5$. So the minimum is $4$, reached for $1\le x\le 5$. Distances make it even more direct: $f(x)$ is the distance from $x$ to $1$ plus the distance to $5$, which is smallest when $x$ stands between the two points, where it equals their distance $4$.

The graph is a broken line, sloping on both sides and flat in the middle, and its lowest part is a whole horizontal segment $y=4$, so the minimum $4$ is reached not at one point but on the whole interval $1\le x\le 5$. The graph also solves inequalities directly: $|x-1|+|x-5|\le 6$ is the part of the broken line not above the horizontal line $y=6$; $6-2x=6$ gives $x=0$ and $2x-6=6$ gives $x=6$, so the solution is $0\le x\le 6$.
