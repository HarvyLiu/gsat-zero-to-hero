## Vectors: a size and a direction

A <Term t="math.vector">vector</Term> is a quantity with both a size and a direction, drawn as a directed segment. The vector pointing from $A$ to $B$ is written $\overrightarrow{AB}$; $A$ is its start point <Zh>始點</Zh> and $B$ its end point <Zh>終點</Zh>.

On the coordinate plane, if $A(x_1,y_1)$ and $B(x_2,y_2)$, then

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=(x_2-x_1,\,y_2-y_1)
$$

that is, **end point minus start point**. The length $|\overrightarrow{AB}|$ of the vector is the distance between $A$ and $B$, found with the distance formula: the vector $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2)$ has length $|\vec{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}$.

Two vectors with the same size and the same direction are the same vector, wherever they are drawn, so a vector can be slid to any position. The vector $\overrightarrow{OP}$ that starts at the origin $O$ is the **position vector** <Zh>位置向量</Zh> of $P$: if $P(x,y)$, then $\overrightarrow{OP}=(x,y)$, with the same coordinates as the point. The curriculum's notes for Math A say this unit should work with position vectors as far as possible, with linear combinations as its main goal. The vector of length $0$ is the **zero vector** <Zh>零向量</Zh>, written $\vec{0}=(0,0)$.

**Example.** Given $A(-1,3)$ and $B(2,-1)$, find $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $|\overrightarrow{AB}|$.

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=(2-(-1),\,-1-3)=(3,-4),\qquad |\overrightarrow{AB}|=\sqrt{3^2+(-4)^2}=5
$$

Note that $\overrightarrow{BA}=(-3,4)$: the same length, the opposite direction.

<Perspective title="By sliding: move the start point to the origin">

Slide the whole picture so that $A$ moves to the origin: subtract $(-1,3)$ from every point's coordinates. $A$ becomes $(0,0)$ and $B$ becomes $(2-(-1),\,-1-3)=(3,-4)$. "End point minus start point" really means "move the start point to the origin and see where the end point lands". The length does not change either: the distance from the origin to $(3,-4)$ is $\sqrt{9+16}=5$.

## Adding, subtracting and scaling

Adding, subtracting and scaling vectors are all done **coordinate by coordinate**. With $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2)$, $\vec{b}=(b_1,b_2)$ and a real number $r$:

$$
\vec{a}\pm\vec{b}=(a_1\pm b_1,\,a_2\pm b_2),\qquad r\vec{a}=(ra_1,\,ra_2)
$$

In pictures:

- **Adding**: put the start of $\vec{b}$ at the end of $\vec{a}$; walking from the first start to the last end gives $\vec{a}+\vec{b}$, e.g. $\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{AC}$. When two vectors are drawn as two sides of a parallelogram, their sum is the diagonal from the shared start point.
- **Scaling**: $r\vec{a}$ has length $|r|$ times $|\vec{a}|$; it points the same way as $\vec{a}$ when $r>0$ and the opposite way when $r<0$.
- **Subtracting**: $\vec{a}-\vec{b}=\vec{a}+(-1)\vec{b}$.

**Example.** $\vec{a}=(2,-1)$ and $\vec{b}=(-1,3)$. Find $3\vec{a}-2\vec{b}$.

$$
3\vec{a}-2\vec{b}=(6,-3)-(-2,6)=(8,-9)
$$

<Perspective title="By drawing: walk it nose to tail">

Instead of writing coordinates, you can "walk" along the picture. $3\vec{a}=(6,-3)$: from the origin go $6$ right and $3$ down, to $(6,-3)$. $-2\vec{b}=(2,-6)$: from $(6,-3)$ go another $2$ right and $6$ down, to $(8,-9)$. From the origin to where you stop is $3\vec{a}-2\vec{b}=(8,-9)$.

This also explains why the coordinates can be worked out separately: the "how far right" of each leg adds up to how far right you went in total, and the "how far up" adds up to how far up you went, and the two directions never interfere.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Why is the vector from start to end equal to the difference of two position vectors?">

To get from $O$ to $B$ you can go to $A$ first and then on to $B$: $\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{OB}$. Moving a term across gives $\overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{OB}-\overrightarrow{OA}$. A position vector has the same coordinates as its point, so this is exactly "end point minus start point".

</StudentAsks>

### Parallel vectors

Two non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are parallel (pointing the same way or opposite ways) exactly when one is a real multiple of the other: $\vec{b}=t\vec{a}$. In coordinates, **the matching coordinates are in proportion**; when a denominator could be $0$, check by cross-multiplying instead: $a_1b_2=a_2b_1$.

**Example.** $(k,6)$ is parallel to $(2,3)$. Find $k$.

The coordinates are in proportion: $\frac{k}{2}=\frac{6}{3}$, so $k=4$. Check: $(4,6)=2(2,3)$.

<Perspective title="By slope: parallel means lying on the same line through the origin">

Put both vectors' start points at the origin. Parallel vectors point the same way or opposite ways, so their end points lie on the same line through the origin, which means the **slopes from the origin are equal**. The slope of $(2,3)$ is $\frac{3}{2}$ and the slope of $(k,6)$ is $\frac{6}{k}$; setting $\frac{6}{k}=\frac{3}{2}$ gives $k=4$.

A vertical vector (first coordinate $0$) has no slope and needs separate treatment; that is exactly why cross-multiplying, $a_1b_2=a_2b_1$, is safer: $3k=2\times 6$ also gives $k=4$, with no worry about a denominator of $0$.

<Perspective later title="By a 2×2 determinant: parallel vectors enclose no area" needs="2×2 determinants and parallelogram areas (Math A, “Vector operations and 3×3 determinants”)" href="/en/math/s116a-vector-operations/">

Later in Math A you will learn that the parallelogram spanned by $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2)$ and $\vec{b}=(b_1,b_2)$ has area equal to the absolute value of the 2×2 determinant, $|a_1b_2-a_2b_1|$. When the two vectors are parallel they lie along one line and enclose no area, so the determinant is $0$; the converse also holds. Here: $\begin{vmatrix}k&6\\2&3\end{vmatrix}=3k-12=0$, so $k=4$. It is the same equation as cross-multiplying, now with the meaning "area".

</Perspective>

## Linear combinations: building every vector from two

A vector of the form $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}$ ($x$, $y$ real) is a <Term t="math.linear-combination">linear combination</Term> of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are **non-zero and not parallel**, every vector in the plane can be written as $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}$, and in only one way. Finding $x$ and $y$ means solving a pair of linear equations in two unknowns.

**Example.** $\vec{a}=(1,2)$ and $\vec{b}=(3,-1)$. Write $\vec{c}=(5,3)$ as $x\vec{a}+y\vec{b}$.

$$
x(1,2)+y(3,-1)=(x+3y,\,2x-y)=(5,3)
$$

Solving $x+3y=5$ and $2x-y=3$ gives $x=2$ and $y=1$. Check: $2(1,2)+(3,-1)=(5,3)$.

<Perspective title="By drawing: parallel lines along the two directions">

Instead of solving equations, you can find the coefficients with a parallelogram. Treat the two lines through the origin along $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ as "slanted axes". From $C(5,3)$ draw a line parallel to $\vec{b}$; it meets the line of $\vec{a}$ at $(2,4)$, and $(2,4)=2\vec{a}$, so $x=2$. The leftover piece is $(5,3)-(2,4)=(3,-1)=\vec{b}$, so $y=1$.

In other words, $x\vec{a}$ and $y\vec{b}$ are the two sides of the parallelogram whose diagonal is $\overrightarrow{OC}$. Because $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are not parallel, these parallel lines always meet, and meet only once; that is the picture behind "in only one way".

### Dividing a segment: points on a segment are linear combinations too

If $P$ lies on the segment $\overline{AB}$ with $\overline{AP}:\overline{PB}=m:n$, then

$$
\overrightarrow{OP}=\frac{n}{m+n}\overrightarrow{OA}+\frac{m}{m+n}\overrightarrow{OB}
$$

To remember it: **the coefficients cross over**. A point nearer $B$ gives $\overrightarrow{OB}$ the larger coefficient, and the two coefficients add up to $1$. The midpoint is the case $m=n$: $\overrightarrow{OM}=\frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{OA}+\frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{OB}$.

**Example.** $A(1,1)$ and $B(7,4)$; $P$ is on $\overline{AB}$ with $\overline{AP}:\overline{PB}=2:1$. Find the coordinates of $P$.

$$
\overrightarrow{OP}=\frac{1}{3}(1,1)+\frac{2}{3}(7,4)=\left(\frac{1+14}{3},\,\frac{1+8}{3}\right)=(5,3)
$$

So $P(5,3)$. Another way to see it: $\overrightarrow{AB}=(6,3)$, and walking $\frac{2}{3}\overrightarrow{AB}=(4,2)$ from $A$ lands on $(5,3)$.

<Graph desc="Drag the sliders m and n. The segment AB runs from A(1, 1) to B(7, 4), and the point P divides it so that AP:PB=m:n; P has coordinates ((n+7m)/(m+n), (n+4m)/(m+n)). When m is bigger than n, P is nearer B; when n is bigger than m, P is nearer A; when m=n, P is the midpoint (4, 2.5). By default m=2 and n=1, and P is (5, 3), as in the example." x={[-1, 9]} y={[-1, 6]}
  params={[{ name: 'm', min: 1, max: 5, step: 1, value: 2 }, { name: 'n', min: 1, max: 5, step: 1, value: 1 }]}
  segments={[{ from: [1, 1], to: [7, 4], color: 'blue' }]}
  points={[{ at: [1, 1], label: 'A' }, { at: [7, 4], label: 'B' }, { at: ['(n+7*m)/(m+n)', '(n+4*m)/(m+n)'], label: 'P', color: 'amber' }]} />

<Perspective title="By a lever: P is the balance point of weights at the ends">

Think of the segment as a very light rod with weights hung at both ends. The balance point sits **nearer the heavier end**, and its distances to the two ends are in the inverse ratio of the weights. For $\overline{AP}:\overline{PB}=m:n$, hang weight $n$ at $A$ and weight $m$ at $B$; the balance point is the weighted average

$$
\frac{n\cdot A+m\cdot B}{m+n}
$$

This is exactly the section formula, and "the coefficients cross over" now has a reason: a large coefficient $m$ on $B$ means the $B$ end is heavy, so $P$ is pulled towards $B$. In the example, hang $1$ at $A(1,1)$ and $2$ at $B(7,4)$: $\left(\frac{1\cdot 1+2\cdot 7}{3},\,\frac{1\cdot 1+2\cdot 4}{3}\right)=(5,3)$. Check: $\overline{AP}=|(4,2)|=2\sqrt{5}$ and $\overline{PB}=|(2,1)|=\sqrt{5}$, a ratio of $2:1$.

</Perspective>

## The inner product: turning two vectors into a number

When two non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ start from the same point, the angle $\theta$ they make ($0^\circ\le\theta\le 180^\circ$) is called the angle between them. The <Term t="math.inner-product">inner product</Term> is defined as

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

In coordinates it has a form that is easy to calculate:

$$
(a_1,a_2)\cdot(b_1,b_2)=a_1b_1+a_2b_2
$$

Why are the two equal? Put the starts of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ at the origin, with ends $A$ and $B$; then $\overrightarrow{AB}=\vec{b}-\vec{a}$. Apply the <Term t="math.law-of-cosines">law of cosines</Term> from the chapter on the laws of sines and cosines to $\triangle OAB$:

$$
|\vec{b}-\vec{a}|^2=|\vec{a}|^2+|\vec{b}|^2-2|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta
$$

In coordinates the left side expands to $(b_1-a_1)^2+(b_2-a_2)^2=|\vec{a}|^2+|\vec{b}|^2-2(a_1b_1+a_2b_2)$. Comparing the two sides gives $a_1b_1+a_2b_2=|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta$.

<Graph desc="Drag the sliders p and q. The orange arrow is the fixed a=(3, 1), and the dashed line is the line through the origin that carries a; the blue arrow is b=(p, q). A second dashed segment drops a perpendicular from the end of b to that line, with foot F=((3p+q)/10)(3, 1), and the green arrow from the origin to F is the shadow of b in the direction of a. The inner product a·b=3p+q equals |a| times the signed shadow length: when the shadow points the same way as a the inner product is positive (an acute angle), when it points the opposite way it is negative (an obtuse angle), and when the shadow shrinks to the origin the inner product is 0 and the vectors are perpendicular, for example b=(−1, 3).   points={[{ at: ['3*(3*p+q)/10', '(3*p+q)/10'], label: 'F', color: 'green' }]} />

<Perspective title="By shadows: inner product = length × signed shadow length">

$|\vec{b}|\cos\theta$ is exactly the length of the shadow that $\vec{b}$ casts in the direction of $\vec{a}$ (for an obtuse angle $\cos\theta<0$, the shadow falls the opposite way, and its length counts as negative). So

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=|\vec{a}|\times(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta)=|\vec{a}|\times\text{(signed shadow length)}
$$

Check it with the figure's default values: $\vec{a}=(3,1)$ and $\vec{b}=(1,2)$, and the foot is $F(1.5,0.5)$ (check: $(1,2)-(1.5,0.5)=(-0.5,1.5)$ has inner product $-1.5+1.5=0$ with $(3,1)$, so it really is perpendicular). The shadow length is $|\overrightarrow{OF}|=\sqrt{1.5^2+0.5^2}=\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}$, and multiplying by $|\vec{a}|=\sqrt{10}$ gives $5$, the same as the coordinate calculation $3\times 1+1\times 2=5$.

This view makes the sign obvious: a shadow pointing the same way as $\vec{a}$ means positive, the opposite way means negative, and a shadow shrunk to a point means $0$, that is, perpendicular.

</Perspective>

A few properties you will use straight away:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{a}=\lvert\vec{a}\rvert^2$ | a vector makes an angle of $0^\circ$ with itself, and $\cos 0^\circ=1$ |
| $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=0$ | the two non-zero vectors are **perpendicular** ($\cos 90^\circ=0$) |
| $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}>0$ | the angle satisfies $0^\circ\le\theta<90^\circ$ (acute, or the same direction) |
| $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}<0$ | the angle satisfies $90^\circ<\theta\le 180^\circ$ (obtuse, or opposite directions) |
| $\cos\theta=\dfrac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{\lvert\vec{a}\rvert\lvert\vec{b}\rvert}$ | finding the angle from coordinates |

**Example (angle).** Find the angle between $\vec{a}=(3,1)$ and $\vec{b}=(1,2)$.

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=3+2=5,\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{5}{\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt{5}}=\frac{5}{5\sqrt{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

So $\theta=45^\circ$.

<Perspective title="By the triangle: the three sides show 45° directly">

Call the ends of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ $A(3,1)$ and $B(1,2)$, and look at the three sides of $\triangle OAB$:

$$
\overline{OA}=\sqrt{10},\qquad \overline{OB}=\sqrt{5},\qquad \overline{AB}=|(1-3,\,2-1)|=\sqrt{5}
$$

$\overline{OB}^2+\overline{AB}^2=5+5=10=\overline{OA}^2$, so $\angle OBA=90^\circ$; and $\overline{OB}=\overline{AB}$, so this is an isosceles right triangle, and the base angle is $\angle AOB=45^\circ$. Without the inner product you get the same answer. On the other hand, the inner product does not need a lucky special triangle: it works for any two vectors.

**Example (perpendicular).** $(2,k)$ is perpendicular to $(3,-6)$. Find $k$.

The inner product is $0$: $2\times 3+k\times(-6)=6-6k=0$, so $k=1$.

<Perspective title="By slope: perpendicular means the slopes multiply to −1">

Draw both vectors from the origin; they are perpendicular exactly when the lines they lie on are perpendicular. The slope of $(2,k)$ is $\frac{k}{2}$ and the slope of $(3,-6)$ is $-2$. As the chapter on equations of lines showed, two lines that both have slopes are perpendicular when the slopes multiply to $-1$: $\frac{k}{2}\times(-2)=-1$, so $k=1$.

This method fails for horizontal or vertical vectors (a vertical line has no slope); "inner product equals $0$" has no exceptions, so in the exam lead with the inner product and use slopes as a check.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Is the inner product a vector?">

No, the inner product is a **number**. $a_1b_1+a_2b_2$ is just a real number, and it can be positive, negative or $0$. So something like $(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{c}$ is "a number times the vector $\vec{c}$", and the result is parallel to $\vec{c}$. What gives you a vector is adding and subtracting vectors, or scaling one.

</StudentAsks>

## Orthogonal projection: the shadow of one vector on another's direction

In the chapter on the laws of sines and cosines, the <Term t="math.orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</Term> of a segment on a line had length $\ell\cos\theta$. With vectors: put the starts of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ at the same point and drop a perpendicular from the end of $\vec{a}$ to the line that $\vec{b}$ lies along; the vector from that shared start point to the foot of the perpendicular is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{a}$ on $\vec{b}$. It is parallel to $\vec{b}$, and

$$
\text{projection of }\vec{a}\text{ on }\vec{b}=\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}\,\vec{b}
$$

Its length is $\dfrac{|\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}|}{|\vec{b}|}$, that is, $|\vec{a}||\cos\theta|$. When $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}<0$ (an angle greater than $90^\circ$), the projection points the opposite way to $\vec{b}$.

**Example.** Find the orthogonal projection of $\vec{a}=(2,11)$ on $\vec{b}=(3,4)$.

$$
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}=6+44=50,\qquad |\vec{b}|^2=25,\qquad \frac{50}{25}(3,4)=(6,8)
$$

The projection is $(6,8)$, of length $10$. What is left over, $\vec{a}-(6,8)=(-4,3)$, has inner product $-12+12=0$ with $\vec{b}$, so it is perpendicular to $\vec{b}$. So $\vec{a}$ has been split into a part parallel to $\vec{b}$ and a part perpendicular to $\vec{b}$.

<Perspective title="By splitting: the formula grows by itself">

You can do this without memorising the formula. The projection is parallel to $\vec{b}$, so call it $t\vec{b}$; the leftover part $\vec{a}-t\vec{b}$ must be perpendicular to $\vec{b}$, so its inner product with $\vec{b}$ is $0$:

$$
(\vec{a}-t\vec{b})\cdot\vec{b}=\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}-t|\vec{b}|^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad t=\frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}
$$

That is the coefficient in the projection formula. For this example, substitute the coordinates directly: $(2-3t,\,11-4t)\cdot(3,4)=6-9t+44-16t=50-25t=0$, so $t=2$ and the projection is $2(3,4)=(6,8)$. The slider in the figure above does the same thing: it looks for a point $s\vec{b}$ on the line where the joining segment is perpendicular to the line, and that point is also the one nearest the end of $\vec{a}$.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="By lines: the projection ends at the foot of the perpendicular">

The end of the projection is the foot of the perpendicular from the point $(2,11)$ to the line $y=\frac{4}{3}x$ (that is, $4x-3y=0$), which you can find with the methods of the chapter on equations of lines. The perpendicular through $(2,11)$ has slope $-\frac{3}{4}$: $y-11=-\frac{3}{4}(x-2)$. Solve it together with $y=\frac{4}{3}x$, multiplying both sides by $12$:

$$
16x-132=-9x+18\quad\Rightarrow\quad 25x=150,\quad x=6,\ y=8
$$

The foot is $(6,8)$, matching the formula. The point-to-line distance formula checks the length of the perpendicular part: $\dfrac{|4\times 2-3\times 11|}{\sqrt{4^2+3^2}}=\dfrac{25}{5}=5=|(-4,3)|$.

</Perspective>

### How far Math A and Math B go

The inner product, orthogonal projection, the angle between vectors, and the tests for parallel and perpendicular vectors are learned in both Math A <Zh>數A</Zh> and Math B <Zh>數B</Zh>. After this, Math A goes on to use vectors for areas and to learn 2×2 determinants <Zh>二階行列式</Zh> and the Cauchy inequality <Zh>柯西不等式</Zh>; the curriculum notes that Math B does not include areas and determinants, or the Cauchy inequality. Those Math A extensions are left to the Math A chapter "Vector operations and 3×3 determinants".
