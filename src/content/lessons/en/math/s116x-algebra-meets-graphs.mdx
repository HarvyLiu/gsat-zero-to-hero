## How to read this chapter

Each of the five problems below goes with one earlier unit: absolute value, the maximum and minimum of a quadratic, lines and circles, polynomial inequalities, and the symmetry of a quadratic. For each one the problem comes first, then the different solutions one after another, side by side, and finally a short note on which to use when. Try one method of your own first, then compare it with the others and see whether one of them is quicker than yours.

## Problem 1: solve $|2x-5|<3$

#### Way 1: distance (number line)

Take out the coefficient of $x$ so that it looks like a distance:

$$
|2x-5|=2\left|x-\tfrac{5}{2}\right|<3 \iff \left|x-\tfrac{5}{2}\right|<\tfrac{3}{2}
$$

So $x$ is closer than $\frac{3}{2}$ to $\frac{5}{2}$: $\frac{5}{2}-\frac{3}{2}<x<\frac{5}{2}+\frac{3}{2}$, that is, $1<x<4$.

<NumberLine desc="Solutions of |2x−5|<3: centre 5/2, reaching 3/2 to each side. The shaded part runs from 1 to 4, with open circles at both ends (endpoints not included)." from={-1} to={6} step={0.5} labelEvery={2}
  intervals={[{ a: 1, b: 4, open: [true, true] }]}
  points={[{ x: 2.5, label: '5/2' }]} />

<Perspective title="Way 2: sandwich (algebra)">

An <Term t="math.absolute-value">absolute value</Term> less than $3$ means the inside is squeezed between $-3$ and $3$:

$$
-3<2x-5<3 \iff 2<2x<8 \iff 1<x<4
$$

Add $5$ to all three parts, then divide all three by $2$ (positive, so the inequalities keep their direction). No need to take out the coefficient first; just keep calculating.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Way 3: graph (a V and a horizontal line)">

Draw $y=|2x-5|$: its tip is at $\left(\frac{5}{2},0\right)$ and its two sides have slopes $\pm 2$. Then draw the horizontal line $y=3$. Solving $|2x-5|<3$ means finding the $x$ where the V is **below** the line.

Setting $2x-5=3$ and $2x-5=-3$ gives the crossing points at $x=4$ and $x=1$. Between them the V is lower than the line, so $1<x<4$.

<Graph desc="The V-shaped graph y=|2x−5| has its tip at (5/2, 0); the red dashed line is the horizontal line y=k. Drag k: the two meet at x=(5−k)/2 and x=(5+k)/2, and the red segment on the x-axis marks where the V is below the line, which is the solution of |2x−5|<k. By default k=3, the crossings are (1, 3) and (4, 3), and the solution is 1<x<4." x={[-1, 6]} y={[-1, 7]} height={280}
  params={[{ name: 'k', min: 0.5, max: 5, step: 0.5, value: 3 }]}
  functions={[{ expr: 'abs(2*x-5)', label: 'y=|2x−5|' }, { expr: 'k', color: 'red', dashed: true }]}
  segments={[{ from: ['(5-k)/2', 0], to: ['(5+k)/2', 0], color: 'red' }]}
  points={[{ at: ['(5-k)/2', 'k'], color: 'red' }, { at: ['(5+k)/2', 'k'], color: 'red' }, { at: [2.5, 0], label: '(5/2, 0)' }]} />

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Way 4: square both sides (a quadratic inequality)">

Neither side is negative, so $|2x-5|<3 \iff (2x-5)^2<9$:

$$
4x^2-20x+25<9 \iff 4x^2-20x+16<0 \iff (x-1)(x-4)<0
$$

The product is negative between the roots $1$ and $4$, so $1<x<4$. This step turns the absolute-value inequality into a polynomial inequality like the one in Problem 4.

</Perspective>

**Which to use when** (the curriculum's absolute-value inequalities are, as a rule, of the form $|x-a|<b$ and $|x-a|>b$; the last two below are only extension practice for comparing methods):

- An ordinary $|ax+b|<c$ or $|ax+b|>c$: the sandwich or the distance view is quickest, and the distance view also shows at a glance whether the answer is "squeezed in the middle" or "out on both sides".
- When the right-hand side is not a constant, such as $|x-1|<x$: drawing the graph (compare the V with the line $y=x$) is least likely to miss a case. The answer is $x>\frac{1}{2}$.
- When both sides have absolute values, such as $|x-1|<|x+3|$: square both sides. The $x^2$ terms cancel, leaving a linear inequality, and the answer is $x>-1$.

## Problem 2: the largest fenced area against a wall

**Problem**　You have $20$ m of fencing to make a rectangle against a very long wall (the side along the wall needs no fence). Let the side perpendicular to the wall be $x$ m. What is the largest possible area?

The side parallel to the wall is $20-2x$, so the area is $A=x(20-2x)$, with $0<x<10$.

#### Way 1: completing the square

<Term t="math.completing-the-square">Completing the square</Term>:

$$
A=-2x^2+20x=-2(x^2-10x)=-2(x-5)^2+50
$$

Since $-2(x-5)^2\le 0$, $A\le 50$, with equality at $x=5$ (and $x=5$ is inside $0<x<10$). The largest area is $50$ square metres, from a $5\times 10$ rectangle.

<Perspective title="Way 2: graph (the vertex is halfway between the roots)">

No need to expand. $A=x(20-2x)$ is $0$ at $x=0$ and $x=10$, where the parabola meets the $x$-axis. The graph of a <Term t="math.quadratic-function">quadratic function</Term> is symmetric, with its axis exactly halfway between the roots: $x=\frac{0+10}{2}=5$. The coefficient of $x^2$ is negative, so the parabola opens downward and the vertex is the highest point:

$$
A(5)=5\times(20-10)=50
$$

All this method needs is the two values of $x$ that make the expression $0$, so it is especially quick when the problem already comes as a product.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Way 3: the AM-GM inequality">

$2x$ and $20-2x$ are both positive and **their sum is fixed at $20$**. By the <Term t="math.am-gm-inequality">AM-GM inequality</Term>:

$$
\sqrt{2x(20-2x)}\le\frac{2x+(20-2x)}{2}=10 \iff 2x(20-2x)\le 100 \iff A\le 50
$$

Equality holds when $2x=20-2x$, that is, at $x=5$. The equality case can happen, so the maximum is $50$. Note that you must pair $2x$ with $20-2x$ (a constant sum), not $x$ with $20-2x$ (a sum that changes with $x$).

</Perspective>

### A variation: the wall is only $8$ m long

If the wall is only $8$ m long, the side parallel to it cannot exceed $8$: $20-2x\le 8$, so $x\ge 6$ and $6\le x<10$. Now the three methods behave differently:

- **AM-GM** still says $A\le 50$, but equality needs $x=5$, which is not allowed, so $50$ **cannot be reached**. AM-GM gives only an upper bound; it does not tell you the actual maximum.
- **Completing the square or the graph**: $A=-2(x-5)^2+50$ decreases to the right of $x=5$, so on the allowed range the maximum is at the left end, $x=6$: $A=6\times 8=48$.

The curriculum lists "quadratic functions in context on a closed interval" <Zh>閉區間內的二次函數情境應用</Zh> under linear and quadratic functions; this problem practises the same idea: when the vertex is outside the allowed range, the maximum or minimum happens at an end of the range.

<Graph desc="The parabola of the area A=x(20−2x) for x from 0 to 10, with vertex (5, 50). Drag the wall length L: the side 20−2x parallel to the wall cannot exceed L, so x must be at least 10−L/2. The red segment on the x-axis marks the allowed x, and the dashed line marks its left end. For L≥10 the vertex is allowed and the largest area is 50; for L<10 the vertex is excluded and the maximum moves to the left end x=10−L/2. With L=8 the left end is x=6 and the largest area is 48." x={[-1, 11]} y={[-5, 60]} height={320}
  params={[{ name: 'L', label: 'wall length L', min: 4, max: 20, step: 1, value: 8 }]}

  segments={[{ from: ['10-L/2', 0], to: [10, 0], color: 'red' }, { from: ['10-L/2', 0], to: ['10-L/2', '(10-L/2)*(20-2*(10-L/2))'], dashed: true }]}
  points={[{ at: [5, 50], label: '(5, 50)' }, { at: ['(15-L/2+abs(5-L/2))/2', '((15-L/2+abs(5-L/2))/2)*(20-(15-L/2+abs(5-L/2)))'], label: 'max', color: 'red' }]} />

<StudentAsks q="AM-GM proved that A≤50, so why isn't the answer 50?">

$A\le 50$ is true, but it only says the area "never exceeds $50$"; it does not say $50$ can actually be achieved. Reaching $50$ requires $x=5$, and with a short wall $x=5$ is not allowed. So whenever you use AM-GM for a maximum or minimum, always check **whether the equality case lies inside the allowed range**. If it doesn't, complete the square or look at the graph and check the ends of the range.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Finding the highest point with a derivative" needs="derivatives (分科; not on the 學測)">

$A(x)=20x-2x^2$ and $A'(x)=20-4x$. $A'(x)=0$ gives $x=5$; $A$ increases for $x<5$ and decreases for $x>5$. On the range $6\le x<10$, $A$ is decreasing throughout, so the maximum is at $x=6$, namely $48$. Same conclusion as completing the square; the GSAT does not need this tool.

</Perspective>

**Which to use when:** completing the square always works and handles a restricted range directly; when the expression is already a product, the midpoint of the roots finds the vertex fastest; AM-GM suits "fixed sum, largest product" or "fixed product, smallest sum", but always check that the equality case can happen.

## Problem 3: a line meeting a circle in two points

**Problem**　The line $x+y=k$ meets the circle $(x-1)^2+(y+2)^2=8$ in two points. Find the range of $k$.

The centre is $(1,-2)$ and the radius is $r=\sqrt{8}=2\sqrt{2}$.

#### Way 1: the distance from the centre to the line

Two points $\iff d<r$. Write the line as $x+y-k=0$:

$$
d=\frac{|1+(-2)-k|}{\sqrt{1^2+1^2}}=\frac{|k+1|}{\sqrt{2}}<2\sqrt{2}\iff |k+1|<4\iff -5<k<3
$$

The last step is exactly an absolute-value inequality from Problem 1: $k$ is closer than $4$ to $-1$.

<Graph desc="The circle (x−1)²+(y+2)²=8 (centre (1, −2), radius 2√2) and the line x+y=k. Drag k to slide the line. The orange segment is the distance from the centre to the line, d=|k+1|/√2, ending at the foot of the perpendicular. For −5<k<3, d<r and they meet in two points; at k=3 the line touches the circle at (3, 0), and at k=−5 it touches at (−1, −4); otherwise they do not meet." x={[-5, 7]} y={[-7, 3]} height={340}
  params={[{ name: 'k', min: -8, max: 6, step: 0.5, value: 1 }]}
  circles={[{ center: [1, -2], r: 'sqrt(8)' }]}
  lines={[{ through: [['k', 0], ['k-1', 1]], color: 'blue' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [1, -2], to: ['1+(k+1)/2', '-2+(k+1)/2'], color: 'amber' }]}
  points={[{ at: [1, -2], label: '(1, −2)' }, { at: ['1+(k+1)/2', '-2+(k+1)/2'], label: 'foot' }, { at: [3, 0], label: '(3, 0)', color: 'red' }, { at: [-1, -4], label: '(−1, −4)', color: 'red' }]} />

<Perspective title="Way 2: substitute and look at the discriminant">

From the line, $y=k-x$. Substitute into the circle:

$$
(x-1)^2+(k-x+2)^2=8 \iff 2x^2-(2k+6)x+(k+2)^2-7=0
$$

Two points means this quadratic equation has two distinct real roots, so its discriminant <Zh>判別式</Zh> satisfies $D>0$:

$$
D=(2k+6)^2-8\big((k+2)^2-7\big)=-4k^2-8k+60=-4(k+5)(k-3)
$$

$D>0 \iff (k+5)(k-3)<0 \iff -5<k<3$, the same as Way 1. Plot $D$ as a function of $k$ and you get a downward parabola that is above the axis between $-5$ and $3$: one more quadratic inequality.

<Graph desc="The discriminant D as a function of k: the horizontal axis is k, and D=−4(k+5)(k−3) is a downward parabola meeting the axis at k=−5 and k=3, with vertex (−1, 64). The red segment on the axis marks where D>0, namely −5<k<3, the values of k for which the line meets the circle in two points." x={[-8, 6]} y={[-40, 75]} height={280}
  functions={[{ expr: '-4*(x+5)*(x-3)', label: 'D(k)' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [-5, 0], to: [3, 0], color: 'red' }]}
  points={[{ at: [-5, 0], label: '−5' }, { at: [3, 0], label: '3' }, { at: [-1, 64], label: '(−1, 64)' }]} />

At $k=3$ the equation becomes $2(x-3)^2=0$, and the double root $x=3$ is exactly the $x$-coordinate of the point of contact $(3,0)$: the algebraic method hands you the intersection points as well.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Way 3: find the two points of contact first (graph)">

As $k$ grows, the line $x+y=k$ slides up and to the right. It is perpendicular to the vector $(1,1)$, so the two points of contact lie on the line through the centre in direction $(1,1)$, one radius away from the centre. The vector $(1,1)$ has length $\sqrt{2}$, so going one radius $2\sqrt{2}$ means going $2$ times $(1,1)$:

$$
(1,-2)\pm 2(1,1)=(3,0)\ \text{or}\ (-1,-4)
$$

Check them in the circle's equation: $2^2+2^2=8$ and $(-2)^2+(-2)^2=8$. The two tangent lines are $k=3+0=3$ and $k=-1+(-4)=-5$. The line cuts through the circle exactly when it lies between the two tangent lines, so $-5<k<3$. This method needs no distance formula and gives the boundary points of contact in one go.

</Perspective>

**Which to use when:** if the question only asks "how many points" or "the range of $k$", distance is fastest; if it also wants the intersection points or the point of contact, use the discriminant, because the roots are the intersection points; for the boundary values (the tangent lines), finding the points of contact on the graph is a good way to check your answer.

## Problem 4: solve $(x-3)(x^2-4)\ge 0$

#### Way 1: a sign chart

Factor completely first: $(x-3)(x^2-4)=(x+2)(x-2)(x-3)$, with roots $-2$, $2$ and $3$. On the rightmost piece ($x>3$) all three factors are positive, and moving left the sign changes each time you pass a single root:

| range | $x<-2$ | $-2<x<2$ | $2<x<3$ | $x>3$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sign of $(x+2)(x-2)(x-3)$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |

The inequality includes equality, so the roots count too: $-2\le x\le 2$ or $x\ge 3$, written $[-2,2]\cup[3,\infty)$. Check $x=0$: $(-3)(-4)=12\ge 0$.

<NumberLine desc="Solutions of (x−3)(x²−4)≥0: the closed interval from −2 to 2 (filled endpoints), and everything from 3 rightwards (3 filled)." from={-4} to={5}
  intervals={[{ a: -2, b: 2, open: [false, false] }, { a: 3, b: 'inf', open: [false, false] }]} />

<Perspective title="Way 2: look at the graph of the cubic">

The <Term t="math.polynomial-function">polynomial function</Term> $y=(x+2)(x-2)(x-3)$ has leading term $x^3$, so its graph runs from bottom left to top right, crossing the $x$-axis at $-2$, $2$ and $3$ in turn. The inequality $\ge 0$ asks where the graph is above or touching the $x$-axis: the "hill" from $-2$ to $2$, and everything to the right of $3$.

<Graph desc="The cubic y=(x+2)(x−2)(x−3) runs from bottom left to top right, crossing the x-axis at x=−2, 2 and 3. It is above the x-axis between −2 and 2, and for x>3; red segments on the x-axis mark the solutions −2≤x≤2 and x≥3." x={[-3, 4.5]} y={[-14, 16]} height={300}
  functions={[{ expr: '(x+2)*(x-2)*(x-3)' }]}
  points={[{ at: [-2, 0], label: '−2' }, { at: [2, 0], label: '2' }, { at: [3, 0], label: '3' }, { at: [0, 12], label: '(0, 12)' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [-2, 0], to: [2, 0], color: 'red' }, { from: [3, 0], to: [4.5, 0], color: 'red' }]} />

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Way 3: split into cases and compare x² with 4">

You don't even have to factor $x^2-4$. Split by the sign of $x-3$:

- $x>3$: $x-3>0$, so you need $x^2-4\ge 0$, that is, $x^2\ge 4$. For $x>3$, $x^2>9$, so this always holds: all of $x>3$ is a solution.
- $x=3$: the left side is $0$, so it holds.
- $x<3$: $x-3<0$, so you need $x^2-4\le 0$, that is, $x^2\le 4$, giving $-2\le x\le 2$ (all less than $3$).

Together: $-2\le x\le 2$ or $x\ge 3$. The third case can be read off a graph: the parabola $y=x^2$ is not above the line $y=4$ exactly for $-2\le x\le 2$.

<Graph desc="The parabola y=x² and the horizontal line y=4 meet at (−2, 4) and (2, 4). The dashed line x=3 is where the cases split." x={[-4, 4.5]} y={[-1, 12]} height={260}

  points={[{ at: [-2, 4], label: '(−2, 4)' }, { at: [2, 4], label: '(2, 4)' }]} />

</Perspective>

**Which to use when:** once the polynomial is fully factored, the sign chart is fastest and safest; the graph helps you remember "sign changes moving left from the right", and also shows a double root (touching the $x$-axis without crossing); splitting into cases suits an expression that is "one factor times something easy to judge", or a moment when you can't remember how to factor.

## Problem 5: finding a quadratic from two equal values

**Problem**　The quadratic function $f(x)=x^2+bx+c$ has $f(-1)=f(5)$ and minimum value $-4$. Find $b$ and $c$, and find the vertex of $y=f(-x)$.

#### Way 1: algebra

$f(-1)=f(5)$: $1-b+c=25+5b+c$, so $6b=-24$ and $b=-4$. Complete the square:

$$
f(x)=x^2-4x+c=(x-2)^2+c-4
$$

The minimum $c-4=-4$, so $c=0$ and $f(x)=x^2-4x$. Then $f(-x)=(-x)^2-4(-x)=x^2+4x=(x+2)^2-4$, with vertex $(-2,-4)$.

<Perspective title="Way 2: symmetry (graph)">

A parabola is symmetric about its axis, and the points $x=-1$ and $x=5$ with equal values are mirror images, so the axis is at their midpoint $x=\frac{-1+5}{2}=2$. The leading coefficient is $1$ and the minimum is $-4$, so the vertex is $(2,-4)$ and you can write at once

$$
f(x)=(x-2)^2-4=x^2-4x
$$

so $b=-4$ and $c=0$. This graph is $y=x^2$ shifted $2$ to the right and $4$ down.

The graph of $y=f(-x)$ is the mirror image of $y=f(x)$ in the $y$-axis, so the vertex $(2,-4)$ flips to $(-2,-4)$ with no further calculation.

<Graph desc="The solid curve y=f(x)=x²−4x has vertex (2, −4) and passes through the two points (−1, 5) and (5, 5) at the same height; the red dashed line is the axis x=2. </Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Does f(−1)=f(5) always mean the axis is x=2?">

For a quadratic function, yes. Its graph is symmetric, and on one side of the axis it keeps rising or keeps falling, so two different $x$ on the same side never give equal values. Two different $x$ with equal values must therefore lie on opposite sides of the axis, at the same distance from it. The algebra in Way 1 proves this too: the condition $f(-1)=f(5)$ forces $b=-4$, which is the axis $x=-\frac{b}{2}=2$.

</StudentAsks>

**Which to use when:** when the problem gives two equal function values, find the axis by symmetry first; it often finishes the problem in one step. The algebraic method always works and is good for checking. For transformations like $f(x-h)$ and $f(-x)$, thinking "move the whole graph" is faster than substituting and expanding again.

## Summary: which to use when

| type of problem | try first | when to switch |
|---|---|---|
| $\lvert ax+b\rvert<c$, $\lvert ax+b\rvert>c$ | sandwich or distance | draw a graph if the right side is not a constant; square if both sides have absolute values |
| max or min of a quadratic | complete the square, or the midpoint of the roots | with a fixed sum or product AM-GM works, but check that equality can happen |
| how many points a line and a circle share | distance from the centre to the line | use the discriminant when you need the coordinates |
| a factored polynomial inequality | sign chart | look at the graph for double roots or to check |
| symmetry of a quadratic | midpoint of two equal-value points | go back to algebra to check, or when the condition is not symmetric |

When two methods give different answers, one of them has usually missed a condition, such as a range restriction, the equality case or the direction of an inequality. That is exactly why knowing one more method pays off: each one checks the other.
