## What a radian is

On a circle of radius $r$, a central angle <Zh>圓心角</Zh> $\theta$ cuts off an arc of length $s$ <Zh>弧長</Zh>. The ratio

$$
\theta=\frac{s}{r}
$$

is the <Term t="math.radian">radian</Term> measure of that central angle (in Chinese the radian is also called <Zh>弳度</Zh>). In other words, radian measure says how many radii long the arc is.

- For the same central angle, scaling the circle scales the arc and the radius by the same factor, so the ratio $\frac{s}{r}$ doesn't change. Radian measure depends only on the size of the angle, not on the size of the circle.
- When the arc is exactly as long as the radius, the central angle is $1$ radian.
- The arc of a full turn is $2\pi r$, so a full turn is $\frac{2\pi r}{r}=2\pi$ radians.

When angles are in radians the word "radians" is usually left out: $\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}$ means $\frac{\pi}{3}$ radians. To mean degrees you must write the $^\circ$.

### Converting between radians and degrees

A full turn is $360^\circ$ and also $2\pi$ radians, so

$$
180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians},\qquad 1^\circ=\frac{\pi}{180}\text{ radians},\qquad 1\text{ radian}=\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\approx 57.3^\circ
$$

To go from degrees to radians, multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$; to go from radians to degrees, multiply by $\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}$, which amounts to replacing $\pi$ by $180^\circ$. It pays to know the common angles by heart:

| Degrees | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $120^\circ$ | $135^\circ$ | $150^\circ$ | $180^\circ$ | $270^\circ$ | $360^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radians | $\frac{\pi}{6}$ | $\frac{\pi}{4}$ | $\frac{\pi}{3}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ | $\frac{3\pi}{4}$ | $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ | $\pi$ | $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ | $2\pi$ |

**Example.** Convert $210^\circ$ to radians, then convert $\frac{5\pi}{12}$ to degrees.

$210\times\frac{\pi}{180}=\frac{7\pi}{6}$. The other way, $\frac{5\pi}{12}\times\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}=5\times 15^\circ=75^\circ$.

<Perspective title="Count steps: one step of 30° is π/6">

You don't have to multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$ every time. Cut a full turn into $12$ steps, like a clock face: each step is $360^\circ\div12=30^\circ$, which is also $2\pi\div12=\frac{\pi}{6}$; half a step is $15^\circ=\frac{\pi}{12}$. Converting becomes counting steps:

- $210^\circ=7\times30^\circ$ is $7$ steps, so it is $7\times\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac{7\pi}{6}$.
- $\frac{5\pi}{12}=5\times\frac{\pi}{12}$ is $5$ half-steps, so it is $5\times15^\circ=75^\circ$.
- $150^\circ=5\times30^\circ$ is $5$ steps, that is $\frac{5\pi}{6}$, exactly the angle used in the sector example below.

Counting steps also tells you where the terminal side is: $\frac{7\pi}{6}$ is $1$ step past half a turn ($6$ steps), so its terminal side is in the third quadrant. Note that we count counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis, unlike a clock, which runs clockwise from $12$ o'clock.

Radians work for <Term t="math.generalized-angle">generalised angles</Term> too: counterclockwise is positive, clockwise is negative, and a full turn is $2\pi$, so $\theta$ and $\theta+2k\pi$ ($k$ an integer) are coterminal. For example, $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $-\frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi=\frac{3\pi}{2}$ both have their <Term t="math.terminal-side">terminal side</Term> on the negative $y$-axis.

In the curriculum, the learning content for both Math A and Math B includes the definition of radian measure, arc length and sector area; the Math A notes add that converting between radians and degrees will be practised often in later topics.

<StudentAsks q="Is θ = 2 the same as 2°?">

No. An angle written without $^\circ$ is in radians, so $\theta=2$ means $2$ radians, about $2\times 57.3^\circ=114.6^\circ$. In standard position, an angle of $2$ radians has its terminal side in the second quadrant. A radian measure doesn't have to contain $\pi$: $\frac{\pi}{3}$ radians is about $1.05$ radians, and both are written in radians.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Straighten the circle: a radian measure is a number on the number line">

On a circle of radius $1$, the radian measure $\theta$ equals the arc length $s$. Imagine starting at the point $(1,0)$ and "straightening" the circumference, going counterclockwise, onto the positive half of the number line: however long an arc you travel, that is the number you reach. The quadrant boundaries $\frac{\pi}{2}$, $\pi$, $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, $2\pi$ sit at about $1.57$, $3.14$, $4.71$, $6.28$ on the line.

- $2$: $1.57<2<3.14$, between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$, so the second quadrant (matching the $114.6^\circ$ above).
- $4$: $3.14<4<4.71$, between $\pi$ and $\frac{3\pi}{2}$, so the third quadrant.
- $5.5$: $4.71<5.5<6.28$, between $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and $2\pi$, so the fourth quadrant.

## Arc length and sector area

A central angle $\theta$ is the fraction $\frac{\theta}{2\pi}$ of a full turn $2\pi$, so its arc and its sector <Zh>扇形</Zh> are that fraction of the whole circumference and the whole disc:

$$
s=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\times 2\pi r=r\theta,\qquad A=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\times \pi r^2=\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta=\frac{1}{2}rs
$$

In these formulas $\theta$ **must be in radians**. If the angle is given in degrees, convert it first, or work with "what fraction of a full turn": a sector with central angle $\theta^\circ$ has area $\frac{\theta}{360}\times\pi r^2$.

The last form, $A=\frac{1}{2}rs$, is very handy: if you know the radius and the arc, you get the area without finding the angle. It looks a lot like the triangle area "$\frac{1}{2}\times$ base $\times$ height".

The resemblance is no accident. Cut the sector from the centre into many thin slices; each slice is very nearly a triangle with its apex at the centre, height about the radius $r$, and a short piece of arc as its base. Take the sector with radius $4$ and arc $12$ (central angle $3$ radians) and cut it into $6$ slices: each has central angle $0.5$ radians and a base of about $4\times0.5=2$.

Straighten the bases of the $6$ slices and line them up on one line, with every apex at height $4$: you get a row of saw teeth. Treating each slice as a triangle with base $2$ and height $4$, each has area $\frac{1}{2}\times2\times4=4$, so together they make $6\times4=24$, which is $\frac{1}{2}\times4\times12$. The bases add up to the arc length $s$ and the height is $r$, so the total area is $\frac{1}{2}rs$. The thinner you cut, the more each slice looks like a true triangle and the smaller the error, until it equals the sector's area exactly. This matches the $24$ that the example further down gets from the formula.

**Example.** A sector has radius $6$ and central angle $150^\circ$. Find its arc length, area and perimeter.

$150^\circ=\frac{5\pi}{6}$. Arc $s=6\times\frac{5\pi}{6}=5\pi$; area $A=\frac{1}{2}\times 6^2\times\frac{5\pi}{6}=15\pi$. The perimeter of a sector is two radii plus the arc: $6+6+5\pi=12+5\pi$.

**Example.** A sector has perimeter $20$ and radius $4$. Find its central angle and its area.

Perimeter $=2r+s$, so the arc is $s=20-8=12$. The central angle is $\theta=\frac{s}{r}=\frac{12}{4}=3$ radians. The area is $A=\frac{1}{2}rs=\frac{1}{2}\times 4\times 12=24$.

<StudentAsks q="Is the perimeter of a sector just its arc length?">

No. A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc, so its perimeter is $2r+s$. Only when a question asks for the "arc length" do you count the arc alone.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Why radians are preferred: for small angles sin θ is about θ" needs="treating sine as a function of a real number, y=sin x, and drawing its graph (next chapter: Trig graphs and periodic models)" href="/en/math/s116-periodic-graphs/">

On a circle of radius $1$, the arc cut off by a central angle $\theta$ has length $\theta$, while the distance from the point on the terminal side to the $x$-axis is $\sin\theta$. For a small angle the arc and that vertical segment are almost the same length, so $\sin\theta\approx\theta$. Check with special angles: at $\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}\approx0.524$, $\sin\theta=0.5$, already close; at $\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\approx1.571$, $\sin\theta=1$, far off.

As a graph it is even clearer: near the origin, $y=\sin x$ almost hugs the line $y=x$. This only works in radians; in degrees, $\sin x^\circ\approx\frac{\pi}{180}x$, with an awkward extra factor. That is one reason the graphs of the trigonometric functions later use radians on the horizontal axis.
