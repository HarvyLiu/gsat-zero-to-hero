This chapter is for Math A only: the sum and difference formulas and combining sine and cosine are 11A content, outside the 11B (Math B) syllabus.

## The sum and difference formulas

For any angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

$$
\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta
$$

$$
\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta
$$

Replace $\beta$ with $-\beta$ and use $\sin(-\beta)=-\sin\beta$ and $\cos(-\beta)=\cos\beta$ to get the difference formulas:

$$
\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta
$$

$$
\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta
$$

To remember them: **$\sin$ is "$\sin\cos$ with $\cos\sin$", and the sign follows the original plus or minus; $\cos$ is "$\cos\cos$ with $\sin\sin$", and the sign is the opposite of the original.**

<Perspective title="In a picture: two heights stacked make sin(α+β)">

Take the case where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are acute and $\alpha+\beta\le 90^\circ$. From the origin $O$ draw a segment $\overline{OP}$ of length $1$ with direction angle $\alpha+\beta$, so the height of $P$ is exactly $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$. Then draw a ray with direction angle $\alpha$ and drop a perpendicular from $P$ to it, with foot $Q$. The right triangle $OQP$ has hypotenuse $1$ and angle $\beta$ at $O$, so $\overline{OQ}=\cos\beta$ and $\overline{QP}=\sin\beta$.

The height of $P$ splits into two pieces:

- The height of $Q$: $\overline{OQ}$ has direction angle $\alpha$, so its height is $\overline{OQ}\sin\alpha=\cos\beta\sin\alpha$.
- The rise from $Q$ up to $P$: $\overline{QP}$ is perpendicular to $\overline{OQ}$, so $\overline{QP}$ also makes the angle $\alpha$ with the vertical, and its upward part is $\overline{QP}\cos\alpha=\sin\beta\cos\alpha$.

Add the two: $\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta$. Read the same picture sideways: the $x$-coordinate of $Q$ is $\cos\beta\cos\alpha$, and $P$ sits $\overline{QP}\sin\alpha=\sin\beta\sin\alpha$ further left, so $\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta$. The minus sign is that step to the left.

The Math A formula sheet lists only the sum formulas for $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$. The difference, double angle and half angle formulas are not on it, so you need to be able to derive them from the sum formulas yourself.

**Example.** Find $\sin 75^\circ$ and $\cos 15^\circ$.

$$
\sin 75^\circ=\sin(45^\circ+30^\circ)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}
$$

$$
\cos 15^\circ=\cos(45^\circ-30^\circ)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}
$$

The two answers agree, and that is no accident: $75^\circ$ and $15^\circ$ are complementary, so $\sin 75^\circ=\cos 15^\circ$.

**Example.** Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be acute angles with $\sin\alpha=\frac{3}{5}$ and $\cos\beta=\frac{5}{13}$. Find $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$.

The formula needs all four values, so fill in the missing ones first. Sine and cosine of an acute angle are both positive, so $\cos\alpha=\sqrt{1-\frac{9}{25}}=\frac{4}{5}$ and $\sin\beta=\sqrt{1-\frac{25}{169}}=\frac{12}{13}$.

$$
\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\frac{3}{5}\cdot\frac{5}{13}+\frac{4}{5}\cdot\frac{12}{13}=\frac{15+48}{65}=\frac{63}{65}
$$

<StudentAsks q="Where does the sum formula come from?">

One way to see it uses the unit circle and the <Term t="math.law-of-cosines">law of cosines</Term>. Take $P(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and $Q(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$ on the unit circle. The angle between the radii $\overline{OP}$ and $\overline{OQ}$ differs from $\alpha-\beta$ only by a sign or a whole number of $360^\circ$ turns, so $\cos\angle POQ=\cos(\alpha-\beta)$.

By the distance formula: $\overline{PQ}^2=(\cos\alpha-\cos\beta)^2+(\sin\alpha-\sin\beta)^2=2-2(\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta)$.

By the law of cosines: $\overline{PQ}^2=1^2+1^2-2\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot\cos(\alpha-\beta)=2-2\cos(\alpha-\beta)$.

Setting the two equal gives $\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta$, and every other formula follows from it. Knowing where the formulas come from helps you remember the signs.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="With the inner product: the difference formula in one line">

The unit circle derivation above is even shorter with the <Term t="math.inner-product">inner product</Term> of plane vectors. $\overrightarrow{OP}=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and $\overrightarrow{OQ}=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$ both have length $1$, and the cosine of the angle between them is $\cos(\alpha-\beta)$. The inner product can be worked out two ways:

$$
\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\overrightarrow{OQ}=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta\quad\text{(multiply coordinates and add)}
$$

$$
\overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\overrightarrow{OQ}=\lvert\overrightarrow{OP}\rvert\lvert\overrightarrow{OQ}\rvert\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos(\alpha-\beta)\quad\text{(length times length times cosine of the angle)}
$$

The two results are equal, and that is the difference formula. Replace $\beta$ with $-\beta$ to get $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$; and $\sin\theta=\cos(90^\circ-\theta)$ gives $\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\big((90^\circ-\alpha)-\beta\big)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta$.

For example, with $\alpha=75^\circ$ and $\beta=30^\circ$ the two vectors are $45^\circ$ apart, so $\cos 75^\circ\cos 30^\circ+\sin 75^\circ\sin 30^\circ=\cos 45^\circ=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

<Perspective later title="With a rotation matrix: both formulas at once" needs="rotation matrices (Math A: Bayes' theorem and applications of matrices)" href="/en/math/s116a-bayes-matrix-applications/">

The matrix for a counterclockwise rotation by $\alpha$ about the origin is $\begin{bmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix}$. Rotating the unit-circle point $(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$ at direction angle $\beta$ by $\alpha$ lands on the point $(\cos(\alpha+\beta),\sin(\alpha+\beta))$ at direction angle $\alpha+\beta$. Work it out by matrix multiplication:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta\\\sin\beta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta\\\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta\end{bmatrix}
$$

Match the two components and the sum formulas for $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$ appear together. For this to count as a derivation, the rotation matrix itself must not come from the sum formulas: a rotation keeps lengths and angles, so $(1,0)$ goes to $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ and the perpendicular $(0,1)$ goes to $(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)$, and these two images are the columns of the matrix.

</Perspective>

## The sum and difference formulas for tangent

Divide $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$ by $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$, then divide top and bottom by $\cos\alpha\cos\beta$, and you get the sum formula for <Term t="math.tangent">tangent</Term>; the difference formula works the same way:

$$
\tan(\alpha+\beta)=\frac{\tan\alpha+\tan\beta}{1-\tan\alpha\tan\beta},\qquad \tan(\alpha-\beta)=\frac{\tan\alpha-\tan\beta}{1+\tan\alpha\tan\beta}
$$

The curriculum says the tangent versions are to be treated "as derivation practice" <Zh>以推論練習為原則</Zh>: the point is to know how they come out of the $\sin$ and $\cos$ formulas, not to memorise a separate set.

**Example.** Find $\tan 15^\circ$.

$$
\tan 15^\circ=\tan(45^\circ-30^\circ)=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{\sqrt{3}+1}=\frac{(\sqrt{3}-1)^2}{2}=2-\sqrt{3}
$$

<Perspective title="With geometry: extend a 30° right triangle">

You can find $\tan 15^\circ$ without the formula. Draw a right triangle $ABC$ with $\angle C=90^\circ$, $\angle B=30^\circ$ and $\overline{AC}=1$; then $\overline{BC}=\sqrt{3}$ and $\overline{AB}=2$. Extend $\overline{CB}$ to $D$ so that $\overline{BD}=\overline{BA}=2$.

$\triangle ABD$ is isosceles, so $\angle BAD=\angle D$; $\angle ABC$ is its exterior angle, so $\angle BAD+\angle D=30^\circ$ and $\angle D=15^\circ$. In the right triangle $ADC$:

$$
\tan 15^\circ=\frac{\overline{AC}}{\overline{DC}}=\frac{1}{2+\sqrt{3}}=\frac{2-\sqrt{3}}{(2+\sqrt{3})(2-\sqrt{3})}=2-\sqrt{3}
$$

The same as the formula gives. As a bonus, $\overline{AD}^2=1+(2+\sqrt{3})^2=8+4\sqrt{3}=(\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2})^2$, so $\cos 15^\circ=\frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4}$, matching what the difference formula gave earlier.

## The double and half angle formulas

Put $\beta=\alpha=\theta$ in the sum formulas and you get the <Term t="math.double-angle-formula">double angle formulas</Term>:

$$
\sin 2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta
$$

$$
\cos 2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1=1-2\sin^2\theta
$$

Know all three forms of $\cos 2\theta$: if you're given $\cos\theta$, use $2\cos^2\theta-1$; if you're given $\sin\theta$, use $1-2\sin^2\theta$.

Replace $\theta$ with $\frac{\theta}{2}$ in $\cos 2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1=1-2\sin^2\theta$ and rearrange to get the <Term t="math.half-angle-formula">half angle formulas</Term>:

$$
\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2},\qquad \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}
$$

<Perspective title="With graphs: sin²x is a squashed, raised cosine wave">

Another way to write the half angle formulas is $\sin^2 x=\frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos 2x$ and $\cos^2 x=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos 2x$. Read them as graphs: start from $y=\cos 2x$ (period $\pi$), squash it to half its height and raise it by $\frac{1}{2}$, and you get $y=\cos^2 x$; flip it upside down first and then do the same, and you get $y=\sin^2 x$.

So $\sin^2 x$ and $\cos^2 x$ both lie between $0$ and $1$, have midline $y=\frac{1}{2}$ and period $\pi$ (not $2\pi$), and the two curves mirror each other across the midline, adding up to $1$ everywhere. When you see a squared sine or cosine, the half angle formulas turn it into a first-degree expression in $\cos 2x$: a tool for lowering the degree.

**Example.** Let $90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ with $\sin\theta=\frac{4}{5}$. Find $\sin 2\theta$, $\cos 2\theta$, $\sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ and $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}$.

$\theta$ is in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative: $\cos\theta=-\frac{3}{5}$.

$$
\sin 2\theta=2\cdot\frac{4}{5}\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)=-\frac{24}{25},\qquad \cos 2\theta=1-2\cdot\frac{16}{25}=-\frac{7}{25}
$$

Also $45^\circ<\frac{\theta}{2}<90^\circ$ is in the first quadrant, so sine and cosine are both positive:

$$
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{4}{5}}=\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{5},\qquad \cos\frac{\theta}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{5}}=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{5}
$$

Check: $2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}=2\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}=\frac{4}{5}=\sin\theta$. It works.

<StudentAsks q="When I take the square root in a half angle formula, how do I choose the sign?">

The formula only gives you the square; the sign depends on which quadrant $\frac{\theta}{2}$ is in, not $\theta$. Halve the range of $\theta$ to get the range of $\frac{\theta}{2}$, then use "all positive in the first quadrant, $\sin$ positive in the second, $\tan$ positive in the third, $\cos$ positive in the fourth". For example, if $180^\circ<\theta<270^\circ$, then $90^\circ<\frac{\theta}{2}<135^\circ$ is in the second quadrant, so $\sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ is positive and $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}$ is negative.

</StudentAsks>

## Combining $a\sin x+b\cos x$ into one sine

In this section $x$ is measured in <Term t="math.radian">radians</Term>. Read the sum formula backwards:

$$
r\sin(x+\varphi)=r\cos\varphi\cdot\sin x+r\sin\varphi\cdot\cos x
$$

So if you find $r>0$ and $\varphi$ with $r\cos\varphi=a$ and $r\sin\varphi=b$, then

$$
a\sin x+b\cos x=r\sin(x+\varphi),\qquad r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\quad \cos\varphi=\frac{a}{r},\quad \sin\varphi=\frac{b}{r}
$$

You can picture $(a,b)$ as a point in the coordinate plane: $r$ is its distance from the origin and $\varphi$ is its direction angle.

Once it is combined, you can read off three things straight away:

- Since $-1\le\sin(x+\varphi)\le 1$, the **maximum of $a\sin x+b\cos x$ is $r$ and the minimum is $-r$**.
- The <Term t="math.amplitude">amplitude</Term> is $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
- The <Term t="math.period">period</Term> is still $2\pi$: when the two waves $a\sin x$ and $b\cos x$, which have the same frequency, are added, the frequency does not change; the amplitude is $r$, and the graph is shifted sideways by $\lvert\varphi\rvert$.

**Example.** Combine $\sin x+\sqrt{3}\cos x$ and find its maximum and minimum.

$r=\sqrt{1+3}=2$, with $\cos\varphi=\frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin\varphi=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so take $\varphi=\frac{\pi}{3}$ (that is, $60^\circ$):

$$
\sin x+\sqrt{3}\cos x=2\sin\left(x+\frac{\pi}{3}\right)
$$

The maximum is $2$ and the minimum is $-2$.

**Example.** Find the maximum and minimum of $3\sin x+4\cos x$.

$r=\sqrt{9+16}=5$, with $\cos\varphi=\frac{3}{5}$ and $\sin\varphi=\frac{4}{5}$. This $\varphi$ is not a special angle, but that doesn't affect the answer: $3\sin x+4\cos x=5\sin(x+\varphi)$, with maximum $5$ and minimum $-5$. For a maximum or minimum, $r$ is often all you need.

<Perspective title="With the inner product: the maximum is when two vectors point the same way">

Think of $3\sin x+4\cos x$ as the inner product of two vectors: $\vec{u}=(3,4)$ is fixed, and $\vec{v}=(\sin x,\cos x)$ has length $1$ and turns around the unit circle as $x$ changes. Let $\theta$ be the angle between them:

$$
3\sin x+4\cos x=\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}=\lvert\vec{u}\rvert\lvert\vec{v}\rvert\cos\theta=5\cos\theta
$$

Since $-1\le\cos\theta\le 1$, the maximum is $5$ and the minimum is $-5$, as combining gave. This view also tells you **when** the maximum happens: when $\vec{v}$ points the same way as $\vec{u}$, that is $\vec{v}=\frac{1}{5}(3,4)$, so $\sin x=\frac{3}{5}$ and $\cos x=\frac{4}{5}$; the minimum comes when they point opposite ways. The same goes for any $a\sin x+b\cos x$: the maximum is just $\lvert(a,b)\rvert=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

<StudentAsks q="Sine is at most 1 and cosine is at most 1, so isn't their sum at most 2?">

No. $\sin x$ is largest at $x=\frac{\pi}{2}$, where $\cos x=0$; the two never peak at the same $x$. Combine them: $r=\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt{2}$, so $\sin x+\cos x=\sqrt{2}\sin\left(x+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$, and the maximum is $\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41$, not $2$. The amplitude is $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, not $a+b$.
