## Cones, generators and cuts

First, the parts of a cone. A **right circular cone** <Zh>直圓錐</Zh> has an **apex** <Zh>頂點</Zh> and an **axis** <Zh>軸</Zh> (the line through the apex and the centre of the base; it is perpendicular to the base). Each straight line on the cone's surface through the apex is a **generator** <Zh>母線</Zh>. Every generator of a right circular cone makes the same angle with the axis; this chapter calls that angle $\alpha$.

Extend every generator through the apex to the other side and you get a **double cone**: two cones joined at their apexes <Zh>上下兩個頂點相接的圓錐</Zh>. Slice it with a plane, and the curve where the plane meets the cone's surface is a <Term t="math.conic-section">conic section</Term>.

The curriculum notes say that here you get to know conic sections **visually**, as the cuts a plane makes in a cone; their standard-form equations are not studied here, as they belong to <Zh>G-12甲-1</Zh>. So every decision in this chapter is made by looking at angles, not by writing equations.

## One angle decides the shape

Let the plane **miss the apex**, and let its angle with the axis be $\theta$ ($0^\circ\le\theta\le 90^\circ$). Picture the plane starting perpendicular to the axis and tilting steeper and steeper:

| Plane's angle with the axis | Plane and generators | Cut |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta=90^\circ$ | perpendicular to the axis, meets every generator | circle |
| $\alpha<\theta<90^\circ$ | slanted, still meets every generator, cuts only one cone | ellipse (closed) |
| $\theta=\alpha$ | parallel to exactly one generator | parabola (not closed, on one cone only) |
| $\theta<\alpha$ | steeper than the generators, parallel to two generators, cuts both cones | hyperbola (two branches) |

Why? When the plane meets every generator, the cut goes all the way round the cone, so it is a closed circle or ellipse. Once the plane is parallel to one generator, it never meets that generator, so the cut runs off in that direction for ever: that's a parabola. Tilt it a little more and the plane cuts the other cone too, so the cut splits into two branches, one on each cone: that's a hyperbola.

The side view below draws this. Look along the cutting plane and the plane shrinks to a line, while the cone shrinks to two generators crossing at the apex.

**Example.** A double cone's generators make an angle $\alpha=35^\circ$ with the axis. Planes that miss the apex make angles of $90^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $35^\circ$ and $15^\circ$ with the axis. What is each cut?

Just compare $\theta$ with $35^\circ$:

- $\theta=90^\circ$: perpendicular to the axis, so the cut is a circle.
- $\theta=60^\circ$: $35^\circ<60^\circ<90^\circ$, so the cut is an ellipse.
- $\theta=35^\circ$: equal to $\alpha$, so the plane is parallel to a generator and the cut is a parabola.
- $\theta=15^\circ$: less than $\alpha$, so both cones are cut and the cut is a hyperbola.

Note: sliding the plane parallel to itself, nearer to or farther from the apex (as long as it misses the apex), does not change the **kind** of curve, only its size. The kind is decided by the two angles $\theta$ and $\alpha$ alone. So the same plane can cut two cones with different $\alpha$ into different curves.

<Perspective title="Side view: one line and two generators">

You can decide without picturing the 3D figure. Look along the cutting plane (along the direction in the plane that is perpendicular to the axis): the plane shrinks to a line and the cone shrinks to two generators crossing at the apex. That is the side view above. This side view is a plane of symmetry of both the cone and the cutting plane, so the cut is symmetric about it, and the points of the cut that lie in it are the two ends of the cut along the plane's tilt. So just look at where the line crosses the two generators (including their continuations into the other cone):

- Both crossings on the same cone: both ends of the cut are there, so the curve is closed: an ellipse (a circle when the line is perpendicular to the axis).
- The line is parallel to one generator, so only one crossing is left: the cut is missing an end and runs off for ever: a parabola.
- One crossing on the upper cone and one on the lower cone: both cones are cut: a hyperbola.

Try it with the example's $\alpha=35^\circ$, dragging $\theta$ in the side view to $90$, $60$, $35$ and $15$ in turn:

- $\theta=90^\circ$: the line is horizontal and crosses the left and right generators at the same height, so the cut is a circle.
- $\theta=60^\circ$: the line is less steep than the generators, so heading up to the right it still catches the right-hand generator. Both crossings are on the upper cone, so the cut is an ellipse.
- $\theta=35^\circ$: the line is parallel to the right-hand generator and never catches it; it crosses only the left one, so the cut is a parabola.
- $\theta=15^\circ$: the line is steeper than the generators. Heading up to the right it pulls away from the right-hand generator, but heading down to the left it meets that generator's continuation through the apex into the lower cone, so the cut is a hyperbola.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Check it with equations: the equation of the cut" needs="equations of conic sections (分科; not on the 學測)">

Put the apex at the origin and the axis along the $z$-axis. A point at height $z$ is on the cone when its distance from the axis is $|z|\tan\alpha$, so the cone is

$$
x^2+y^2=z^2\tan^2\alpha
$$

Let the cutting plane be a distance $p$ from the apex, and measure true lengths in the plane along two perpendicular directions: $X$ along the plane's tilt (the direction of the blue line in the side view) and $Y$ along the direction in the plane perpendicular to the axis, with the origin at the foot of the perpendicular from the apex. The points of the plane are $(Y,\ X\sin\theta-p\cos\theta,\ X\cos\theta+p\sin\theta)$; substituting into the cone gives the cut

$$
Y^2=\tan^2\alpha\,(X\cos\theta+p\sin\theta)^2-(X\sin\theta-p\cos\theta)^2
$$

Expanding the right-hand side, the coefficient of $X^2$ is

$$
\tan^2\alpha\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta\,(\tan^2\alpha-\tan^2\theta)\qquad(\theta\ne 90^\circ)
$$

- $\theta>\alpha$: the coefficient is negative, so after moving terms across, $X^2$ and $Y^2$ have coefficients of the same sign: an ellipse. At $\theta=90^\circ$ the equation becomes $X^2+Y^2=p^2\tan^2\alpha$, a circle of radius $p\tan\alpha$.
- $\theta=\alpha$: the coefficient is $0$, and $Y^2$ equals a linear expression in $X$: a parabola.
- $\theta<\alpha$: the coefficient is positive, so after moving terms across, $X^2$ and $Y^2$ have coefficients of opposite signs: a hyperbola.

The sign of the coefficient depends only on $\theta$ and $\alpha$, not on $p$: exactly why sliding the plane does not change the kind of curve.

<StudentAsks q="Isn't a circle just a kind of ellipse? Why treat it separately?">

You can see it that way: the circle is the special case $\theta=90^\circ$, where the plane is exactly perpendicular to the axis and every point of the cut is the same distance from the axis. Tilt the plane even slightly and the cut stretches into an ordinary ellipse. If the options list circle and ellipse separately, check whether the plane is **exactly** perpendicular to the axis.

</StudentAsks>

<StudentAsks q="What if the plane goes through the apex?">

Then you don't get any of the four curves above. A plane through the apex can cut out just **a single point**, the apex ($\theta>\alpha$); **one straight line** ($\theta=\alpha$, where the plane touches the cone along a generator); or **two straight lines crossing at the apex** ($\theta<\alpha$). The only straight lines on a cone are its generators, and they all pass through the apex, so a plane that misses the apex never cuts out a straight line.

</StudentAsks>

### Cuts parallel to the base: the radius from similar triangles

A plane parallel to the base is perpendicular to the axis, so the cut is a circle. Its radius shows up in the slice through the axis: the small right triangle formed by the apex, the centre of the cut and a point on the cut is similar to the large right triangle formed by the apex, the centre of the base and a point on the base circle.

**Example.** A right circular cone has height $15$ and base radius $6$. A plane parallel to the base and $5$ from the apex cuts it. What is the radius of the cut?

The two right triangles are similar, so corresponding sides are in proportion:

$$
\frac{r}{6}=\frac{5}{15}\iff r=2
$$

<Perspective title="With coordinates: the radius is proportional to the distance">

Put the slice through the axis on a coordinate plane, with the apex at the origin and the axis pointing down along the $y$-axis. The right-hand generator passes through $(0,0)$ and $(6,-15)$, so its equation is

$$
y=-\frac{5}{2}x
$$

The plane parallel to the base and $5$ from the apex shows up in the slice as the line $y=-5$. Substituting into the generator's equation gives $x=2$, so $r=2$.

Or say it with trigonometric ratios: the generators' angle $\alpha$ with the axis has $\tan\alpha=\dfrac{6}{15}=\dfrac{2}{5}$, so the cut at distance $h$ from the apex has radius $r=h\tan\alpha=\dfrac{2}{5}h$. The radius is proportional to the distance from the apex: make the distance $\dfrac{1}{3}$ as big and the radius is $\dfrac{1}{3}$ as big too.

</Perspective>

## Conic sections around you: flashlights and lamp shades

The curriculum's examples are the light from lamp shades and flashlights. The key is to find the **cone of light** first: the rays leaving the bulb past the edge of the opening form a cone, with the bulb as its apex and the edge rays as its generators. The wall or table top is the plane that cuts the cone.

**Flashlight.** Say the flashlight's edge rays make $15^\circ$ with its axis, and the wall makes an angle $\theta$ with the axis of the light cone:

- Pointed straight at the wall ($\theta=90^\circ$): the edge of the bright patch is a circle.
- Slanted, with $15^\circ<\theta<90^\circ$: the patch stretches into an ellipse.
- Slanted until $\theta=15^\circ$: one edge ray is parallel to the wall, the edge of the patch is a parabola, and the patch runs off without closing.
- Slanted further, with $\theta<15^\circ$: the edge of the patch is a hyperbola. A flashlight sends out only one cone of light, so you see only **one branch** on the wall.

**Lamp shade.** If a lamp shade is open at the top and the bottom, the light from the top opening forms an upward cone and the light from the bottom opening a downward cone, both with their apex at the bulb. The shade's axis is vertical and the wall beside it is vertical too, so the wall is parallel to the axis ($\theta=0^\circ$) and misses the bulb. Here $\theta$ is smaller than the edge rays' angle with the axis, so the edges of the bright patches above and below on the wall are each one branch of a hyperbola.

<Perspective title="Count the rays: which edge rays reach the wall?">

Think about it from the light's side. A flashlight shines only forwards, so each edge ray is a **ray** leaving the bulb, not a whole line; the edge of the bright patch is where the edge rays land on the wall.

- Every edge ray reaches the wall: the edge goes all the way round, a circle or an ellipse.
- Exactly one edge ray is parallel to the wall and never reaches it: the edge is missing an end in that direction and runs off for ever, a parabola.
- Some edge rays point away from the wall and never reach it: only the rays heading towards the wall leave an edge, one branch of a hyperbola. If the rays that miss were extended **backwards** through the bulb, they would land somewhere else on the wall, and that is the other branch of the hyperbola; but a flashlight doesn't shine backwards, so you see only one branch.

A lamp shade open at the top and the bottom sends light both up and down, so both branches show. For the flashlight with $\alpha=15^\circ$, drag $\theta$ in the figure below to $0^\circ$ (the wall parallel to the axis): the right-hand ray points away from the wall and only its backward extension meets the wall (about $7.5$ below the bulb), so the edge of the patch is one branch of a hyperbola.
