## Generalised angles: turning from the positive $x$-axis

To put an angle on the coordinate plane, we place its vertex at the origin and its **initial side** <Zh>始邊</Zh> along the positive $x$-axis. This is called **standard position** <Zh>標準位置</Zh>. The side where the initial side stops after turning is the <Term t="math.terminal-side">terminal side</Term>.

- Turning counterclockwise gives a positive angle; turning clockwise gives a negative angle.
- A directed angle like this is a <Term t="math.generalized-angle">generalised angle</Term>. It can be negative, and it can be larger than $180^\circ$. The curriculum notes that the range of generalised angles is at first limited to $-180^\circ$ to $360^\circ$, and every problem in this chapter stays in that range.

An angle is in whichever quadrant its terminal side is in. An angle whose terminal side lies on an axis (for example $0^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$, $-90^\circ$) is in no quadrant.

| Angle $\theta$ ($0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$) | Angle $\theta$ ($-180^\circ$ to $0^\circ$) | Terminal side |
|---|---|---|
| $0^\circ<\theta<90^\circ$ | | first quadrant |
| $90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ | | second quadrant |
| $180^\circ<\theta<270^\circ$ | $-180^\circ<\theta<-90^\circ$ | third quadrant |
| $270^\circ<\theta<360^\circ$ | $-90^\circ<\theta<0^\circ$ | fourth quadrant |

Drag the slider below and watch the initial side turn to the terminal side: a positive angle turns counterclockwise, a negative one clockwise. To tell the quadrant, just look at where the arrow finally stops.

### Coterminal angles

Angles with the same terminal side are **coterminal angles** <Zh>同界角</Zh>. A full turn is $360^\circ$ and brings the terminal side back where it was, so

$$
\theta \text{ and } \theta+360^\circ\times k\ (k \text{ an integer}) \text{ have the same terminal side}
$$

To tell whether two angles are coterminal, check whether their difference is an integer multiple of $360^\circ$.

**Example.** Which quadrants are the terminal sides of $-150^\circ$ and $300^\circ$ in?

$-150^\circ+360^\circ=210^\circ$, which is between $180^\circ$ and $270^\circ$, so $-150^\circ$ is in the third quadrant. $300^\circ$ is between $270^\circ$ and $360^\circ$, in the fourth quadrant; it can also be written as the coterminal angle $300^\circ-360^\circ=-60^\circ$.

**Example.** Between $-180^\circ$ and $360^\circ$, find every angle coterminal with $200^\circ$.

$200^\circ-360^\circ=-160^\circ$ is in the range; $200^\circ+360^\circ=560^\circ$ is not. So there are two: $200^\circ$ and $-160^\circ$.

<Perspective title="On a number line: coterminal angles come every 360°">

Think of angles as numbers on a number line. The angles coterminal with $200^\circ$ are $200^\circ+360^\circ\times k$: on the number line, one point every $360$ starting from $200$, that is $\dots,-520,-160,200,560,\dots$. The range in the question, $-180^\circ$ to $360^\circ$, is a stretch of length $540$, longer than $360$ but shorter than $720$, so either one or two points of this list land in it, and you never need to keep adding or subtracting. In the figure, only $-160$ and $200$ land in that stretch.

Telling the quadrant is the same move: add or subtract $360^\circ$ to bring the angle into the stretch from $0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$, then see which $90$-long cell it lands in. For example, $-150$ moves $360$ to the right to $210$, which is in the cell from $180$ to $270$: the third quadrant.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Are −30° and 330° the same angle?">

They are not the same angle, but they are coterminal. $-30^\circ$ turns $30^\circ$ clockwise and $330^\circ$ turns $330^\circ$ counterclockwise: different directions and different amounts. They just stop on the same terminal side. Whenever a question is only about where the terminal side is, such as which quadrant, the two angles give the same answer.

</StudentAsks>

## Polar coordinates: distance plus direction

In rectangular coordinates, the point $P(x,y)$ means "$x$ to the right, $y$ up". You can also say it another way: "how far from the origin, and how many degrees turned from the positive $x$-axis". That is <Term t="math.polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</Term>.

- The origin $O$ is called the **pole** <Zh>極點</Zh>, and the positive $x$-axis is the **polar axis** <Zh>極軸</Zh>.
- For $P\ne O$, let $r=\overline{OP}$ and let $\theta$ be a generalised angle whose terminal side passes through $P$. The polar coordinates of $P$ are written $[r,\theta]$: **distance first, then angle**.
- One point can be written in many ways: $[r,\theta]$ and $[r,\theta+360^\circ\times k]$ are the same point. For example, $[3,-90^\circ]$ and $[3,270^\circ]$ are both $(0,-3)$.
- The origin has $r=0$, and its angle can be any angle.

### Converting on grid paper

The curriculum mentions using grid paper to convert between polar and rectangular coordinates. Plot $P$ on grid paper ($P$ not on an axis) and drop a perpendicular from $P$ to the $x$-axis: you get a right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{OP}=r$. For special angles, two special right triangles give you the side lengths:

| Triangle | Ratio of sides |
|---|---|
| $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ | $1:1:\sqrt{2}$ |
| $30^\circ$–$60^\circ$–$90^\circ$ | $1:\sqrt{3}:2$ ($1$ opposite $30^\circ$, $\sqrt{3}$ opposite $60^\circ$) |

Once you have the two legs, the quadrant of $P$ decides the signs of $x$ and $y$. In the other direction, from $(x,y)$ to polar coordinates, the Pythagorean theorem <Zh>畢氏定理</Zh> gives $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, and the shape of the triangle together with the quadrant decides $\theta$.

**Example.** Convert the polar coordinates $[4,150^\circ]$ to rectangular coordinates.

The terminal side of $150^\circ$ is in the second quadrant, $180^\circ-150^\circ=30^\circ$ from the negative $x$-axis. The hypotenuse is $4$, so the vertical side, opposite the $30^\circ$ angle, is $4\times\frac{1}{2}=2$, and the horizontal side is $2\sqrt{3}$. In the second quadrant $x$ is negative and $y$ positive, so the point is $(-2\sqrt{3},\,2)$.

**Example.** Convert the rectangular coordinates $(2,-2)$ to polar coordinates.

$r=\sqrt{2^2+(-2)^2}=2\sqrt{2}$. Both legs are $2$, an isosceles right triangle, so $OP$ makes $45^\circ$ with the positive $x$-axis. The point is in the fourth quadrant, so the angle can be $-45^\circ$ or the coterminal angle $315^\circ$. The polar coordinates are $[2\sqrt{2},-45^\circ]$, which can also be written $[2\sqrt{2},315^\circ]$.

<Perspective title="By symmetry: flip a first-quadrant point">

You don't have to redraw the triangle every time. Find the matching point in the first quadrant, then let a reflection decide the signs:

- $[r,180^\circ-\theta]$ is the reflection of $[r,\theta]$ in the $y$-axis: $(x,y)\to(-x,y)$.
- $[r,-\theta]$ is the reflection of $[r,\theta]$ in the $x$-axis: $(x,y)\to(x,-y)$.
- $[r,\theta+180^\circ]$ is the point symmetric to $[r,\theta]$ through the origin: $(x,y)\to(-x,-y)$.

The example $[4,150^\circ]$: $150^\circ=180^\circ-30^\circ$ and $[4,30^\circ]=(2\sqrt{3},2)$; reflecting in the $y$-axis gives $(-2\sqrt{3},2)$, the same answer as before. The example $(2,-2)$: it is the reflection of $(2,2)$ in the $x$-axis, and $(2,2)=[2\sqrt{2},45^\circ]$, so $(2,-2)=[2\sqrt{2},-45^\circ]$.

The next chapter, "Trigonometric ratios", defines $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ as $\frac{x}{r}$ and $\frac{y}{r}$ for a point on the terminal side (for an acute $\theta$ these are "horizontal side $\div$ hypotenuse" and "vertical side $\div$ hypotenuse"), giving $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$ with the signs built in, so you can convert even when the angle is not special.

<Perspective later title="Convert in one step with trigonometric ratios" needs="trigonometric ratios of generalised angles (next chapter: Trigonometric ratios)" href="/en/math/s116-trig-ratios/">

With the trigonometric ratios of generalised angles, converting is just substituting into $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$. For example, for $[4,150^\circ]$: $\cos150^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin150^\circ=\frac{1}{2}$, so $x=-2\sqrt{3}$ and $y=2$, and the signs come out by themselves. It works for angles that are not special, too: $[2,20^\circ]$ is about $(2\times0.9397,\,2\times0.3420)\approx(1.879,\,0.684)$. In the other direction, from $(x,y)$ to $\theta$, you look for the angle with both $\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}$ and $\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}$.

</Perspective>

## Angle of inclination: a line's direction as an angle

When a line $L$ crosses the $x$-axis, the smallest angle $\theta$ you turn **counterclockwise** from the positive $x$-axis to reach $L$ is the **angle of inclination** <Zh>斜角</Zh> of $L$; a horizontal line has angle of inclination $0^\circ$. So an angle of inclination always satisfies $0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ$.

The curriculum notes that in the plane, the ideas of <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> and angle of inclination are equivalent. Side by side:

| Angle of inclination $\theta$ | Slope $m$ | Line |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta=0^\circ$ | $m=0$ | horizontal |
| $0^\circ<\theta<90^\circ$ | $m>0$ | rises to the right |
| $\theta=90^\circ$ | no slope | vertical |
| $90^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ | $m<0$ | falls to the right |

The closer the angle of inclination is to $90^\circ$, the steeper the line and the larger the absolute value of its slope.

To get the slope from the angle of inclination, borrow polar coordinates: the line through the origin with angle of inclination $\theta$ ($\theta\ne90^\circ$) passes through the point with polar coordinates $[r,\theta]$. Convert that point to rectangular coordinates $(a,b)$, and the slope is $\frac{b}{a}$.

**Example.** Find the slope of the line through the origin with angle of inclination $120^\circ$.

The line passes through $[2,120^\circ]$. $120^\circ$ is $60^\circ$ from the negative $x$-axis and the hypotenuse is $2$, so the horizontal side is $1$ and the vertical side is $\sqrt{3}$: the point is $(-1,\sqrt{3})$. The slope is $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{-1}=-\sqrt{3}$, and the line is $y=-\sqrt{3}\,x$. The angle of inclination is obtuse, and the slope is indeed negative.

<Perspective title="By symmetry: supplementary inclinations, opposite slopes">

The line through the origin with angle of inclination $60^\circ$ passes through $[2,60^\circ]=(1,\sqrt{3})$, so its slope is $\sqrt{3}$. The line with angle of inclination $120^\circ=180^\circ-60^\circ$ is exactly its reflection in the $y$-axis: the point $(1,\sqrt{3})$ flips to $(-1,\sqrt{3})$, the vertical change stays the same and the horizontal change changes sign, so the slope is $-\sqrt{3}$. In general, for $0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ$ with $\theta\ne90^\circ$, the lines with angles of inclination $\theta$ and $180^\circ-\theta$ are symmetric about the $y$-axis, and their slopes are opposites. So for an obtuse angle, find the slope for the acute angle $180^\circ-\theta$ and put a minus sign in front.

Once you have learned the trigonometric ratios, you will see that the slope is the tangent of the angle of inclination.

<Perspective later title="With the tangent: the slope is tan θ" needs="the tangent among the trigonometric ratios of generalised angles (next chapter: Trigonometric ratios)" href="/en/math/s116-trig-ratios/">

The line through the origin with angle of inclination $\theta$ ($\theta\ne90^\circ$) passes through $[r,\theta]=(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)$, so its slope is $m=\frac{r\sin\theta}{r\cos\theta}=\tan\theta$. For example, $\tan120^\circ=-\sqrt{3}$, as in the example. At $\theta=90^\circ$, $\cos\theta=0$ and $\tan\theta$ is undefined, which matches "a vertical line has no slope". In the other direction, a line with slope $1$ has angle of inclination $45^\circ$, because $\tan45^\circ=1$.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can an angle of inclination be negative, or more than 180°?">

No. A line has no head or tail: turning it through $180^\circ$ lands it back on itself. So $\theta$ and $\theta+180^\circ$ describe lines in the same direction, and we agree to take only the one with $0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ$. For example, a line with angle of inclination $135^\circ$ is not written as $-45^\circ$ or $315^\circ$.

</StudentAsks>
