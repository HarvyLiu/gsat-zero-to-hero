> **An elective trick, not on the GSAT.** The derivative tests for increase and decrease, extrema and second derivatives in this chapter belong to the grade-12 advanced electives <Zh>數學甲</Zh> (code <Zh>F-12甲-4</Zh>) and <Zh>數學乙</Zh> (code <Zh>F-12乙-4</Zh>). They are in the scope of the AST (<Zh>分科測驗</Zh>) and are not part of the GSAT. Every GSAT problem can be solved with grade 10–11 methods alone; every derivative method in this chapter is shown next to a grade 10–11 method.

## Review: the derivative is the slope of a tangent

The last chapter started from secant lines. Take $A(a,f(a))$ and $B(a+h,f(a+h))$ on the curve $y=f(x)$. As $h$ gets closer and closer to $0$, the value the secant slope $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ approaches is the **derivative** of $f$ at $x=a$, written $f'(a)$: the <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> of the tangent to the curve at $A$. Sending each $x$ to $f'(x)$ gives a new function, the **derivative function** (<Zh>導函數</Zh>), also written $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

For a <Term t="math.polynomial-function">polynomial function</Term> you only need three rules:

- $(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$ ($n$ a positive integer), and the derivative of a constant is $0$;
- $(cf)'=c\,f'$ ($c$ a constant);
- $(f+g)'=f'+g'$.

Differentiating the derivative once more gives the **second derivative** (<Zh>二階導數</Zh>), written $f''(x)$.

**Example**　$f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x+1$ (the example from "Graphs of cubic functions"). Find $f'(x)$ and $f''(x)$, and the tangent at $x=2$.

$$
f'(x)=3x^2-12x+9,\qquad f''(x)=6x-12
$$

$f(2)=8-24+18+1=3$ and $f'(2)=12-24+9=-3$, so the tangent at $x=2$ is $y=-3(x-2)+3$.

<Perspective title="Expand in (x−h): the tangent slope is the linear coefficient">

In "Graphs of cubic functions" you wrote $f(x)$ as a polynomial in $(x-h)$ and dropped the $(x-h)^2$ and higher terms to get the line that approximates the graph near $x=h$. For this example, put $x=t+2$:

$$
f(t+2)=(t+2)^3-6(t+2)^2+9(t+2)+1=t^3-3t+3
$$

So $f(x)=(x-2)^3-3(x-2)+3$, and the approximating line near $x=2$ is $y=-3(x-2)+3$. Its slope $-3$ (the coefficient of $(x-2)$) is exactly $f'(2)$, and the line is exactly the tangent found with the rules. In other words, the grade-10 "approximating line" is the tangent; the derivative is just a way to get its slope directly, without expanding.

</Perspective>

## The sign of the derivative: is the graph going up or down?

If on some range $x_1<x_2$ always gives $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$, we say $f$ is **increasing** (<Zh>遞增</Zh>) there: the graph runs up to the right. If $x_1<x_2$ always gives $f(x_1)>f(x_2)$, we say $f$ is **decreasing** (<Zh>遞減</Zh>).

The derivative gives a test:

- if $f'(x)>0$ everywhere on an interval, then $f$ is increasing on that interval;
- if $f'(x)<0$ everywhere on an interval, then $f$ is decreasing on that interval.

The intuition: if the tangent at every point tilts up to the right, the curve keeps going up. A rigorous proof needs more calculus; here we understand it through tangent slopes.

**Example**　Find where $f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x+1$ increases and where it decreases.

$f'(x)=3x^2-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3)$. This quadratic opens upward, so by the method of "Polynomial inequalities" it is positive outside its two roots and negative between them.

| $x$ | $x<1$ | $1$ | $1<x<3$ | $3$ | $x>3$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $f'(x)$ | $+$ | $0$ | $-$ | $0$ | $+$ |
| $f(x)$ | increasing | $5$ | decreasing | $1$ | increasing |

So $f$ increases for $x<1$ and $x>3$ and decreases for $1<x<3$: the graph goes up, then down, then up.

<Perspective title="Without derivatives: the sign of a difference">

Grade-10 methods can prove the same increase and decrease; they just take more work. From the last section, $f(x)=(x-2)^3-3(x-2)+3$, so the graph is the graph of $g(t)=t^3-3t$ shifted $2$ right and $3$ up, and it is enough to study $g$. Let $t_1<t_2$:

$$
g(t_2)-g(t_1)=(t_2^3-t_1^3)-3(t_2-t_1)=(t_2-t_1)\left(t_1^2+t_1t_2+t_2^2-3\right)
$$

Since $t_2-t_1>0$, the sign of $g(t_2)-g(t_1)$ is the sign of the bracket.

- $1\le t_1<t_2$: $t_1^2\ge1$, $t_2^2>1$ and $t_1t_2>1$, so the bracket is positive and $g$ is increasing. The same holds for $t_1<t_2\le-1$ (both numbers are negative, so their product is greater than $1$).
- $-1\le t_1<t_2\le1$: $t_1^2\le1$, $t_2^2\le1$ and $t_1t_2<1$ (the numbers are different, so the product cannot equal $1$), so the bracket is negative and $g$ is decreasing. For example, with $t_1=-1$ and $t_2=1$ the bracket is $1+(-1)+1-3=-2$.

So $g$ increases for $t\le-1$ and $t\ge1$ and decreases for $-1\le t\le1$. Shifting by $2$, $f$ increases for $x\le1$ and $x\ge3$ and decreases for $1\le x\le3$, in agreement with the derivative. The advantage of the derivative is that you don't need to guess the dividing points $\pm1$ first: solving $f'(x)=0$ hands them to you.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Does the graph always turn where f′(x)=0?">

No: what matters is whether $f'$ **changes sign**. Take $y=x^3$: $f'(x)=3x^2$ is $0$ at $x=0$, but positive on both sides. At the origin the tangent is horizontal, but past the origin the graph still carries on upward; it is increasing all the way. So always look at the sign of $f'$ on both sides of the point; solving $f'(x)=0$ alone is not enough.

</StudentAsks>

## Local maxima, local minima and the second derivative

If $f(c)$ is larger than the values of $f$ near $x=c$, $f(c)$ is called a **local maximum** (<Zh>極大值</Zh>); if it is smaller than the nearby values, it is a **local minimum** (<Zh>極小值</Zh>). Together they are called **extrema** (<Zh>極值</Zh>). The word "near" matters: an extremum is only compared with nearby values, and need not be the largest or smallest value of the whole function.

From the test in the last section:

- if $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $x=c$ (up, then down), $f(c)$ is a local maximum;
- if $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $x=c$ (down, then up), $f(c)$ is a local minimum;
- if $f'$ does not change sign at $x=c$, $f(c)$ is not an extremum.

**Example**　The local maximum of $f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x+1$ is $f(1)=5$, and its local minimum is $f(3)=1$. Note that $f(5)=125-150+45+1=21>5$, so the local maximum $5$ is not the largest value of $f$; in fact a cubic like this one keeps going up to the right and down to the left, so it has no largest or smallest value at all.

**Second-derivative test**　If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$, then $f(c)$ is a local maximum; if $f''(c)>0$, then $f(c)$ is a local minimum. In the example $f''(x)=6x-12$, with $f''(1)=-6<0$ and $f''(3)=6>0$: the same conclusion.

The second derivative also tells you which way the graph bends. On an interval where $f''(x)>0$, the tangent slope $f'(x)$ keeps growing and the graph is **concave up** (<Zh>凹口向上</Zh>, shaped like $y=x^2$); where $f''(x)<0$, the tangent slope keeps shrinking and the graph is **concave down** (<Zh>凹口向下</Zh>). In the example the graph is concave down for $x<2$ and concave up for $x>2$, and the point where it switches, $(2,3)$, is exactly the point of symmetry from "Graphs of cubic functions". For a general cubic $f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$, $f''(x)=6ax+2b$ is $0$ at $x=-\frac{b}{3a}$, the $x$-coordinate of the point of symmetry.

<Perspective title="Double roots: the horizontal tangent touches the graph twice">

The local maximum and minimum can also be confirmed with the grade-10 <Term t="math.factor-theorem">factor theorem</Term>, with no derivatives at all. The tangent at an extremum is horizontal; the horizontal line $y=5$ meets the graph at $x=1$ and should "touch and bounce back", which is a double root. Check:

$$
f(x)-5=x^3-6x^2+9x-4=(x-1)^2(x-4)
$$

($x=1$ gives $0$, and synthetic division by $x-1$ twice gives the rest.) Near $x=1$ (for $x<4$), $(x-1)^2\ge0$ and $x-4<0$, so $f(x)-5\le0$, that is, $f(x)\le5$: $f(1)=5$ really is larger than the nearby values, so it is a local maximum. Similarly,

$$
f(x)-1=x^3-6x^2+9x=x(x-3)^2
$$

so near $x=3$ (for $x>0$), $f(x)-1\ge0$, and $f(3)=1$ is a local minimum. This is the same thing as the last chapter's double-root test $f(a)=f'(a)=0$: $f'(1)=0$ and $f(1)-5=0$, so $(x-1)^2$ divides $f(x)-5$.

</Perspective>

## Counting real roots: which side of the $x$-axis are the extrema on?

Let $f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$ with $a>0$.

- If $f'(x)=0$ does not have two distinct real roots, then $f'(x)\ge0$, $f$ increases all the way, and the graph crosses the $x$-axis only once: $f(x)=0$ has **exactly one real root**.
- If $f'(x)=0$ has two distinct real roots $\alpha<\beta$, the graph goes up, down, then up, with local maximum $M=f(\alpha)$ and local minimum $m=f(\beta)$, and $M>m$.

| Local maximum $M$, local minimum $m$ | Graph and the $x$-axis | Number of distinct real roots |
|---|---|---|
| $M>0$ and $m<0$ | high point above, low point below: three crossings | $3$ |
| $M=0$ or $m=0$ | one extreme point lies on the $x$-axis (a double root) | $2$ |
| $M$ and $m$ have the same sign | both extreme points on the same side of the $x$-axis | $1$ |

When $a<0$, multiply $f$ by $-1$ and use the table; the roots do not change.

The number of real roots of $f(x)=k$ is the number of points where the horizontal line $y=k$ meets the curve $y=f(x)$, and the same idea applies.

**Example**　Find the number of distinct real roots of $x^3-6x^2+9x+1=k$ for each $k$.

The local maximum is $5$ and the local minimum is $1$. For the horizontal line $y=k$:

- $k>5$ or $k<1$: it meets the curve once, $1$ real root;
- $k=5$ or $k=1$: it touches the curve at a turning point and meets it once more, $2$ distinct real roots;
- $1<k<5$: it meets the curve three times, $3$ real roots.

Drag k (from −2 to 8): when k is between the local minimum 1 and the local maximum 5, the line meets the curve at three points; at k=5 the line touches the high point (1, 5), and at k=1 it touches the low point (3, 1), each time leaving only two meeting points; for k>5 or k<1 it meets the curve only once." x={[-1, 5]} y={[-3, 9]}

  functions={[{ expr: 'x^3-6*x^2+9*x+1', label: 'y=f(x)' }, { expr: 'k+0*x', label: 'y=k', color: 'amber', dashed: true }]}
  points={[{ at: [1, 5], label: '(1, 5)' }, { at: [3, 1], label: '(3, 1)' }]} />

<Perspective title="Without derivatives: compare coefficients to find the boundary k">

The values of $k$ on the boundary are the ones that give $f(x)-k$ a double root. Compare coefficients, as in grade 10. Suppose

$$
x^3-6x^2+9x+(1-k)=(x-r)^2(x-q)=x^3-(2r+q)x^2+(r^2+2rq)x-r^2q
$$

Comparing coefficients: $2r+q=6$, $r^2+2rq=9$ and $r^2q=k-1$. The first gives $q=6-2r$; substituting into the second:

$$
r^2+2r(6-2r)=9\iff -3r^2+12r-9=0\iff (r-1)(r-3)=0
$$

- $r=1$: $q=4$ and $k-1=1\cdot4=4$, so $k=5$;
- $r=3$: $q=0$ and $k-1=9\cdot0=0$, so $k=1$.

So there is a double root only for $k=5$ and $k=1$. These two values split $k$ into three stretches. While $k$ moves within one stretch, the horizontal line is never tangent to the curve anywhere, so meeting points never merge or split and their number stays the same; so in each stretch, try one $k$ and count meeting points (for example, at $k=3$, substituting $x=0,1,3,4$ gives $f(x)-3=-2,2,-2,2$: three sign changes). The answer matches the one from the extrema; the derivative just hands you $5$ and $1$ in one step.

</Perspective>

## Optimization: largest and smallest values

To find the largest or smallest value on a range, compare two kinds of candidates: the points in the range where $f'(x)=0$, and the endpoints of the range.

**Example 1 (a quadratic)**　With $40$ m of fencing you enclose a rectangular garden against a wall (the side along the wall needs no fence). Let each of the two sides perpendicular to the wall be $x$ m. What is the largest possible area? What if the site requires $x\le8$?

The side parallel to the wall is $40-2x$, so the area is $A(x)=x(40-2x)=40x-2x^2$ for $0<x<20$. $A'(x)=40-4x$ changes from positive to negative at $x=10$, so the largest area is $A(10)=200$ m².

If $0<x\le8$: on this range $A'(x)=40-4x>0$, so $A$ increases all the way and the largest value is at the endpoint $x=8$: $A(8)=8\times24=192$ m².

  functions={[{ expr: 'x*(40-2*x)', label: 'y=A(x)', domain: [0, 20] }]}
  segments={[{ from: [8, 0], to: [8, 192], dashed: true, color: 'amber' }]}
  points={[{ at: [10, 200], label: '(10, 200)' }, { at: [8, 192], label: '(8, 192)', color: 'amber' }]} />

<Perspective title="The grade-10 way: complete the square, then ask whether the vertex is in range">

For the largest or smallest value of a <Term t="math.quadratic-function">quadratic function</Term>, <Term t="math.completing-the-square">completing the square</Term> is all the GSAT needs:

$$
A(x)=-2x^2+40x=-2(x-10)^2+200
$$

The parabola opens downward with vertex $(10,200)$, so the largest area is $200$. If $0<x\le8$, the vertex $x=10$ is outside the range; to the left of the vertex the graph keeps rising, so the largest value is at the endpoint nearest the vertex, $x=8$: $A(8)=-2\cdot4+200=192$.

Comparing the two: the derivative's $A'(x)=0$ finds exactly the vertex, and "if the vertex is out of range, look at the endpoints" corresponds to "$A'$ does not change sign on the range".

</Perspective>

**Example 2 (a cubic)**　From a square sheet of card with side $12$ cm, cut a small square of side $x$ cm from each corner and fold up an open box. What is the largest possible volume?

The base of the box is a square of side $12-2x$ and its height is $x$, so the volume is

$$
V(x)=x(12-2x)^2=4x^3-48x^2+144x,\qquad 0<x<6
$$

$V'(x)=12x^2-96x+144=12(x-2)(x-6)$. Inside $0<x<6$ the only root is $x=2$, where $V'$ changes from positive to negative, so the largest volume is $V(2)=2\times8^2=128$ cm³.

<Graph desc="The graph of y=V(x)=x(12−2x)² for x from 0 to 6: it rises from 0 to the highest point (2, 128), then falls to (6, 0). The dashed line is the horizontal line y=128; it touches the curve from above at x=2, and the rest of the curve lies below it." x={[-0.5, 6.5]} y={[-10, 150]}
  functions={[{ expr: 'x*(12-2*x)^2', label: 'y=V(x)', domain: [0, 6] }, { expr: '128+0*x', label: 'y=128', color: 'amber', dashed: true }]}
  points={[{ at: [2, 128], label: '(2, 128)' }]} />

<Perspective title="Check without derivatives: V(x)−128 is a square times a negative number">

Once you have the answer, the factor theorem confirms it. $V(2)=128$, so $x=2$ is a root of $V(x)-128$, and it should be a double root (the horizontal line $y=128$ touches the graph there).

$$
V(x)-128=4x^3-48x^2+144x-128=4(x^3-12x^2+36x-32)=4(x-2)^2(x-8)
$$

For $0<x<6$, $(x-2)^2\ge0$ and $x-8<0$, so $V(x)-128\le0$, with equality only at $x=2$. That proves $128$ is the largest value, using nothing but polynomial factoring.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Is the largest value always where f′(x)=0?">

No. In Example 1 with the limit $x\le8$, $A'(x)$ is never $0$ on the range, and the largest value is at the endpoint $x=8$. So list both "the points in the range where $f'(x)=0$" and "the endpoints", and compare. If an endpoint is not included in the range (like $x=0$ and $x=6$ for $0<x<6$), look at the values of the function near it: in Example 2 the volume is close to $0$ near both ends.

</StudentAsks>

## Using it on GSAT problems: the two methods side by side

The left column below is the grade 10–11 method the GSAT needs; the right column is the check or shortcut the derivative offers. In the exam the left column is enough; the right column is good for checking your answer.

| Problem type | Grade 10–11 method | With derivatives (AST tool) |
|---|---|---|
| Where a cubic increases or decreases | write it as $a(x-h)^3+p(x-h)+k$ and look at the sign of $p$; for the dividing points, look at the sign of a difference | solve $f'(x)>0$ and $f'(x)<0$ |
| Number of real roots of a cubic equation, or of solutions of $f(x)=k$ | plug in values and watch the sign changes; compare coefficients to find the $k$ that gives $f(x)-k$ a double root | find the local maximum and minimum and see which stretch they (or $k$) are in |
| Largest or smallest value of a quadratic on a range | complete the square to find the vertex, then compare the endpoints of the range | $f'(x)=0$ finds the vertex; the endpoints still need comparing |
| Point of symmetry of a cubic | $h=-\frac{b}{3a}$, $k=f(h)$ | $f''(h)=0$; it is also the midpoint of the two extreme points |
| Confirming that a value is the largest or smallest | use the factor theorem to write $(x-a)^2\times(\cdots)$ and look at the sign | the sign change of $f'$, or the sign of $f''$ |
