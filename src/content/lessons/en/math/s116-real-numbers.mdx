## Every point on the number line is a real number

<Term t="math.real-number">Real numbers</Term> come in two kinds:

- **<Term t="math.rational-number">Rational numbers</Term>** can be written as a ratio of two integers $\frac{p}{q}$ ($q\ne 0$). Their decimals either stop or **repeat**, for example $\frac{1}{4}=0.25$ and $\frac{1}{3}=0.333\cdots=0.\overline{3}$.
- **<Term t="math.irrational-number">Irrational numbers</Term>** cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. Their decimals **never stop and never repeat**, for example $\sqrt{2}=1.41421\cdots$ and $\pi=3.14159\cdots$.

Every point on the number line is a real number. For any two real numbers $a$ and $b$, exactly one of $a<b$, $a=b$ and $a>b$ is true. This is the **trichotomy law** <Zh>三一律</Zh>. So any two real numbers can be compared, and on the number line you can always tell which one is to the left.

An irrational number's decimal never ends, but you can still draw exactly where it sits on the number line. Take $\sqrt{5}$:

1. Walk from $0$ to $2$ on the number line, then draw a vertical segment of length $1$ up from $2$. This makes a right triangle with legs $2$ and $1$.
2. By Pythagoras' theorem the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt{5}$.
3. With centre $0$ and the hypotenuse as radius, swing an arc down to the number line. Where it lands is $\sqrt{5}$.

The picture also checks the estimate: the hypotenuse is longer than the long leg $2$ and shorter than the two legs together, $3$, so $2<\sqrt{5}<3$. The arc lands a little to the right of $2$, which agrees with the $2.23\cdots$ we compute below.

### Turning a repeating decimal into a fraction

A repeating decimal is always rational, and you can work out exactly which fraction it is.

**Example.** Write $0.\overline{12}$ as a fraction in lowest terms.

Let $x=0.1212\cdots$. The repeating block has two digits, so multiply by $100$:

$$
100x=12.1212\cdots
$$

Subtract the two lines and the repeating parts cancel: $99x=12$, so $x=\frac{12}{99}=\frac{4}{33}$.

<Perspective title="Shortcut: the repeating block over the same number of 9s">

The subtraction above works the same way every time. If the repeating block has $n$ digits, multiply by $10^n$; after subtracting, the left side is $(10^n-1)x$, which is $n$ nines times $x$, and the right side is the repeating block itself. So

$$
0.\overline{d_1d_2\cdots d_n}=\frac{d_1d_2\cdots d_n}{\underbrace{99\cdots9}_{n\text{ nines}}}
$$

For example $0.\overline{3}=\frac{3}{9}=\frac{1}{3}$, $0.\overline{12}=\frac{12}{99}=\frac{4}{33}$ and $0.\overline{142857}=\frac{142857}{999999}=\frac{1}{7}$ (because $142857\times 7=999999$). Remember to reduce at the end.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="The other way round: long division shows why a fraction must repeat">

Work out $\frac{4}{33}$ by long division and watch the **remainders**:

| Step | Dividend | Quotient digit | Remainder |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $40$ | $1$ | $7$ |
| 2 | $70$ | $2$ | $4$ |
| 3 | $40$ | $1$ | $7$ |

At step 3 the dividend is $40$ again, so every step from here on repeats steps 1 and 2, and the decimal is $0.1212\cdots$.

In general, a remainder when dividing by $q$ can only be one of the $q$ values $0,1,\cdots,q-1$. If a remainder is $0$, the division stops (a terminating decimal). Otherwise some remainder must come back within $q$ steps, and from there the digits repeat forever (a repeating decimal). That is why a rational number's decimal either stops or repeats, and the subtraction method above shows the converse.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Repeating decimals as an infinite geometric series" needs="Infinite geometric series (elective trick)">

Split $0.\overline{12}$ into pieces:

$$
0.\overline{12}=\frac{12}{100}+\frac{12}{100^2}+\frac{12}{100^3}+\cdots
$$

This is an infinite geometric series with first term $\frac{12}{100}$ and ratio $\frac{1}{100}$. Its sum is $\dfrac{\frac{12}{100}}{1-\frac{1}{100}}=\frac{12}{99}=\frac{4}{33}$, the same answer as the subtraction method. For the GSAT you only need the subtraction method.

</Perspective>

### Estimating an irrational number

Calculators aren't allowed on the exam, so you need to estimate roots by squeezing them between squares.

**Example.** Estimate $\sqrt{5}$ to one decimal place.

Since $2.2^2=4.84<5<5.29=2.3^2$, we get $2.2<\sqrt{5}<2.3$. For one more decimal place, try $2.23^2=4.9729$ and $2.24^2=5.0176$, which gives $2.23<\sqrt{5}<2.24$.

Drag c to move the point (c, c²) along the parabola: when c² is less than 5, c is to the left of √5; when c² is more than 5, c is to the right. For example c=2.2 gives c²=4.84 and c=2.3 gives c²=5.29, so √5 is squeezed between 2.2 and 2.3." x={[1.9, 2.6]} y={[3.5, 7]} height={300}

  segments={[{ from: ['c', 3.5], to: ['c', 'c^2'], dashed: true }, { from: [1.9, 'c^2'], to: ['c', 'c^2'], dashed: true }]}

<Perspective title="Shortcut: write √5=2+d and squeeze it with algebra">

$\sqrt{5}$ is a little more than $2$, so let $\sqrt{5}=2+d$ with $0<d<1$. Squaring gives

$$
(2+d)^2=5 \iff 4d+d^2=1
$$

- Since $d^2>0$, we get $4d<1$, so $d<\frac{1}{4}$ and $\sqrt{5}<2.25$.
- From $d<\frac{1}{4}$ we get $d^2<\frac{1}{16}$, so $4d=1-d^2>\frac{15}{16}$ and $d>\frac{15}{64}=0.234375$, which gives $\sqrt{5}>2.234$.

Two lines of work give $2.234<\sqrt{5}<2.25$, so to one decimal place it is $2.2$, matching the squaring trials. This works for any root that is close to a perfect square; for example $\sqrt{10}=3+d$ gives $6d+d^2=1$, so $d<\frac{1}{6}$.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Why must √2 be irrational? Maybe we just haven't found the fraction yet?">

No. Use proof by contradiction: suppose $\sqrt{2}=\frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms. Squaring gives $p^2=2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even and so is $p$. Write $p=2k$; substituting gives $q^2=2k^2$, so $q$ is even too. Both $p$ and $q$ are even, which contradicts "lowest terms", so $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be a fraction. The curriculum notes that this proof is not a direct exam target, but it is a good example of proof by contradiction.

</StudentAsks>

## Absolute value is distance

The <Term t="math.absolute-value">absolute value</Term> $|a|$ is the distance from $a$ to $0$ on the number line, so $|3|=|-3|=3$. Even more useful:

$$
|x-a| = \text{the distance between } x \text{ and } a
$$

With that idea, all three kinds of problem are just measuring distances on the number line (here $b>0$):

| Problem | Meaning | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| $\lvert x-a\rvert=b$ | $x$ is exactly $b$ from $a$ | $x=a-b$ or $x=a+b$ |
| $\lvert x-a\rvert<b$ | $x$ is closer than $b$ to $a$ | $a-b<x<a+b$ |
| $\lvert x-a\rvert>b$ | $x$ is farther than $b$ from $a$ | $x<a-b$ or $x>a+b$ |

<Graph desc="The x-axis is the number line. Drag a and b: from the centre a, one arrow of length b points left and one points right; their tips are a−b and a+b, the two solutions of |x−a|=b. The red segment between them is where |x−a|<b, and the parts outside them are where |x−a|>b. By default a=1 and b=2, so the two solutions are −1 and 3." x={[-9, 9]} y={[-1, 2]} height={180}

With $\le$ or $\ge$ the endpoints are included too, for example $|x-a|\le b \iff a-b\le x\le a+b$.

You can write the answer as an **interval** <Zh>區間</Zh>, where a square bracket $[\ ]$ includes the endpoint and a round bracket $(\ )$ leaves it out: $a-b<x<a+b$ becomes $(a-b,\,a+b)$, and "$x\le -4$ or $x\ge 3$" becomes $(-\infty,-4]\cup[3,\infty)$. The curriculum uses intervals only to write answers; set operations between intervals are not tested.

**Example.** Solve $|2x+1|\ge 7$.

First take out the coefficient of $x$ so the expression looks like a distance:

$$
|2x+1|=2\left|x-\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right|\ge 7 \iff \left|x-\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right|\ge \tfrac{7}{2}
$$

That says $x$ is at least $\frac{7}{2}$ away from $-\frac{1}{2}$: $x\le -\frac{1}{2}-\frac{7}{2}=-4$ or $x\ge -\frac{1}{2}+\frac{7}{2}=3$.

<NumberLine desc="The solution of |2x+1|≥7: the centre is −1/2, and the answer reaches 7/2 to each side. Shaded are the ray x≤−4 running left and the ray x≥3 running right; −4 and 3 are filled circles (endpoints included)." from={-6} to={5}
  intervals={[{ a: '-inf', b: -4 }, { a: 3, b: 'inf' }]}
  points={[{ x: -0.5, label: '−1/2', open: true }]} />

<Perspective title="Algebraically: split into cases">

You can also skip taking out the coefficient and split by definition. $|u|\ge 7$ means $u$ is at least $7$ from $0$, that is, $u\ge 7$ or $u\le -7$. With $u=2x+1$:

- $2x+1\ge 7 \iff x\ge 3$
- $2x+1\le -7 \iff x\le -4$

The answer is again $x\le -4$ or $x\ge 3$. If you start from the definition $|u|=\begin{cases}u,&u\ge 0\\-u,&u<0\end{cases}$, split into the cases $x\ge -\frac{1}{2}$ and $x<-\frac{1}{2}$: the first gives $2x+1\ge 7$, so $x\ge 3$; the second gives $-(2x+1)\ge 7$, so $x\le -4$. Each part lies inside its own case, so the result is the same.

Splitting into cases saves you from taking out the coefficient; the distance view shows at a glance whether the answer is squeezed in the middle or out on both sides.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Graphically: a V shape and a horizontal line">

Draw $y=|2x+1|$ in the coordinate plane. For $x\ge -\frac{1}{2}$ it is the line $y=2x+1$, and for $x<-\frac{1}{2}$ it is the line $y=-2x-1$; the two pieces meet at $\left(-\frac{1}{2},0\right)$ in a V shape. Then draw the horizontal line $y=7$.

Solving $|2x+1|\ge 7$ means finding the $x$ where the V is on or above the horizontal line. Setting $2x+1=7$ and $-2x-1=7$, the crossings are at $x=3$ and $x=-4$. Outside the two crossings the V is higher than the line, so the answer is $x\le -4$ or $x\ge 3$. For $|2x+1|<7$ you would take the part of the V below the line instead: $-4<x<3$.

<Perspective later title="Square both sides: turn it into a quadratic inequality" needs="Quadratic inequalities" href="/en/math/s116-polynomial-inequalities/">

When both sides are non-negative, $|u|\ge 7 \iff u^2\ge 49$. So

$$
(2x+1)^2\ge 49 \iff 4x^2+4x-48\ge 0 \iff (x+4)(x-3)\ge 0
$$

which gives $x\le -4$ or $x\ge 3$. This method is especially handy when both sides have absolute values. For example, squaring $|x-1|<|x+3|$ cancels the $x^2$ and leaves the linear inequality $-2x+1<6x+9$, so $x>-1$ (that is, "closer to $1$ than to $-3$": to the right of $-1$, the midpoint of $1$ and $-3$).

</Perspective>

### Tolerance: the ± on a label

Products and parts are often labelled "$50\pm 0.2$ cm", meaning the length differs from $50$ cm by no more than $0.2$ cm. As an absolute value:

$$
|x-50|\le 0.2 \iff 49.8\le x\le 50.2
$$

Here $0.2$ is the **tolerance** <Zh>誤差範圍</Zh>. When you see "$\pm$", think "no farther than this from the centre".

<NumberLine desc="The accepted range for 50±0.2 cm: a number line from 49.7 to 50.3 with the centre 50 marked. The closed interval from 49.8 to 50.2 is shaded, with filled circles at both ends, so a length of exactly 49.8 or 50.2 cm also passes." from={49.7} to={50.3} step={0.1}
  intervals={[{ a: 49.8, b: 50.2 }]}
  points={[{ x: 50, label: '50' }]} />

<Perspective title="The other way round: find the centre and radius from a range">

Questions often give the range and ask for the absolute value. The range $p\le x\le q$ has **centre** at the midpoint $\frac{p+q}{2}$ and **radius** half its length, $\frac{q-p}{2}$, so

$$
p\le x\le q \iff \left|x-\tfrac{p+q}{2}\right|\le \tfrac{q-p}{2}
$$

For example $1\le x\le 7$ has centre $4$ and radius $3$, so it is $|x-4|\le 3$. "Out on both sides" works the same way: $x<-1$ or $x>5$ has centre $2$ and radius $3$, so it is $|x-2|>3$. A label like $50\pm 0.2$ simply tells you the centre and the radius directly.

</Perspective>
