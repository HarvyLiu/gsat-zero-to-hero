## The standard equation of a circle

A circle is all the points in the plane at a fixed distance from one fixed point. The fixed point is the **centre** <Zh>圓心</Zh> and the fixed distance is the **radius** <Zh>半徑</Zh>. If the centre is $(h,k)$ and the radius is $r$ ($r>0$), then a point $P(x,y)$ is on the circle exactly when its distance from the centre is $r$:

$$
\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}=r
$$

Squaring both sides gives the circle's **standard equation** <Zh>標準式</Zh>:

$$
(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2
$$

Watch the signs when you read the centre: $(x+3)^2$ is $(x-(-3))^2$, so the centre's $x$-coordinate is $-3$. The right-hand side is $r^2$, so take its square root to get the radius.

The standard equation is really the Pythagorean theorem: to get from the centre to a point on the circle you go $|x-h|$ across and $|y-k|$ up or down, and these two legs and the radius form a right triangle.

A point on a circle can also be described by how far you turn: start on the ray from the centre $(h,k)$ in the positive $x$ direction, turn it anticlockwise through $\theta$, and it meets the circle at

$$
(h+r\cos\theta,\ k+r\sin\theta)
$$

Check by substituting: $(r\cos\theta)^2+(r\sin\theta)^2=r^2(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=r^2$, exactly the standard equation. For example, on the circle $(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2=25$ the point with $\theta=90^\circ$ is $(-2,8)$, the top of the circle.

**Example.** Find the equation of the circle with centre $(-2,3)$ that passes through the point $(1,7)$.

The radius is the distance from the centre to $(1,7)$: $r=\sqrt{(1+2)^2+(7-3)^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5$. So the circle is $(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2=25$.

### Inside, on or outside the circle

Substituting a point into $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2$ gives the square of its distance from the centre. Compare it with $r^2$: less than $r^2$ means inside, equal means on the circle, greater means outside. For example, substituting the origin into the circle from the example above gives $(0+2)^2+(0-3)^2=13<25$, so the origin is inside.

The inside of the circle can also be written in set-builder notation <Zh>描述式集合符號</Zh> as $\{(x,y)\mid (x+2)^2+(y-3)^2<25\}$, read "the set of all points $(x,y)$ with $(x+2)^2+(y-3)^2<25$": what comes after the bar $\mid$ is the condition each point must meet. (x^2-4x+4)+(y^2+6y+9)=3+4+9
$$

which gives $(x-2)^2+(y+3)^2=16$: centre $(2,-3)$, radius $4$.

## How a line meets a circle

A line and a circle can meet in only three ways: in two points, in exactly one point, or not at all. When they meet in exactly one point the line is **tangent** <Zh>相切</Zh> to the circle; the line is a **tangent line** <Zh>切線</Zh> and the point is the **point of contact** <Zh>切點</Zh>. A line that meets the circle in two points is a **secant** <Zh>割線</Zh>. There are two ways to decide which case you are in:

- **Geometric test**: find the distance $d$ from the centre to the line, and compare it with the radius $r$.
- **Algebraic test**: substitute the line into the circle's equation, eliminate one variable to get a quadratic equation, and look at its discriminant <Zh>判別式</Zh> $D$.

The distance from the centre $(h,k)$ to the line $ax+by+c=0$ uses the formula from the last chapter:

$$
d=\frac{|ah+bk+c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}
$$

| Meeting points | Relation | Geometric test | Algebraic test |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2$ | meet (secant) | $d<r$ | $D>0$ |
| $1$ | tangent | $d=r$ | $D=0$ |
| $0$ | do not meet | $d>r$ | $D<0$ |

**Example.** How does the circle $C:(x-2)^2+(y-1)^2=5$ meet the line $L:x+2y-9=0$?

Geometric test: the centre is $(2,1)$ and the radius is $\sqrt{5}$.

$$
d=\frac{|2+2\cdot 1-9|}{\sqrt{1^2+2^2}}=\frac{5}{\sqrt{5}}=\sqrt{5}
$$

$d=r$, so $L$ is tangent to $C$.

Algebraic test: from $L$, $x=9-2y$. Substituting into $C$ gives $(7-2y)^2+(y-1)^2=5$, which tidies up to $5y^2-30y+45=0$, that is, $y^2-6y+9=0$. The discriminant is $D=36-36=0$, so again they are tangent. Solving gives $y=3$, and then $x=3$: the point of contact is $(3,3)$.

Replace the line by $x+2y=c$ and change $c$, and you can watch the three cases switch as $d$ and $r$ change places.

The distance $d$ from the centre to a line is the distance to the **nearest** point of the line, so $d$ is never more than the distance from the centre to any point on the line. As soon as the line passes through some point $Q$ **inside** the circle, $d\le\overline{CQ}<r$, and you know without calculating that it meets the circle in two points.

**Example.** For every real number $k$, how many points do the line $L_k:kx-y-k+1=0$ and the circle $x^2+y^2=4$ share?

Write the equation as $k(x-1)-(y-1)=0$: whatever $k$ is, the point $(1,1)$ is on $L_k$. And $1^2+1^2=2<4$, so $(1,1)$ is inside the circle. Hence $d\le\sqrt{2}<2$, and every $L_k$ meets the circle in two points.

Computing the distance the hard way: $d=\dfrac{|1-k|}{\sqrt{k^2+1}}<2\iff(1-k)^2<4(k^2+1)\iff 3k^2+2k+3>0$, and $3k^2+2k+3=3\left(k+\dfrac13\right)^2+\dfrac83>0$ always holds. Same conclusion, much more work.

<StudentAsks q="Which of the two tests should I use?">

If the question only asks how many points they share, the geometric test is usually faster: it is a single distance. If the question wants the coordinates of the meeting points or the point of contact, use the algebraic test, because the roots you solve for are those points.

</StudentAsks>

### Chord length

When a line meets a circle in two points, the segment between them is a **chord** <Zh>弦</Zh>. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord lands at the chord's midpoint, so half the chord, $d$ and $r$ form a right triangle:

$$
\text{chord length}=2\sqrt{r^2-d^2}
$$

For example, for the line $y=1$ and the circle $x^2+y^2=10$: $d=1$, so the chord length is $2\sqrt{10-1}=6$. Solving directly agrees: $x^2=9$, so the meeting points are $(-3,1)$ and $(3,1)$, which are $6$ apart.

## Tangent lines

To find a tangent you need just one fact: **the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact**. So the distance from the centre to a tangent line is exactly the radius.

**Example (given the point of contact).** Find the tangent to the circle $(x+1)^2+(y-2)^2=25$ at the point $P(2,6)$.

First check that $P$ is on the circle: $(2+1)^2+(6-2)^2=9+16=25$. The centre is $C(-1,2)$, and the radius $\overline{CP}$ has slope $\dfrac{6-2}{2+1}=\dfrac{4}{3}$. The tangent is perpendicular to it, so its slope is $-\dfrac{3}{4}$:

$$
y-6=-\frac{3}{4}(x-2)\iff 3x+4y-30=0
$$

The blue line passes through P with slope m: drag m, and only at m=−3/4 (perpendicular to the radius) does the line touch the circle in just one point; at every other slope it cuts into the circle and meets it again." x={[-7, 9]} y={[-4, 10]} height={360}

<Perspective title="Without perpendicular slopes: solve d=r for the tangent">

The line through $P(2,6)$ with slope $m$ is $y-6=m(x-2)$, that is, $mx-y+6-2m=0$. It is a tangent exactly when its distance from the centre $(-1,2)$ equals the radius $5$:

$$
\frac{|-m-2+6-2m|}{\sqrt{m^2+1}}=5\iff(4-3m)^2=25(m^2+1)\iff 16m^2+24m+9=0\iff(4m+3)^2=0
$$

There is only one solution, $m=-\dfrac34$, the same as before. The equation has a double root, which reflects that "through a point on the circle there is only one tangent". The vertical line $x=2$ has no slope and must be checked separately: its distance from the centre is $3\ne5$, so it is not a tangent. The advantage of this method is that it still works when $P$ is outside the circle; then you get two tangents (and if one of them is vertical, check it separately like this).

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Shortcut: the tangent formula, and why it works">

The tangent to the circle $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$ at the point $(x_0,y_0)$ on it is

$$
(x_0-h)(x-h)+(y_0-k)(y-k)=r^2
$$

There are two reasons. First, substituting $(x_0,y_0)$ turns the left-hand side into $(x_0-h)^2+(y_0-k)^2=r^2$, so the line passes through the point of contact. Second, its slope is $-\dfrac{x_0-h}{y_0-k}$ and the radius has slope $\dfrac{y_0-k}{x_0-h}$; their product is $-1$, so they are perpendicular (when the radius is horizontal or vertical, the formula gives a vertical or horizontal tangent, which also works).

For the example: $(2+1)(x+1)+(6-2)(y-2)=25$, that is, $3x+4y-30=0$. To remember it: split each square in the standard equation into two factors, one using the point of contact and one using $(x,y)$.

</Perspective>

**Example (given the slope).** Find the lines with slope $2$ that are tangent to the circle $x^2+y^2=5$.

Let the tangent be $y=2x+k$, that is, $2x-y+k=0$. Its distance from the centre $(0,0)$ equals the radius $\sqrt{5}$:

$$
\frac{|k|}{\sqrt{2^2+1^2}}=\sqrt{5}\iff |k|=5
$$

So there are two tangents, $y=2x+5$ and $y=2x-5$, one on each side of the circle.

<Perspective title="Find the points of contact on a diameter">

The line from the centre to a point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent, so both points of contact lie on the diameter through the centre that is perpendicular to the tangents. The tangents have slope $2$, so this diameter has slope $-\dfrac12$: it is $y=-\dfrac12x$. Substituting into $x^2+y^2=5$ gives $\dfrac54x^2=5$, so $x=\pm2$, and the points of contact are $(2,-1)$ and $(-2,1)$. Then by point-slope form: $y+1=2(x-2)$, that is, $y=2x-5$; and $y-1=2(x+2)$, that is, $y=2x+5$. This method finds the points of contact as a bonus.

You can also check with the algebraic test: substituting $y=2x+k$ into the circle gives $5x^2+4kx+k^2-5=0$; for a tangent the discriminant is $D=16k^2-20(k^2-5)=100-4k^2=0$, so $k=\pm5$.

</Perspective>

### Tangent length

Draw a tangent from a point $P$ outside the circle, touching it at $T$. Since $\overline{CT}\perp\overline{PT}$, $\triangle CTP$ has a right angle, and the Pythagorean theorem <Zh>畢氏定理</Zh> gives

$$
\overline{PT}=\sqrt{\overline{PC}^2-r^2}
$$

For example, the tangent length from $P(5,0)$ to the circle $x^2+y^2=9$ is $\sqrt{25-9}=4$.

<Perspective title="Substitute, and you get the square of the tangent length">

When you tested whether a point is inside or outside, substituting it into $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2$ gave $\overline{PC}^2$. So substituting a point $P$ outside the circle into $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2-r^2$ gives exactly $\overline{PC}^2-r^2=\overline{PT}^2$. If the circle is written in expanded general form (with $x^2$ and $y^2$ both having coefficient $1$, and $0$ on the right-hand side), the left-hand side is the same expression multiplied out, so substituting works the same way.

**Example.** For the tangent from $P(6,1)$ to the circle $x^2+y^2-4x+6y-3=0$, substitute $P$ into the left-hand side: $36+1-24+6-3=16$, so the tangent length is $\sqrt{16}=4$. Check: the circle is $(x-2)^2+(y+3)^2=16$, $\overline{PC}^2=4^2+4^2=32$, and $\overline{PT}=\sqrt{32-16}=4$.

</Perspective>
