## The cell theory: a conclusion pieced together by many people

After Hooke, microscopes kept getting better, and people saw more and more:

- **In the 1670s**, the Dutchman Antonie van Leeuwenhoek <Zh>雷文霍克</Zh>, using microscopes with lenses he ground himself, saw living tiny organisms in pond water, and saw bacteria too. He was one of the first people to see living cells.
- **In the early 1830s**, the British scientist Robert Brown <Zh>布朗</Zh> described the <Term t="biology.nucleus">nucleus</Term> in plant cells and pointed out that it is a common structure in cells.
- **In 1838**, the German botanist Matthias Schleiden <Zh>許來登</Zh> proposed that plants are all made of cells; **in 1839**, Theodor Schwann <Zh>許旺</Zh>, a German scientist who studied animals, proposed that animals are made of cells too.
- **In 1855**, the German physician Rudolf Virchow <Zh>魏修</Zh> proposed that all cells come from cells that already exist.

Put together, these conclusions are the three points of the <Term t="biology.cell-theory">cell theory</Term>:

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
3. All cells come from cells that already exist.

<StudentAsks q="Hooke saw cells in 1665. Why did the cell theory have to wait until the 1830s?">

Seeing boxes in one material is not the same as knowing that "all living things" are made of cells. To reach that conclusion, people needed better microscopes, needed to see cells in many kinds of plants and animals, and needed to find structures such as the nucleus that all kinds of cells share. What Schleiden and Schwann did was induction: they combined many observations into one general conclusion. The third point had to wait even longer, until someone made clear where new cells come from.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Seeing this history as a scientific process">

Here is the same history again, this time as four steps in how science moves forward:

| Step | What happened | People |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A new tool | Microscopes let people see structures too small for the naked eye | Hooke, Leeuwenhoek |
| 2. Observations pile up | Cells and nuclei are seen in different organisms | Leeuwenhoek, Brown |
| 3. Induction to a general rule | Plants and animals are all made of cells | Schleiden, Schwann |
| 4. Filling the gap | New cells come from existing cells | Virchow |

What makes the cell theory important is that it puts plants, animals and tiny single-celled organisms under one basic unit: however large or complex an organism is, you can study it starting from its cells. The third point is also where the later chapter on cell division begins: cells divide into new cells.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="In numbers: how much surface does a thin extension buy?">

Work it out with a simple box model. Treat the body of a root hair cell as a cube with sides of 10 units, and add an extension with a 2 × 2 cross-section, 20 units long:

- The body: surface area $6\times10\times10=600$, volume $10^3=1000$.
- The extension: volume $2\times2\times20=80$. It adds four side faces, $4\times(2\times20)=160$, and one end, $2\times2=4$; but the $2\times2=4$ patch where it joins the body is no longer exposed, so the surface area goes up by $160$ in all.

$$
\frac{160}{600}\approx 27\%,\qquad \frac{80}{1000}=8\%
$$

The volume grows by only 8%, but the surface area by about 27%. A long, thin extension uses very little material to gain a lot of surface for taking up water. When you infer function from shape, area is often the key.

</Perspective>

## Making slides: letting light through a thin layer

A light microscope sends light through the specimen, so the specimen must be thin enough, ideally one layer of cells. The required-biology lab activities use four ways to make a slide:

| Method | What you do | Typical material |
|---|---|---|
| Smear <Zh>抹片法</Zh> | Scrape gently with a toothpick or cotton bud and spread what comes off evenly on the slide | lining cells from the inside of the cheek |
| Squash <Zh>壓片法</Zh> | Put the material on the slide, add a cover slip and press gently so the cells spread into a thin layer | a soft root tip |
| Fold and tear <Zh>折撕法</Zh> | Fold a leaf and tear it apart, and take the clear, thin epidermis at the edge of the tear | the lower epidermis of a leaf (to see guard cells) |
| Free-hand section <Zh>徒手切片</Zh> | Cut the material into very thin slices with a blade | cross-sections of a stem or leaf |

Question 13 of the 112 GSAT tested observing the guard cells of a <Zh>石蓮</Zh> leaf, and its options described actions rather than naming the methods. So learn what you *do* in each method, not just its name.

Find the orange strip at the tear in the figure: that layer is what goes on the slide, not the thick green leaf tissue beside it. Think about it: why *tear* here rather than *cut*? When you tear, the thin epidermis comes away from the leaf tissue as one very thin layer; with a blade it is hard to cut that thin.

<StudentAsks q="Why can't I just put a whole leaf under the microscope?">

Light cannot get through thick tissue, and with many layers of cells stacked up, the image blurs into a mess. All four methods share one aim: to make a thin layer that light can pass through, with cells that don't overlap. They differ only because the materials differ, so the way to get a thin layer differs too.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Turn it round: choose the method from the material">

Exam questions often turn the table round: they give you the material and ask for the method. Then ask three questions in order:

1. Are the cells already loose, or can they be gently scraped off? If so, use a **smear**.
2. Is the material soft, so that pressing spreads the cells out? If so, use a **squash**.
3. Do you want the outermost layer of skin, or the arrangement of tissues? For the skin layer, **fold and tear**; to see how the tissues are arranged in a cross-section, use a **free-hand section**.

Example: you want to look at the cells in blood. The cells in blood are already loose, so the answer to question 1 is "yes": make a smear, spreading a small drop of blood evenly.

</Perspective>

## The micrometer: comparing cell sizes

A micrometer, a disc engraved with an evenly spaced scale, can be placed in the eyepiece, so that the scale and the cells appear in the field of view together. Counting how many divisions a cell covers lets you compare sizes: at the same magnification, if cell A covers 12 divisions and cell B covers 4, A is about 3 times as long as B. The curriculum's notes say that using a micrometer involves no scale conversion, so this chapter only compares numbers of divisions.

<Predict id="micrometer-zoom" prompt="Under a low-power objective, a cell covers 5 divisions of the eyepiece micrometer. Keeping the same eyepiece, you switch to an objective with 4 times the magnification. About how many divisions does the cell cover now?" answer={20} unit="divisions">

The micrometer is in the eyepiece, so its scale is not enlarged by the objective; what gets enlarged is the image of the cell. The image becomes 4 times as long, so it covers $5\times4=20$ divisions.

</Predict>

## Eukaryotic cells: structures that share the work

From junior high, animal and plant cells both have a <Term t="biology.cell-membrane">cell membrane</Term>, cytoplasm and a nucleus, and plant cells also have a cell wall outside the cell membrane. Required biology adds six structures in the cytoplasm: the <Term t="biology.endoplasmic-reticulum">endoplasmic reticulum</Term>, the <Term t="biology.golgi-apparatus">Golgi apparatus</Term>, the <Term t="biology.vacuole">vacuole</Term>, the <Term t="biology.ribosome">ribosome</Term>, the <Term t="biology.mitochondrion">mitochondrion</Term> and the <Term t="biology.chloroplast">chloroplast</Term>.

| Structure | Main function | Animal cell | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane | Encloses the cell and controls what goes in and out | yes | yes |
| Cell wall | Outside the cell membrane; supports and protects the cell | no | yes |
| Nucleus | Holds the genetic material inside a nuclear envelope; controls the cell's activities | yes | yes |
| Ribosome | A small particle that makes proteins; some are attached to the endoplasmic reticulum, some lie loose in the cytoplasm | yes | yes |
| Endoplasmic reticulum | A network of channels enclosed by membrane; the parts with ribosomes attached help make and transport proteins, and some parts make lipids | yes | yes |
| Golgi apparatus | Modifies proteins and packs them into small vesicles that go to other parts of the cell or out of it | yes | yes |
| Vacuole | Stores water and various substances | some have small ones | yes; a mature cell often has one very large central vacuole |
| Mitochondrion | The main site of respiration; releases the energy in food for the cell to use | yes | yes |
| Chloroplast | Carries out photosynthesis | no | yes, in cells of green parts |

You can think of a cell as a factory. The nucleus is the office that keeps the blueprints, the ribosomes are the workers who make parts, the endoplasmic reticulum is the pipework for processing and transport, the Golgi apparatus is the packing and dispatch department, the cell membrane is the gate that controls what goes in and out, the mitochondria are the power room that supplies energy, and the vacuole is the store. Here is where the analogy breaks: mitochondria do not generate electricity, they release the energy stored in food; and a factory does not grow and divide by itself, while a cell does.

## Prokaryotic cells: cells without a nucleus

A bacterium's cell is very different from the animal and plant cells above. Make a guess first.

<Predict id="prokaryote-organelles" prompt="The table above lists six structures in the cytoplasm: endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, vacuole, ribosome, mitochondrion and chloroplast. How many of them does a bacterial cell have?" answer={1} unit="kinds">

Only one: the ribosome. A bacterium has no nucleus, and none of the other five, which are all enclosed by membranes.

</Predict>

A bacterium's cell is a <Term t="biology.prokaryotic-cell">prokaryotic cell</Term>: it has no nucleus wrapped in a nuclear envelope, and its genetic material lies in one region of the cytoplasm; nor does it have the membrane-enclosed organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria and chloroplasts. It has a cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes, most also have a cell wall, some have a flagellum, and they are usually much smaller than eukaryotic cells. Bacteria and cyanobacteria <Zh>藍綠菌</Zh> are both prokaryotes. Cyanobacteria can carry out photosynthesis, yet they have no chloroplasts.

The cells of animals, plants, fungi (such as yeast) and protists (such as Paramecium) are all <Term t="biology.eukaryotic-cell">eukaryotic cells</Term>: they have a nucleus wrapped in a nuclear envelope, and many kinds of membrane-enclosed organelles in the cytoplasm.
