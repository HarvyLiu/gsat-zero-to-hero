This is a 116 sprint review: it assumes you studied required biology in school and still remember "chromosomes are copied and shared out equally" from junior high. The curriculum says that interphase and the cell-division phase are not split into smaller stages, and that mitosis is not divided into prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. So this chapter uses only two names, interphase and the division phase, and concentrates on how the number of chromosomes changes.

## The cell cycle: interphase and the division phase

The process that runs from the moment a <Term t="biology.eukaryotic-cell">eukaryotic cell</Term> is formed by division until it divides into two cells itself is called the <Term t="biology.cell-cycle">cell cycle</Term>. It has two parts:

- <Term t="biology.interphase">Interphase</Term>: the cell grows and makes a copy of its chromosomes.
- The cell-division phase: one cell splits into two cells.

The cells on a slide are not in step with one another; each has stopped at a different point in its cycle. It is like taking one photo of people running round a track: the longer a stretch of track, the more runners the photo catches on it. So **the share of cells found in a stage is about the same as the share of the cycle's time that the stage takes.**

<Predict id="root-tip-hours" prompt="A student counts 200 cells on an onion root-tip slide: 180 are in interphase and 20 in the division phase. Suppose the cell cycle of these cells takes about 20 hours. About how many hours does interphase take?" answer={18} unit="hours">

Cells in interphase make up $\frac{180}{200}=0.9$ of the total, so interphase takes about $0.9$ of the cycle: $0.9\times20=18$ hours. The division phase gets only $20-18=2$ hours.

</Predict>

<StudentAsks q="Under the microscope nothing seems to move in interphase. Is the cell resting?">

No. A cell in interphase is busy the whole time: it grows, carries out all kinds of metabolism, and makes a copy of its chromosomes. You cannot see separate chromosomes in interphase because they are long and thin and spread through the nucleus. Only in the division phase do they become shorter and thicker, and clear under the microscope.

</StudentAsks>

### Chromosomes are copied before division

<Term t="biology.chromosome">Chromosomes</Term> sit in the <Term t="biology.nucleus">nucleus</Term> and carry the genetic material. During interphase each chromosome is copied into two identical copies, which stay joined together. **Two joined copies still count as one chromosome.** So after copying, the number of chromosomes is the same, but the amount of genetic material has doubled.

Term preview: the main component of the genetic material is DNA, a kind of <Term t="chemistry.nucleic-acid">nucleic acid</Term>. The structure of DNA and how it is copied are taught properly in the chapter "Nucleic acids, the central dogma and transgenic technology"; here we only track it as "the amount of genetic material".

## Cell division: nuclear division plus cytokinesis

The cell-division phase involves two things: first the nucleus divides, then <Term t="biology.cytokinesis">cytokinesis</Term> splits the cell in two. There are two kinds of nuclear division: <Term t="biology.mitosis">mitosis</Term> and <Term t="biology.meiosis">meiosis</Term>. Telling them apart is a point the GSAT has tested, for example in Question 1 of the 114 GSAT. Mitosis comes first.

In mitosis, the changes in the chromosomes can be followed in four steps:

1. The copied chromosomes become shorter and thicker and line up in the middle of the cell.
2. The two copies of each chromosome separate and move to opposite ends of the cell.
3. A new nucleus forms at each end.
4. Cytokinesis splits one cell into two cells.

The result is two daughter cells, **each with the same number of chromosomes and the same genetic material as the parent cell.**

<Perspective title="Follow mitosis with two account books">

Besides looking at the drawing, you can keep just two accounts: one for **the number of chromosomes**, one for **the relative amount of genetic material**. Take a cell with 4 chromosomes as the example, and call the genetic material in a cell that has just divided 2:

| Moment | Chromosomes | Relative amount of genetic material |
|---|---|---|
| Start of interphase | 4 | 2 |
| After copying in interphase | 4 | 4 |
| After mitosis, each daughter cell | 4 | 2 |

Copying changes only the second account: the genetic material doubles while the chromosome number stays the same, because two joined copies count as one chromosome. In division the copies separate and are shared equally between two cells, so the second account goes back to 2 and the first stays at 4. **Copy once, divide once, and the daughter cells are exactly like the parent cell.**

</Perspective>

## Meiosis: gametes carry one set

Human body cells have 46 chromosomes, which can be matched into 23 pairs. Apart from one pair linked to sex in males, the two chromosomes of each pair have the same shape and size; one came from the father and one from the mother. They are called homologous chromosomes <Zh>同源染色體</Zh>. Taking one chromosome from each pair gives 23 chromosomes, called **one set** and written $n$. So human body cells have two sets and are diploid <Zh>雙套</Zh> ($2n=46$); sperm and eggs have only one set and are haploid <Zh>單套</Zh> ($n=23$).

Sperm and eggs together are called <Term t="biology.gamete">gametes</Term>. From junior high: in humans the testes make sperm and the ovaries make eggs. In these organs, reproductive cells form gametes by meiosis:

1. Before division, the chromosomes are copied once.
2. First division: the two chromosomes of each homologous pair separate, one to each cell. The cells go from $2n$ to $n$.
3. Second division: the two copies of each chromosome separate, just as in mitosis. The cells are still $n$.

One cell ends up as four cells, each carrying only one set of chromosomes. In a male, all four develop into sperm; in a female, meiosis ends up forming only one large egg, and the other, small cells break down.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| In which cells | body cells (growth, repair) | reproductive cells in the reproductive organs |
| Copying before division | once | once |
| Divisions in a row | one | two |
| One cell produces | 2 cells | 4 cells |
| Daughter cells' chromosomes | $2n$, the same as the parent | $n$, only one of each homologous pair |

In its analysis of the 112 GSAT, the exam centre suggested that teachers discuss a question: if the gametes' chromosome number is halved, why are the chromosomes still copied before meiosis? Start with a thought experiment.

<Predict id="no-copy" prompt="Suppose there were no copying before meiosis: a cell with 2n = 4 just divides twice in a row, each time sharing its chromosomes equally between two cells. On average, how many chromosomes would each of the final four cells get?" answer={1} unit="chromosomes">

Four chromosomes shared out twice: $4\div2=2$, then $2\div2=1$. Each cell has just 1. But one set is $n=2$ chromosomes (one long, one short), so a cell with only 1 is missing a whole kind of chromosome.

</Predict>

That meiosis divides twice in a row is a known fact; the question is how the chromosomes can be shared out given that. That is why the chromosomes are copied first: the 4 chromosomes are copied, giving 8 portions of genetic material, which two divisions share among four cells, exactly 2 each: one complete set. **Copy once and divide twice in a row, and each of the four gametes gets one complete set.**

## Fertilisation: from one set back to two

A sperm ($n=23$) and an egg ($n=23$) join to form a <Term t="biology.zygote">zygote</Term>, whose chromosome number is back to $23+23=46$, that is, $2n$. In each homologous pair of the zygote, one chromosome came from the father's sperm and one from the mother's egg.

Join meiosis and fertilisation together and you get a cycle. Look only at how each arrow changes the number of chromosome sets:

If gametes were not halved, the chromosome number would double every generation after fertilisation: $46$, $92$, $184$... Meiosis is exactly the step that keeps the chromosome number the same from generation to generation.

## From a zygote to many kinds of cell

The zygote divides by mitosis: one cell becomes two, two become four. Because every division is mitosis, all the cells have $2n=46$ chromosomes, and the same genetic material as the zygote.

<Predict id="zygote-ten" prompt="A zygote goes through mitosis again and again. Suppose that in each round every cell divides once. After 10 rounds, how many cells are there?" answer={1024} unit="cells">

Each round multiplies the number of cells by 2: $1\to2\to4\to8\to\cdots$, so after 10 rounds there are $2^{10}=1024$ cells. Every one of them still has 46 chromosomes.

</Predict>

Yet in an adult's body, nerve cells can pass on signals and muscle cells can contract: their shapes and functions differ. After dividing, cells become different in shape, structure and function; this is called <Term t="biology.cell-differentiation">cell differentiation</Term>. From junior high: cells of similar shape and function group together into tissues, and tissues make up organs.

No. They were all produced from the zygote by mitosis, and mitosis gives each daughter cell the same chromosomes as its parent, so a person's nerve cells and skin cells both have 46 chromosomes and the same genetic material. What changes is the cells' shape, structure and function, not their number of chromosomes.
