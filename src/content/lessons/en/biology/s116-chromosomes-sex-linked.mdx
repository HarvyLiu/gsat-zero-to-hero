## Hereditary factors are on chromosomes

Around 1900, Mendel's work came back to scientists' attention. By then they could already see <Term t="biology.chromosome">chromosomes</Term> under the microscope as cells divided. Around 1902–1903, the American Walter Sutton <Zh>薩登</Zh> studied <Term t="biology.meiosis">meiosis</Term> in grasshoppers and noticed that chromosomes behave almost exactly like Mendel's hereditary factors:

| Mendel's hereditary factors | Homologous chromosomes |
|---|---|
| Paired in body cells, one from the father's side and one from the mother's | Paired in body cells, one from the father's side and one from the mother's |
| The two factors of a pair separate when gametes form | The two homologous chromosomes of a pair separate in meiosis |
| Each gamete carries one factor of each pair | Each gamete carries one chromosome of each pair |
| Paired again after fertilisation | Paired again in the zygote |
| Different pairs of factors assort independently | Different pairs of homologous chromosomes go to gametes independently |

So he proposed that **hereditary factors are on the chromosomes**. This idea is called the chromosome theory of inheritance <Zh>遺傳的染色體學說</Zh>. From then on, a hereditary factor was no longer just an abstract term inferred from ratios, but a real thing on the chromosomes inside cells. Today we call it a gene.

Count the chromosomes in each gamete on the right: always one long and one short, so each carries exactly one of $A$ and $a$ and exactly one of $B$ and $b$. That is the law of segregation. Which short chromosome goes with which long one can happen two ways, equally likely. One meiosis in one cell uses only one of the two ways; over many cells, the four gametes $AB$, $Ab$, $aB$ and $ab$ are each $\frac14$, which is the law of independent assortment. Mendel's two laws are simply how homologous chromosomes behave in meiosis.

## The discovery of sex chromosomes

You learned in junior high that human sex is mainly decided by the sex chromosomes. A human body cell has 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs are the same in males and females and are called autosomes <Zh>體染色體</Zh>; the other pair are the <Term t="biology.sex-chromosome">sex chromosomes</Term>, two X chromosomes in a female (XX) and one X and one smaller Y in a male (XY). "The cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis" said that the two chromosomes of a homologous pair have the same shape and size; a male's X and Y are the exception: they differ in size, yet in meiosis they still separate from each other like a homologous pair.

How were sex chromosomes discovered? Around 1905, the American woman scientist Nettie Stevens <Zh>史蒂文斯</Zh> looked at the chromosomes of mealworm beetles <Zh>黃粉蟲</Zh>: in females, the chromosomes all formed pairs of matching size, but males had one pair made of one large and one small chromosome. She inferred that this pair had to do with sex. At about the same time, the American Edmund B. Wilson <Zh>威爾遜</Zh> reached a similar conclusion in other insects.

<Predict id="sperm-y" prompt="Of all the sperm one man makes, about what percentage carry a Y chromosome?" answer={50} unit="%">

When a man makes sperm, X and Y separate like a homologous pair, and each goes into half the sperm, so half the sperm carry X and half carry Y: $50\%$ carry Y. A woman's two sex chromosomes are both X, so every egg carries X. Whether a child is a boy or a girl is decided by the sperm that joins the egg.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="Boy or girl: a Punnett square and multiplied probabilities">

Treat the sex chromosomes like a pair of "alleles" and use last chapter's Punnett square:

| Egg \ Sperm | X | Y |
|---|---|---|
| X | XX (girl) | XY (boy) |

Each cell is $\frac12$, so boys : girls = $1:1$. You can also multiply probabilities: a boy needs "the egg carries X, and the sperm carries Y". The egg carries X with probability $1$ and the sperm carries Y with probability $\frac12$, so the probability of a boy is

$$
1\times\frac12=\frac12
$$

Both methods say each child has an equal chance of being a boy or a girl.

</Perspective>

## Morgan's white-eyed fruit flies

Around 1910, the American Thomas Hunt Morgan <Zh>摩根</Zh> raised large numbers of fruit flies in his laboratory. Female fruit flies are XX and males XY, and their normal eyes are red. Among the red-eyed flies he found one white-eyed male. He mated it with a red-eyed female, and F1 was all red-eyed. When the F1 flies mated with each other, white eyes appeared again in F2.

```dialogue
T: White eyes vanish in F1 and come back in F2, much like Mendel's white flowers. But Morgan noticed something the pea experiments never showed.
T: What sex are the white-eyed F2 flies?
>> turn: q-white
S: All male! If the eye-colour gene were on an ordinary chromosome, there should be white-eyed flies of both sexes.
T: So Morgan inferred that the eye-colour gene is on the X chromosome, and the Y chromosome doesn't have it.
```

Write $X^W$ for an X carrying the red-eye allele and $X^w$ for an X carrying the white-eye allele; red is dominant. The white-eyed male is $X^wY$ and the red-eyed female $X^WX^W$. The F1 females are $X^WX^w$ and the F1 males $X^WY$, all red-eyed. Mating F1 with F1:

| Egg \ Sperm | $X^W$ | $Y$ |
|---|---|---|
| $X^W$ | $X^WX^W$ (female, red) | $X^WY$ (male, red) |
| $X^w$ | $X^WX^w$ (female, red) | $X^wY$ (male, white) |

Each cell is $\frac14$. Taken together, red : white = $3:1$, just as the law of segregation says; but white eyes appear only in the male column. A male fly has only one X, so whatever allele that X carries is the one he shows. Morgan's experiment placed a particular gene on a particular chromosome, and it was among the strongest early evidence for the chromosome theory of inheritance.

## Sex-linked inheritance: red-green colour blindness

When a gene is on a sex chromosome, so that a trait's inheritance depends on sex, this is <Term t="biology.sex-linked-inheritance">sex-linked inheritance</Term>. Human <Term t="biology.colour-blindness">red-green colour blindness</Term> is an example: its gene is on the X chromosome, and colour blindness ($X^a$) is recessive to normal colour vision ($X^A$). The biology sample items in the CEEC exam specification <Zh>考試說明</Zh> use this notation too.

| Sex | Genotype | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| Female | $X^AX^A$ | normal |
| Female | $X^AX^a$ | normal, but carries the colour-blind allele (a carrier) |
| Female | $X^aX^a$ | red-green colour-blind |
| Male | $X^AY$ | normal |
| Male | $X^aY$ | red-green colour-blind |

A woman is colour-blind only if both her X chromosomes carry $X^a$; a man has only one X, so a single $X^a$ makes him colour-blind. That is why red-green colour blindness is more common in men than in women.

Back to the opening family: the son is $X^aY$; his Y came from his father, so his X can only have come from his mother. She has normal colour vision yet passed on $X^a$, so she is a carrier, $X^AX^a$; the father is $X^AY$. This is the same pairing as Morgan's F1 flies mated with each other: half the sons are colour-blind, and all the daughters have normal vision, half of them carriers.

<Predict id="carrier-daughters" prompt="A different family: the father is red-green colour-blind (XaY), and the mother has normal vision and carries no colour-blind allele (XAXA). What percentage of their daughters are carriers?" answer={100} unit="%">

The father has only one X, which is $X^a$, and he passes it to **every** daughter; the mother always gives $X^A$. So every daughter is $X^AX^a$: $100\%$ are carriers, all with normal colour vision. Each son gets Y from his father and $X^A$ from his mother, so every son is $X^AY$ with normal vision. The colour blindness can't be seen in this generation, yet it may reappear in the next, in a daughter's sons.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="No table: think about sons and daughters separately">

The quickest way through sex-linked inheritance is to think about sons and daughters separately, because their X chromosomes come from different places:

- **Sons**: the X comes from the mother and the Y from the father. So whether a son is colour-blind depends **only on the mother**. If she is $X^AX^a$, each son is colour-blind with probability $\frac12$.
- **Daughters**: one X comes from the father and one from the mother, so you have to look at both parents. In the opening family every daughter has normal colour vision; check it in the left half of the area model.

When a question asks "among all the children", you must also multiply by the probability of a son, $\frac12$. In the opening family, a child who is a colour-blind son needs "is a son, and the mother gives $X^a$":

$$
\frac12\times\frac12=\frac14
$$

"$\frac12$ of the sons are colour-blind" and "colour-blind sons are $\frac14$ of the children" are both right; they differ in what the whole is. In the area model, the first is the share of red within the right half, the second the share of red in the whole square.

</Perspective>

## Reading a pedigree: could it be sex-linked?

Sex-linked inheritance isn't always recessive. If the allele for a trait is on X and is dominant, one copy is enough for the trait to show; this is X-linked dominant inheritance. This chapter covers it only because a reason that fully rules out sex-linked inheritance has to rule it out too (Question 50 of the 112 GSAT, below, asks for exactly that). So a pedigree has at least four hypotheses: autosomal dominant or recessive, and X-linked dominant or recessive. Pedigrees are drawn as in the last chapter: squares are males and circles females.

So only autosomal recessive is left: both parents are $Aa$ and the daughter is $aa$. For this family the reason can be written: "A daughter has the trait but her father doesn't, so it is not X-linked recessive."

<StudentAsks q="Can a daughter be red-green colour-blind?">

Yes, but the condition is stricter: both her X chromosomes must be $X^a$. One comes from her father, so he must be colour-blind ($X^aY$); the other comes from her mother, so the mother must carry at least one $X^a$. In other words, a father with normal colour vision can't have a colour-blind daughter.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Try all four hypotheses">

Above we used quick rules. When unsure, use last chapter's method: for each hypothesis, try every genotype each person could have:

| Hypothesis | Father (no) | Mother (no) | Daughter (yes) | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Autosomal dominant | $aa$ | $aa$ | needs $A$; neither parent has one | contradiction |
| Autosomal recessive | $Aa$ | $Aa$ | $aa$ | fits |
| X-linked dominant | $X^aY$ | $X^aX^a$ | needs $X^A$; neither parent has one | contradiction |
| X-linked recessive | $X^AY$ | $X^AX^A$ or $X^AX^a$ | gets $X^A$ from her father, so can't have the trait | contradiction |

The son without the trait also fits the one hypothesis that works ($AA$ or $Aa$). Trying them one at a time is slow but misses nothing; the quick rules are just this table boiled down:

- **X-linked recessive**: a daughter with the trait must have a father with it; a mother with the trait must have only sons with it.
- **X-linked dominant**: a father with the trait must have only daughters with it.

These rules can only **rule out**: if one is broken, the hypothesis is impossible; if none is broken, you can only say "possible".

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Can a pedigree prove that a trait is sex-linked?">

Usually not. A pedigree can only rule out hypotheses that don't fit; whatever is left is only "possible". In family 1, X-linked dominant and autosomal dominant both fit. To be more certain you need data from more families, or, as Morgan did, planned matings that show whether the offspring's traits are tied to sex.

</StudentAsks>
