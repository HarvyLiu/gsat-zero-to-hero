## Nucleic acids are the genetic material

Required biology makes junior high's "genetic material" precise: it is DNA, one of the <Term t="chemistry.nucleic-acid">nucleic acids</Term>. It carries genetic information; it is replicated before a cell divides and shared out to the daughter cells, and passed on through gametes to the next generation.

Before replication, one chromosome contains one very long DNA molecule, and one DNA molecule carries many genes. A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries a particular piece of genetic information. To sharpen last chapter's claim: a gene is a stretch of the DNA molecule in a chromosome.

Cells contain two kinds of nucleic acid, DNA and RNA. The chemistry chapter "Organic substances in daily life" showed that nucleic acids are built from nucleotides, and each nucleotide has a phosphate, a five-carbon sugar and a nitrogen-containing base. The two nucleic acids differ like this:

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Five-carbon sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Nitrogen-containing bases | four: A, T, C, G | four: A, U, C, G |
| Usual form | two long strands wound round each other (a double helix) | one strand |
| Role in the cell | the genetic material, storing genetic information | carries information transcribed from DNA, used to make proteins |

<StudentAsks q="Cells have both DNA and RNA. So which one is the genetic material?">

For all cellular life, prokaryotic or eukaryotic, it is DNA; RNA is the working copy a cell makes to use DNA's information. Some viruses, such as the one that causes COVID-19, have RNA as their genetic material (Questions 46–48 of the 111 GSAT used viral RNA), but viruses are not cells. Question 5 of the 114 GSAT looked like a question about viruses, but the CEEC <Zh>大考中心</Zh> says it tested the two kinds of nucleic acid in living things, so keep them apart.

## Hands on: crude DNA extraction

The curriculum notes say this inquiry activity should avoid chicken blood, so we use kiwi fruit here. Question 33 of the 115 GSAT was a follow-up on a kiwi crude DNA extraction. Every step has a purpose:

| Step | What you do | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chop and mash the kiwi | Break up the tissue and the cell walls so the cell contents come out easily |
| 2 | Add salt water containing detergent and stir gently | The detergent dissolves the cell membrane and nuclear envelope, which contain lipids, so the DNA leaves the nucleus; the salt helps the DNA dissolve in the solution |
| 3 | Filter | Remove the pulp and cell debris, keeping a filtrate that contains the DNA |
| 4 | Add pineapple juice | The enzyme in the juice breaks down the protein wrapped around the DNA |
| 5 | Slowly pour cold alcohol down the side of the glass | DNA does not dissolve in alcohol, so it comes out as white strands where the two liquids meet, and you can wind it onto a glass rod |

<StudentAsks q="Is that white stringy clump in the alcohol layer one strand of DNA?">

No. One DNA molecule is extremely thin, too thin for the naked eye. What you see is many DNA molecules clumped together, with some other substances mixed in. That is why it is called a "crude" extraction: you get a mixture containing a lot of DNA, not pure DNA.

</StudentAsks>

## The central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein

Genetic information shows up as a trait through proteins: many cell structures are made of proteins, and so are most enzymes. How the information gets from DNA to protein is the <Term t="biology.central-dogma">central dogma</Term>:

- **DNA replication**: using the existing DNA as the template, DNA identical to it is copied. Eukaryotic cells replicate their DNA in interphase, before dividing.
- <Term t="biology.transcription">Transcription</Term>: using the information of one gene on DNA as the template, RNA is made.
- <Term t="biology.translation">Translation</Term>: following the information carried by RNA, <Term t="chemistry.amino-acid">amino acids</Term> are joined one after another into a <Term t="chemistry.protein">protein</Term>.

Replication and transcription both need a <Term t="biology.polymerase">polymerase</Term>: replication uses DNA polymerase and transcription uses RNA polymerase. The curriculum notes say: for DNA replication, name no enzyme or protein except polymerase.

<Perspective later title="Replication and transcription in base letters" needs="base pairing; required biology covers only the ideas, not the mechanisms of replication and transcription">

DNA's two strands are held together by base pairing: A with T, C with G. If one strand reads TACGGA, the other reads ATGCCT. In replication the strands separate and each is a template for a new partner strand, giving two DNA molecules like the original.

In transcription, one strand of a gene is the template for pairing up RNA, with U in place of T: the template TACGGA gives the RNA AUGCCU. So RNA's information matches the gene.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Another view: prokaryotic cells have no nucleus">

Prokaryotic cells such as bacteria have no nucleus; their DNA is in the cytoplasm, but they do have ribosomes. So the places have to be worked out again:

| | Eukaryotic cell | Prokaryotic cell (such as a bacterium) |
|---|---|---|
| Where the DNA is | mainly in the nucleus | in the cytoplasm, not wrapped in a nuclear envelope |
| DNA replication, transcription | nucleus | cytoplasm |
| Translation | ribosomes in the cytoplasm | ribosomes in the cytoplasm |

The rule is just two sentences: **replication and transcription follow the DNA**, happening wherever the DNA is; **translation follows the ribosomes**.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="An analogy: library, copy and kitchen">

The nucleus is a library that never lends its books, and DNA is its set of recipes. To cook, you copy the page you need: that is transcription, and the copy is RNA. The kitchen (a ribosome) follows the copy to make the dish: that is translation, and the dish is a protein.

The analogy breaks twice. The copy is not a photocopy: RNA is a different molecule, with U in place of T. And replication is not transcription: replication copies the whole set once, before division; transcription copies one gene, often many times.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="All the cells in one person have the same DNA. So why do muscle cells and nerve cells look completely different?">

Body cells all come from the zygote by mitosis, so their DNA is the same (with a few exceptions: mature mammalian red blood cells have no nucleus). But different cells transcribe and translate different genes, make different proteins and so take different forms and functions: the <Term t="biology.cell-differentiation">cell differentiation</Term> of "The cell cycle, mitosis and meiosis". How a cell chooses its genes is gene control, which required biology leaves out; exams give it as data.

</StudentAsks>

## One trait, different expressions

The same trait can show differently in different individuals, for two kinds of reason:

1. **Genetic variation**: in junior high you learned that the genetic material can change. The different versions of a gene are its <Term t="biology.allele">alleles</Term>; they may make different proteins and so show different traits, such as purple and white pea flowers. Genetic variation in reproductive cells is passed on to the offspring.
2. **Environmental factors**: the same genotype may show different traits in different environments. Identical twins, for example, have the same genotype, yet if they grow up in different environments, traits such as body weight can still differ.

Questions 37–39 of the 115 GSAT used a turtle whose egg incubation temperature affects the expression of certain genes and so decides whether a male or female hatches: the environment acting through gene expression.

Exams often write gene control into the data. Practise on a hypothetical data set: an insect's larvae all come from one true-breeding line, so they share one genotype. Researchers raised them at different temperatures and measured the relative RNA of genes P and Q. They also found that when P could not be expressed, Q was hardly expressed, and that larvae with a lot of Q grew up darker.

<Perspective title="Another view: a table for where a difference comes from">

When you compare two individuals, ask two things first: is the genotype the same? Is the environment the same?

| | Same environment | Different environment |
|---|---|---|
| Same genotype | the trait should be the same | the difference comes from the environment, e.g. identical twins raised in different environments |
| Different genotype | the difference comes from genetic variation, e.g. different varieties in one flower bed with different flower colours | either cause is possible; you can't tell them apart |

Only when one condition differs can you put the difference down to it. It is the same idea as changing one variable at a time in an experiment.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Is a difference caused by the environment passed on to the next generation?">

Usually not. What is inherited is variation in the genetic material of reproductive cells. Skin darkened by the sun does not change the DNA in reproductive cells, so their children are not born darker for it. In the turtle example, the genes pass on, not the temperature one egg was incubated at.

</StudentAsks>

## Transgenic technology: putting a gene into another organism

DNA made by joining DNA pieces from different sources is called <Term t="biology.recombinant-dna">recombinant DNA</Term>. Using methods like this to put a gene from one kind of organism into the cells of another, so that it shows the trait the gene brings, is <Term t="biology.transgenic-technology">transgenic technology</Term>. The curriculum asks only for its uses, not the mechanism of the technique.

It works because of the central dogma: almost all living things transcribe and translate DNA's information into protein by the same rules, so a bacterium given a human gene can make that protein. Common uses:

- **Medicine**: bacteria carrying the human insulin gene make human insulin in large amounts, for treating diabetes.
- **Farming**: a bacterial insect-resistance gene is put into a crop, so that the crop resists certain insect pests.
- **Ornamental fish and research**: a gene for a fluorescent protein put into fish gives glowing ornamental fish, and scientists experiment with transgenic animals and plants. The biology–chemistry group, Questions 40–43 of the 115 GSAT, used transgenic tobacco, and Question 23 of the 111 GSAT was a gene-transfer item.

<Perspective title="Another view: compared with cross-breeding">

Junior high taught that biotechnology brings benefits and problems. First, tell it apart from traditional breeding:

| | Cross-breeding | Transgenic technology |
|---|---|---|
| How a new trait is obtained | two parents are mated and offspring are selected | a particular gene is put straight into cells |
| Where the genes come from | parents of the same or a closely related species | can be a completely different organism, e.g. a bacterial gene put into a crop |
| How much is brought in at once | the parents' whole sets of genes recombine | only the chosen gene |

Because genes can cross species, society debates it, for example whether a transferred gene could spread to wild plants, and the safety and labelling of genetically modified food. To decide whether a procedure is transgenic, ask: was a gene from one kind of organism put into another?

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Optional: genes that move">

The curriculum lists this scientist's contributions as optional content. The American scientist Barbara McClintock <Zh>麥克林托克</Zh> studied heredity in maize and in the late 1940s found that some genes can change their position on the chromosomes, later called "jumping genes": genes are not always fixed in place. She won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1983.

</Perspective>
