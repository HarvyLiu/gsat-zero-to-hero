## ATP: the energy cells use directly

Muscle contraction, a cell building large molecules such as proteins, and cell division all need energy. The energy source these activities use directly is <Term t="biology.atp">ATP</Term>.

When ATP breaks down into ADP and phosphate, it releases energy, and the cell uses that energy to do work. The other way round, ADP and phosphate can only be rebuilt into ATP if energy is taken in. That energy comes mainly from respiration breaking down organic matter such as glucose.

$$
\mathrm{ATP} \longrightarrow \mathrm{ADP} + \text{phosphate} + \text{energy}
$$

Follow the arrows once round: ATP releases energy and becomes ADP and phosphate, then takes in energy from respiration and becomes ATP again. Look at the direction of the green and orange arrows: energy comes in at the bottom and leaves at the top. ATP only passes it along.

Teachers often call ATP "small change". Glucose is like a large banknote: it holds a lot of energy, but the cell can't pay for each small job with a banknote. Respiration changes the note into lots of coins (ATP), which are spent one at a time. Where the analogy breaks: coins can be saved, but ATP is not a long-term energy store. The ATP in a cell is used up and rebuilt all the time; long-term energy stores are organic substances such as carbohydrates and lipids.

<StudentAsks q="Glucose already has energy in it. Why doesn't the cell just use glucose instead of making ATP first?">

Because the energy source that cell activities are hooked up to is ATP. One molecule of glucose holds far more energy than one molecule of ATP; released all at once, most of it would be of no use to the cell. Respiration passes glucose's energy into many ATP molecules in batches, and wherever energy is needed, ATP is broken down there. So when an item asks for the **direct** energy source, the answer is ATP, not glucose and not sunlight.

</StudentAsks>

Read the figure: the red arrow going down is ATP breaking down, like an exothermic reaction in chemistry, with energy given out. The curriculum does not ask for ATP's structural formula, but the options of Question 32 of the 115 GSAT mentioned what ATP is made of, so here is a preview of the terms only. ATP is made of one nitrogenous base, adenine <Zh>腺嘌呤</Zh>, one five-carbon sugar, ribose <Zh>核糖</Zh>, and three phosphates. That is very like the unit of nucleic acids, the nucleotide (a phosphate, a five-carbon sugar and a nitrogenous base). The "TP" in the name is the three phosphates; ADP has one phosphate fewer. In eukaryotic cells, ATP is made mainly by respiration in the mitochondria.

</Perspective>

## Photosynthesis: storing light energy in organic matter

At the level of the cell, photosynthesis happens in the chloroplasts. They absorb light energy and use carbon dioxide and water to make organic matter such as glucose, giving off oxygen at the same time. In this way light energy is converted into chemical energy and stored in organic matter.

<Predict id="ce-o2-count" prompt="Write the starting materials of photosynthesis as 6 CO₂ and 6 H₂O. The products are 1 glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, and some O₂. Count the atoms: how many O₂ molecules are given off?" answer={6} unit="molecules">

Carbon: 6 on the left, 6 in glucose, which balances. Hydrogen: 12 on the left, 12 in glucose, which also balances. Oxygen: $6\times2+6=18$ on the left; glucose takes 6, leaving 12, which make $12\div2=6$ molecules of $\ce{O2}$.

</Predict>

As an overall equation:

$$
\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}
$$

The conditions left off the arrow are: light energy is needed, and it happens in the chloroplast. This is only the overall account of what goes in and what comes out. Photosynthesis is really a chain of reactions, and the details belong to the elective course.

<StudentAsks q="Is the glucose that plants make there for animals to eat?">

First of all, the plant uses it itself. Plant cells need energy too, so a plant uses some of its glucose for respiration and some to build its body, and stores the surplus as organic substances such as starch. Animals get this organic matter only indirectly, by eating plants or eating animals that ate plants. This is what elementary school called a "producer": the organic matter and chemical energy in an ecosystem come, at the start, mostly from producers' photosynthesis.

</StudentAsks>

## Respiration: passing the energy in organic matter on to ATP

Respiration breaks down organic matter such as glucose and releases the chemical energy in it. When oxygen is available, glucose is broken down completely into carbon dioxide and water; in eukaryotic cells this happens mainly in the mitochondria. Part of the energy released is used to make ATP, and the rest is lost as heat. The junior-high science curriculum notes say "respiration converts chemical energy into heat energy", and that refers to this part.

$$
\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O} + \text{energy (partly stored in ATP, the rest as heat)}
$$

Plant cells and animal cells both carry out respiration to get energy, by day and by night alike.

<StudentAsks q="Is respiration the same as breathing in and out with the lungs?">

No, they are different things. Breathing in and out with the lungs is **breathing movement**, which happens at the level of organs: it brings oxygen into the body and gets rid of carbon dioxide. The respiration in this chapter happens **inside every cell**: it is the chemical reaction that breaks down organic matter and releases energy. The two are linked: the oxygen that the lungs bring in is used in the end by the cells' respiration. Plants and bacteria have no lungs, yet their cells still carry out respiration.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Side by side: one table for both processes">

Put the two processes in the same table, and many items answer themselves:

| | Photosynthesis | Respiration (with oxygen) |
|---|---|---|
| Main site | chloroplast | mitochondrion (eukaryotic cells) |
| Starting materials | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$ | glucose, $\ce{O2}$ |
| Products | glucose, $\ce{O2}$ | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$ |
| Energy conversion | light energy → chemical energy (stored in organic matter) | chemical energy → chemical energy of ATP + heat |
| When it happens | when there is light | by day and by night |

How to use it: given a process, first ask whether it **takes in or gives off** $\ce{CO2}$, then whether energy is **stored in** organic matter or **released from** it. If the two answers agree, you know which process it is.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Term preview: fermentation" needs="respiration in elective biology" >

When there is not enough oxygen, some cells take another route: they break glucose down into smaller organic molecules that still hold quite a lot of energy, releasing only a little. This is <Term t="biology.fermentation">fermentation</Term> (CEEC's documents write it <Zh>發酵作用</Zh>). For example, yeast produces ethanol and carbon dioxide, and human muscle cells produce lactic acid during hard exercise when the oxygen supply falls short. The options of Question 29 of the 115 GSAT mentioned lactic acid and ethanol. Compared with respiration using oxygen, the products of fermentation still keep most of the chemical energy, so far less energy is released.

</Perspective>

## Enzymes: keeping the cell's reactions running

Photosynthesis and respiration are both chains of reactions, and every step needs the help of an <Term t="biology.enzyme">enzyme</Term>. Enzymes are biological catalysts made by cells, and most are proteins. They let reactions run fast enough under mild conditions such as body temperature, and they are not used up by the reaction. Elective textbooks call them <Zh>酶</Zh>.

<Perspective title="Enzymes compared with catalysts in chemistry">

In chemistry you met the <Term t="chemistry.catalyst">catalyst</Term>, for example manganese dioxide speeding up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. Compare it with an enzyme:

- **The same**: both speed up a reaction and are not used up by it; neither supplies the energy the reaction needs.
- **Different**: enzymes are made by living cells and most are proteins; they cannot stand high temperatures and stop working when overheated; and one enzyme usually catalyses only a particular reaction.

So "enzymes are the cell's energy source" is wrong: the energy comes from organic matter and ATP; enzymes only keep the reactions moving.

</Perspective>

## How the two processes connect

Join the last three sections together and you get one route for energy:

$$
\text{light energy} \xrightarrow{\text{photosynthesis}} \text{chemical energy in organic matter} \xrightarrow{\text{respiration}} \text{chemical energy in ATP} \longrightarrow \text{cell activities}
$$

At every conversion some energy is lost as heat. The food animals eat was also made, in the first place, by some producer's photosynthesis, so the energy for your muscle contraction comes, ultimately, from sunlight.

## Reading data: telling the two processes apart from CO₂ changes

Put a potted plant in a sealed transparent box and measure the amount of $\ce{CO2}$ in the box (relative values) with a sensor. In the dark, only respiration happens, and $\ce{CO2}$ keeps rising. In the light, both processes run at once, and the sensor sees only their combined, net change.

<Predict id="ce-gross-uptake" prompt="In the dark, the CO₂ in the box rises by 2 units per hour. With the light on, the CO₂ falls by 5 units per hour. Assuming respiration keeps the same rate with the light on, how many units of CO₂ per hour does photosynthesis actually take in?" answer={7} unit="units">

Put the two processes together:

$$
\text{observed}\ \ce{CO2}\ \text{change} = \text{given off by respiration} - \text{taken in by photosynthesis}
$$

With the light on, respiration still gives off 2 units per hour. The observed change is $-5$, so $2-\text{uptake}=-5$, and the uptake is $2+5=7$ units. Reading off only "falls by 5" misses the 2 units that respiration is giving off at the same time.

</Predict>

<Graph desc="The amount of CO₂ in the box (relative value) against time (hours, 0 to 6). From 0 to 3 hours the box is dark and the line rises straight from 10 to 16, a slope of 2 per hour. At hour 3 the light goes on, marked by a dashed line; from 3 to 6 hours the slope is 2 minus p, where p is the CO₂ photosynthesis takes in per hour. Drag p: when p is below 2 the line still rises, when p equals 2 it is flat, and when p is above 2 it falls." x={[-0.2, 6.3]} y={[0, 25]} height={300} equal={false}

<TryIt>

Drag $p$ to find the value that makes the right-hand part of the line **flat**, and write it down. Is the leaf photosynthesising at that point? Then drag $p$ to 7 and check that the slope of the right-hand part matches the "falls by 5 per hour" in the prediction above.

</TryIt>

The line goes flat at $p=2$: photosynthesis takes in exactly as much $\ce{CO2}$ as respiration gives off. Both are running; they just cancel each other out.

<StudentAsks q="When the line falls after the light goes on, does that mean the plant has stopped respiring?">

No. A falling line only means that photosynthesis takes in $\ce{CO2}$ **faster** than respiration gives it off. Respiration carries on the whole time; photosynthesis simply outweighs it. The other way round, a rising line does not necessarily mean there is no photosynthesis: in very weak light, photosynthesis can be slower than respiration, and the line still rises.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Keeping a ledger: treat the box as an account">

Think of the $\ce{CO2}$ in the box as a bank account: respiration makes "deposits", photosynthesis makes "withdrawals", and the sensor sees only the change in the balance.

| Situation | Deposit (respiration) | Withdrawal (photosynthesis) | Change in balance per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dark | 2 | 0 | $+2$ |
| Light on | 2 | 7 | $-5$ |
| Flat line | 2 | 2 | $0$ |

Every row satisfies "change = deposit − withdrawal". To find the actual photosynthesis, add the respiration column back onto the change you can see. You can check oxygen with the same table, with the roles swapped: photosynthesis gives off $\ce{O2}$ and respiration takes it in.

</Perspective>
