## Shares in a population change: the peppered moth

The <Term t="biology.peppered-moth">peppered moth</Term> (some books call it <Zh>樺尺蛾</Zh>) is a night-flying moth that rests on tree trunks by day. Its body colour comes in a light form and a dark form, and body colour is heritable.

During the Industrial Revolution in nineteenth-century Britain, soot from industrial areas blackened tree trunks, and in many places the share of dark moths kept rising. From the mid-twentieth century, as air pollution was reduced, the trunks got their colour back and the share of light moths rose again. This is the curriculum's example: **the shares of traits in a population change with environment and time.**

<StudentAsks q="Do the birds turn light moths dark?">

No. The birds only eat the moths that stand out; they don't change any moth's colour. Dark moths were there already, and their body colour comes from genetic variation; sooty trunks just make dark moths **more likely to survive and leave offspring**. Once the trunks turn light again, the advantage flips and the share of light moths rises. Which way the shares move depends on the environment at the time.

## How evolutionary ideas developed: from Lamarck to Darwin

Before the nineteenth century, many people held that species never change once they appear. But as you learned in junior high, fossils record many organisms that have disappeared and are not quite like those alive today. Do living things change? And if so, how?

The Frenchman Jean-Baptiste Lamarck <Zh>拉馬克</Zh>, in a book published in 1809, set out a systematic idea of evolution: living things change over time. He explained how the change happens with two claims:

1. <Term t="biology.use-and-disuse">Use and disuse</Term>: organs used often become more and more developed, and organs not used gradually waste away.
2. Inheritance of acquired traits: changes an individual gains during its life by using or not using its organs can be passed to its offspring.

Textbooks often explain this with the giraffe: a giraffe often stretches its neck to eat leaves high up, so the neck is stretched a little; this change is passed to its offspring, and building up over generations, the neck gets longer and longer.

```dialogue
T: Let's test Lamarck's second claim against today's genetics.
T: A laboratory cuts the tails of a group of mice short just after birth, lets them grow up and mate, and does the same in every generation for twenty generations. What will the tails of the newborn mice in the twenty-first generation be like?
>> turn: q-tail
S: Cutting the tail only changed the body, not the genes in the gametes, so the twenty-first generation's tails are still about as long as ordinary mice's.
T: Right. Changes made to the body after birth don't change the genetic material in the gametes, so they aren't inherited. Lamarck was right that living things change, but his explanation, the inheritance of acquired traits, doesn't hold.
```

The Englishman Charles Darwin <Zh>達爾文</Zh> sailed around the world on HMS *Beagle* <Zh>小獵犬號</Zh> from 1831 to 1836, observing living things and fossils in many places along the way, including the Galápagos Islands <Zh>加拉巴哥群島</Zh>. Back home, he spent more than twenty years gathering evidence. Another British naturalist, Alfred Russel Wallace <Zh>華萊士</Zh>, came up with the same mechanism independently, and papers by the two were presented together in 1858. In 1859 Darwin published *On the Origin of Species* <Zh>《物種原始》</Zh>, setting out his theory of evolution.

Read the timeline: how many years separate Lamarck's book from *On the Origin of Species*? Exactly 50. Evolutionary ideas were not thought up by one person all at once; scholar after scholar corrected older explanations with new observations.

Darwin's theory of evolution has two cores:

- **Common ancestry**: today's many different kinds of living things all came from common ancestors, changing gradually over many generations.
- <Term t="biology.natural-selection">Natural selection</Term>: explains **how** living things change. That is the next section's topic.

## Natural selection: the environment picks among differences already there

Darwin's natural selection can be split into four links:

1. **Individuals differ**: the individuals of one population don't have exactly the same traits, and some of the differences come from genetic variation and are heritable.
2. **Overproduction and the struggle for existence**: organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support, individuals compete for resources such as food and space, and not every one survives.
3. **Survival of the fittest**: individuals with traits better suited to the current environment are more likely to survive and leave more offspring.
4. **Building up over generations**: these individuals pass their heritable traits to their offspring, so the trait's share in the population rises generation by generation.

Back to the peppered moth: link 1 is that body colour comes in light and dark forms and is heritable; links 2 and 3 are that bird predation makes conspicuous moths less likely to survive; link 4 is the dark share rising generation after generation.

<StudentAsks q="Does natural selection mean 'only the strongest survive'?">

Not necessarily. "The fittest" means the individuals more likely to survive and leave more offspring **in the environment at the time**, not necessarily the biggest or strongest. The dark moths in a sooty wood weren't stronger, just less conspicuous. When the environment changes, so do the fittest. Note too: natural selection picks among individuals, but what changes is the **population**'s shares, and that takes many generations.

</StudentAsks>

Written link by link:

1. Before the drug was used, a few individuals in the bacterial population already carried a heritable resistance variation.
2. Once the antibiotic was used, most non-resistant bacteria died and the resistant ones were more likely to survive (struggle for existence, survival of the fittest).
3. The surviving resistant bacteria reproduced and passed resistance on to their offspring.
4. With repeated use, over many generations, the share of resistant bacteria in the population grew higher and higher. This is natural selection.

The marking guide shows the difference between ways of writing it. Question 57 of the 112 GSAT asked about antibiotic resistance in bacteria: natural selection (or "struggle for existence and survival of the fittest", or the other Chinese name for natural selection) earned full credit; only "mutation" or only "survival of the fittest" earned 1 point; "slowly adapting" scored 0. Writing only mutation leaves out the environment doing the selecting; writing only survival of the fittest leaves out that the differences were already there and are heritable; "slowly adapting" describes each individual changing itself, which is exactly Lamarck's kind of idea.

<StudentAsks q="Does the resistance mutation appear because the antibiotic was used?">

No. The antibiotic doesn't "order" bacteria to produce resistance variations; resistance variations may already exist in the population before the drug is used. What the antibiotic does is kill the non-resistant individuals and leave the resistant ones. So an explanation should say the differences were "already there", not that they "appeared after the drug was used" or that the bacteria "changed in order to survive".

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="One case, two explanations: Lamarck and Darwin">

Set the giraffe example side by side. Textbooks often use it to compare the two lines of reasoning; how giraffes' necks actually evolved is the subject of further research and other proposals, so here it is used only to see how the reasoning differs.

| Question | Lamarck (use and disuse, acquired traits) | Darwin (natural selection) |
|---|---|---|
| Where do the differences come from? | Each individual stretches its neck during its life, and the neck gets longer | The population already has individuals with longer and shorter necks, and the differences are heritable |
| Who changes? | Each individual lengthens itself | No individual changes; those with longer necks reach food more easily, survive and leave more offspring |
| How does it reach the next generation? | Changes acquired during life are passed on | Only heritable differences are passed on |
| How does the population change? | Every individual slowly lengthens | The share of long-necked individuals rises generation by generation |

To decide whether an explanation is natural selection, check three things: **Were the differences already there, and are they heritable? Do survival and reproduction differ? Does the change happen in the population, across generations?** Only when all three hold is it natural selection.

</Perspective>

## Natural and artificial selection

In *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin also drew comparisons with the animals people raise. In each generation people pick only the individuals they want to breed from; this is artificial selection. Broiler chickens are an example: generation after generation, people have picked fast-growing, meaty chickens to breed from. Questions 37–39 of the 113 GSAT used broiler chickens as their setting and dealt with artificial selection.

Drag $g$ from 0 to 4 and watch the curve's peak: each generation of selection moves the peak one step right, and the population's average weight grows. What is kept is the trait people want, not necessarily a trait that helps a chicken survive in the wild.

<Perspective title="Natural and artificial selection side by side">

| | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides which individuals leave offspring? | The environment (predators, drugs, climate, food and so on) | People |
| By what standard? | Whether they can survive and reproduce in the environment at the time | The traits people want, such as fast growth or high yield |
| Differences selected | Heritable differences already in the population | Same |
| Result | The share of traits suited to the current environment rises | The share of traits people want rises |

The mechanism is the same in both: **picking among heritable differences already there, changing the population over many generations.** The only difference is who does the picking. To decide which one an example is, ask: do people decide which individuals breed, by a trait they want? If so, it's artificial selection; otherwise it's natural selection. For example, a hospital uses the antibiotic, but nobody picks the resistant bacteria they "want" to breed; the resistant bacteria are left by the drug as their environment, so it is still natural selection.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Does artificial selection count as evolution?">

Yes. Evolution is about heritable trait shares in a population changing across generations. The share of "fast-growing" in a broiler population rises generation after generation, so the population really has changed; it's just that people, not the natural environment, do the picking. Darwin used examples of artificial selection precisely to help readers imagine that natural selection could also bring about large changes in nature.

</StudentAsks>
