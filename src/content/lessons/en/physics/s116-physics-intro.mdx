## How scientists approach a question

The curriculum describes the way scientists think as a few attitudes <Zh>科學態度</Zh>:

- **Rational** <Zh>理性</Zh>: a conclusion comes from evidence and reasoning, and you can give a reason for every step.
- **Objective** <Zh>客觀</Zh>: record all the data truthfully and let the conclusion follow the evidence, instead of bending the evidence to your expectation; publish the methods and data so others can repeat the test.
- **Curious** <Zh>好奇</Zh>: ask "why" about what you see, and turn the question into one an experiment can test.
- **Slow to jump to conclusions** <Zh>避免倉促下結論</Zh>: when the evidence is not enough, hold back your judgement and collect more data.

These attitudes matter most when you read data.

**Example**　Back to the opening pendulum. Ming measures two more lengths. Here are the four data points (periods rounded to $0.1\ \mathrm{s}$):

| length $L$ ($\mathrm{m}$) | $0.25$ | $1.00$ | $2.25$ | $4.00$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| period $T$ ($\mathrm{s}$) | $1.0$ | $2.0$ | $3.0$ | $4.0$ |

Was his straight-line prediction right?

Using only the first two points, the line has slope $\frac{2.0-1.0}{1.00-0.25}=\frac{4}{3}$, so the line is $T=\frac{4}{3}L+\frac{2}{3}$. At $L=4.00$ it gives $T=6.0\ \mathrm{s}$, but the measured period is $4.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Plot all four points and they lie on a **curved** line: within the precision of these data, $T=2\sqrt{L}$. Making the length $4$ times as long only makes the period $2$ times as long.

The graph shows it at a glance: near the first two points the line and the curve almost coincide, so two data points simply cannot tell a line from a curve. Only when you widen the range and measure more points does the difference appear. That is why two data points are not enough for a conclusion.

<Perspective title="Plot it another way: square the period and the curve becomes a line">

A curved line is hard to judge, so physicists often plot a different quantity to make the relationship straight. Square each period:

| length $L$ ($\mathrm{m}$) | $0.25$ | $1.00$ | $2.25$ | $4.00$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $T^2$ ($\mathrm{s^2}$) | $1.0$ | $4.0$ | $9.0$ | $16.0$ |

Every column has $T^2=4L$: the four points lie on a straight line **through the origin**. A line through the origin means "proportional", so when $L$ becomes $4$ times as large, $T^2$ becomes $4$ times as large and $T$ becomes $\sqrt{4}=2$ times as large, the same result you read off the curve above.

This view gives you one more check: if a data point sits clearly off this line, go back and check that measurement instead of simply deleting it. When you study planetary motion later, you will see the same trick: plot the data a different way and the pattern becomes obvious.

<StudentAsks q="In maths, two points determine a line. Why can't I use that?">

Two points do determine **one** straight line, but that only says there is exactly one straight line through those two points. It does not say the two quantities are related by a straight line. Infinitely many curves pass through the same two points, and $T=2\sqrt{L}$ above is one of them. The only way to find the real relationship is more data.

</StudentAsks>

One more favourite exam point: change one condition at a time. To find out whether the bob's mass affects the period, keep the length and the angle the same and change only the bob. If you actually do this, you find that for small angles a heavier bob leaves the period almost unchanged. If you change the length and the mass together, a change in period cannot be pinned on either one.

## What physics covers and how it grew

Physics studies matter, energy and the interactions between them, on every scale from the atomic nucleus to the whole universe. It is traditionally divided into mechanics, heat, waves and optics, electromagnetism, and the modern physics of the twentieth century (atoms, nuclei, quantum phenomena, relativity). Research today also includes astrophysics, condensed-matter physics (the study of solids and semiconductors), particle physics, and fields that join physics with biology and medicine.

The curriculum points out that today's big challenges are often **cross-disciplinary**. Energy and the environment, medical imaging, and semiconductors and information technology all need physics to work together with chemistry, biology, engineering and other fields.

The history of physics is not a list of names but a chain of stories: someone discovers something, and others go on to explain or test it. The timeline below shows the discoveries this chapter uses:

## Experiment and theory support each other

Physics walks on two legs. **Experiment** observes and measures; **theory** explains known results and predicts things not yet seen. They support each other, and either one can take the first step.

| when | who | what they did | experiment or theory |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1831 | Faraday | found that changing magnetism can produce a current (electromagnetic induction) | experiment |
| 1860s | Maxwell | built a theory unifying electricity and magnetism and predicted electromagnetic waves | theory |
| late 1880s | Hertz | produced and detected electromagnetic waves by experiment | experiment |
| 1938 | Hahn, Strassmann | bombarded uranium with neutrons and found the lighter element barium among the products | experiment |
| 1939 | Meitner, Frisch | explained it as the uranium nucleus splitting into two lighter nuclei (nuclear fission) | theory |
| 1956 | Lee Tsung-Dao, Yang Chen-Ning | proposed that some radioactive decays might not respect mirror symmetry | theory |
| 1956–1957 | Wu Chien-Shiung | led the experiment that confirmed this prediction | experiment |

**Example**　Use the figure: which stories went "experiment first, then a theory explained it"? Which went "theory predicted first, then an experiment tested it"?

Look at each story's **first arrow**. Faraday's and Hahn's stories start in the lower row with an upward arrow: experiment came first. Lee and Yang's story starts in the upper row with a downward arrow: theory came first. Faraday's story is the most complete: experiment → theory → new experiment. Maxwell's theory first explained the known electromagnetic effects, then predicted electromagnetic waves, and finally Hertz confirmed them by experiment.

In this picture the three stories simply "enter the same cycle at different places":

1. Faraday's story enters at "experimental results" and goes all the way round: experiment → Maxwell's theory → the prediction of electromagnetic waves → Hertz's experiment.
2. Hahn and Strassmann's story also enters at "experimental results" and moves on to "theory": Meitner and Frisch's explanation of fission.
3. Lee and Yang's story enters at "theory" and "prediction" and moves on to "test": Wu's experiment.

The point of the cycle is that **there is no last step**: the result of a test becomes a new experimental result, which may support the theory or force it to change. That is what "experiment and theory support each other" means.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Once a theory passes an experimental test, is it right forever?">

No. A theory that has passed test after test is very reliable, but scientific conclusions always stay open to new evidence: if reliable results someday disagree with it, the theory may be revised or folded into a larger theory. The story of Lee, Yang and Wu is exactly this: a symmetry that many physicists had believed in was overturned by experiment in certain decays.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Symmetry and conservation: Noether's theorem" needs="Conservation of energy (the later energy conservation chapter)" href="/en/physics/s116-energy-conservation/">

The mirror-symmetry story shows how much symmetry matters in physics. Physics has another deep idea: **symmetry** is linked to **conservation**. In 1918 the German mathematician Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of the laws of physics corresponds to a conserved quantity. For example, laws of physics that do not change with time correspond to conservation of energy. When you reach the chapter on conservation of energy, think back to this. The theorem is not tested on the GSAT <Zh>學測</Zh>.

</Perspective>

## Physicists from different backgrounds

The curriculum reminds us that physics was built by people of different genders, backgrounds and ethnic groups. This is not a separate unit but a thread that runs through every later chapter. This chapter's stories already include:

- **Faraday**: English; he had almost no formal schooling, started as a bookbinder's apprentice, and became one of the most important experimental physicists.
- **Marie Curie**: born in Poland, she researched radioactivity in France. She received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903, the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize, and the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911.
- **Lise Meitner**: born in Austria, she left Germany in 1938 and the next year, with Frisch, proposed the explanation of nuclear fission. The 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Hahn alone.
- **Wu Chien-Shiung**: born in China, she moved to the United States in the 1930s, became known for her precise experiments, and led the experiment that confirmed Lee and Yang's prediction. Lee and Yang were also both born in China, and the two received the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics.

You can find all of them on the timeline above.

The point of these stories is not to memorise years but to see how science moves forward. The scientific attitude is also tested inside contexts: among the sample items in the CEEC's exam specification <Zh>考試說明</Zh>, one item set is built around the coloured sheen on the surface of cut meat, and one of its questions is tagged PMc-Ⅴc-3. So when you read a question, ask yourself: is there enough evidence for this conclusion? Which conditions were controlled? Is the experiment testing a theory, or is the theory explaining an experiment?
