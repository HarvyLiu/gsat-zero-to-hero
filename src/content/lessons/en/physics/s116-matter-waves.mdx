## Young's double slit again: fringes are a wave's fingerprint

As reviewed in [Wave properties, the Doppler effect and light as a wave](/en/physics/s116-waves-light/): in the early 1800s Thomas Young in England used <Term t="physics.double-slit">double-slit interference</Term> to support the wave theory of light. After light of one colour passes through two close slits, by <Term t="physics.huygens-principle">Huygens' principle</Term> each slit acts as a new source, and the two waves overlap on the screen:

- Where the two waves arrive **crest on crest** (or trough on trough), they reinforce: a **bright fringe**.
- Where they arrive **crest on trough**, they cancel: a **dark fringe**.

Light added to light can make darkness. Light pictured as tiny balls (the corpuscular theory) can't do that, so alternating bright and dark fringes are a wave's fingerprint. What the GSAT asks for is qualitative: <Term t="physics.interference">interference</Term> fringes mean a wave side; and **the longer the wavelength, the farther apart the fringes**.

The figure below finds the bright fringes with wavefronts. Draw a set of concentric circles around each slit; each circle is a crest, and neighbouring circles are one wavelength apart.

1. Where circles from the two sets **cross**, crests from both waves arrive together and reinforce.
2. Where a circle of one set lies **exactly halfway** between two circles of the other, crest meets trough and they cancel.
3. Join up the crossing points and you get a few "reinforcement lines" fanning out from the slits. Where they hit the screen are the bright fringes; between them are cancellation lines, which hit the screen at the dark fringes.

<Perspective title="Look at one point on the screen: in step they reinforce, half a wavelength out of step they cancel">

Here is another angle: instead of the whole picture, look at **one point** on the screen. Each of the two waves makes that point move up and down, and the point's motion is the sum of the two.

The figure below shows two identical waves (blue and orange) and their sum (red). Drag $s$ to push the orange wave to the right:

- $s=0$: the two waves are exactly in step, crest on crest, and the amplitude of the sum is $2$ times as large: that point is a bright fringe.
- $s=0.5$: half a wavelength out of step, crest meets trough, and the sum is almost a flat line: that point is a dark fringe.
- $s=1$: a whole wavelength out of step, back in step again: a bright fringe again.

Different points on the screen are at different distances from the two slits, so the two waves are out of step by different amounts, and bright and dark alternate. This is the same thing that counting circles shows: where circles cross, the two waves arrive in step.

<Perspective later title="Telling bright from dark by path difference" needs="elective physics (waves, light and sound): path difference and interference fringes, not on the GSAT">

The difference between a point's distances to the two slits is called the **path difference**. If it is a whole number of wavelengths ($0$, $1$, $2$, … times), the waves arrive crest on crest: a bright fringe. If it is a half-integer number ($0.5$, $1.5$, $2.5$, … times), crest meets trough: a dark fringe. So telling bright from dark takes one step: divide the path difference by the wavelength.

For example, with light of wavelength $450\ \mathrm{nm}$, point $P$ on the screen has a path difference of $1350\ \mathrm{nm}$ and point $Q$ has $1125\ \mathrm{nm}$. $P$: $\dfrac{1350}{450}=3$, a whole number, so it is bright. $Q$: $\dfrac{1125}{450}=2.5$, a half-integer, so it is dark.

This agrees with counting circles: a crossing point is a whole number of circles from each slit, so the difference is a whole number of wavelengths; a halfway point differs by a half-integer number of wavelengths.

<StudentAsks q="Do I need to calculate the distance between fringes?">

No. Required physics only asks for the qualitative picture: bright fringes are where the two waves reinforce, dark fringes where they cancel. From the wavefront figure above you can see that **the longer the wavelength, the farther apart the fringes**, and you should be able to judge that trend. Locating bright and dark fringes by path difference, and the quantitative formula for fringe spacing, both belong to elective physics and are not needed for the required part of the GSAT.

</StudentAsks>

## The electron double slit: electrons leave the wave fingerprint too

An electron is a charged particle with a definite mass and charge. When Joseph John Thomson in England discovered it in 1897, it was treated as a tiny particle. Yet if you fire a beam of electrons at two very narrow, very close slits, the fluorescent screen behind them shows **alternating bright and dark fringes**, exactly like light in Young's experiment. This <Term t="physics.double-slit">electron double-slit interference</Term> experiment works on the same idea as Young's: only waves can cancel in some places, so **electrons behave as waves**.

More striking still, turn the beam down until only one electron at a time flies through. Each electron leaves **one dot** on the screen; only as the dots pile up, one by one, do they arrange themselves into fringes. In 1989 a team led by Akira Tonomura at Hitachi in Japan made images of exactly this: fringes building up from electrons sent one at a time.

This experiment was tested in Question 25 of the 114 GSAT <Zh>114學測第25題</Zh>. When you read such a question, hold on to two things: **one electron → one dot** (particle side), **many electrons → interference fringes** (wave side).

The idea that electrons are waves was first put forward in the 1920s by Louis de Broglie in France: if light, a wave, can show a particle side, then the electron, a particle, should also have a wave side. Such waves are called **matter waves**. De Broglie first studied history at university before turning to physics. In the late 1920s Davisson and Germer in the United States, and George Paget Thomson in Britain, each sent electrons onto crystals or through thin metal films and saw the electrons <Term t="physics.diffraction">diffract</Term>, confirming their wave behaviour. Nicely, George Paget Thomson was the son of Joseph John Thomson, who discovered the electron: the father established the electron as a particle, and the son confirmed it as a wave. Physicists from France, England, the United States and Japan, from different countries and backgrounds, carried the idea from a bold hypothesis to an experimental fact.

<Perspective title="By counting: open a second slit, and the dark fringes get fewer electrons">

Here is another angle: instead of looking at the pattern, count how many electrons per minute reach one point. Take point $C$ at the very centre of the screen. With only slit A open, about $30$ electrons per minute reach $C$; with only slit B open, also about $30$.

- **If electrons were only tiny balls**: with both slits open, the electrons from A and from B simply add, so about $30+30=60$ per minute reach $C$. Every point can only gain, never lose.
- **If electrons are waves**: the two waves overlap. $C$ is the same distance from both slits, so the waves arrive crest on crest and reinforce, and more than $60$ electrons reach $C$. At a dark fringe, though, crest meets trough and the waves cancel, and the count is almost $0$: fewer than the $30$ you get with one slit. The total number of electrons does not change: the electrons missing from the dark fringes have gone to the bright ones.

Experiments show the second picture: at some places, **opening a second slit means fewer electrons**. Balls can't do that; only waves can. The figure below plots the two predictions side by side.

<StudentAsks q="If only one electron goes at a time, which slit did it go through?">

That is exactly the question Newtonian mechanics cannot answer. In quantum theory, an electron in flight does not have one definite path the way a ball does. If you add a device beside the slits to find out which slit each electron takes, the interference fringes disappear and only a ball-like spread is left. So the fringes are not caused by electrons colliding with each other; they come from each electron's own wave behaviour. The GSAT only asks you to know that electrons behave as waves, not to explain the quantum-mechanical details.

<Perspective later title="How long is an electron's wavelength? Why a baseball doesn't interfere" needs="the de Broglie wavelength from elective physics (electromagnetism II and quantum phenomena)">

De Broglie gave the wavelength of a matter wave as $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{p}$, where $h\approx 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}$ is Planck's constant and $p=mv$ is momentum.

- An electron: $m\approx 9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$. If $v=1.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, then $p\approx 9.11\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ and $\lambda\approx\dfrac{6.63\times10^{-34}}{9.11\times10^{-25}}\approx 7.3\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$, about the size of an atom (about $10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$). That is why the neatly ordered atoms of a crystal can make electrons diffract.
- A baseball: $m=0.15\ \mathrm{kg}$, $v=40\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so $p=6\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$ and $\lambda\approx 1.1\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{m}$, more than ten orders of magnitude smaller than a nucleus. No slit could ever make it show interference, so a baseball appears to move exactly as Newtonian mechanics says.

The required part of the GSAT only asks for the qualitative fact that electrons have a wave side. This formula and calculation belong to elective physics.

</Perspective>

## Wave–particle duality: photons and electrons have both sides

Put light and electrons side by side:

| What | Evidence of a wave | Evidence of a particle |
|---|---|---|
| Light | double-slit interference, diffraction | photoelectric effect: energy is handed to electrons one photon at a time |
| Electrons | electron double-slit interference, electron diffraction | separate dots on the screen; a definite mass and charge |

Light and electrons both have a wave side and a particle side. This is <Term t="physics.wave-particle-duality">wave–particle duality</Term>. Not only photons and electrons: **all microscopic particles** show wave–particle duality. The story of light runs from wave to particle: first Young's interference and Maxwell's electromagnetic waves, then Einstein's light quanta. The story of the electron runs the other way: first treated as a particle, then shown to have a wave side through de Broglie's matter waves, electron diffraction and the electron double slit.

<Perspective title="In two stages: it travels like a wave and is detected like a particle">

Rather than asking "is it really a wave or a particle?", split one run of the experiment into two stages:

1. **Travelling**: on the way from the slits to the screen, the electron (or photon) passes through both slits like a wave and overlaps with itself. This decides where it is **more likely** to land: likely at bright fringes, almost never at dark ones.
2. **Detection**: the moment it hits the screen, it is recorded at **one point**, with all its energy delivered there, like a particle.

Look at the two experiments again this way. The <Term t="physics.photoelectric-effect">photoelectric effect</Term> looks at light at the detection stage: light's energy is handed to the electrons in the metal one portion at a time, so the particle side shows. The double-slit fringes are what many travelling stages add up to, so the wave side shows. Turn light down until it is extremely faint and run a double slit with a sensitive detector, and you also see photons arrive one by one and slowly build up fringes, just like electrons.

<StudentAsks q="So do light and electrons switch between being a wave and being a particle?">

They don't switch between two kinds of thing. Each is one kind of "quantum" object, and our everyday words "wave" and "particle" each describe only one side of it. Which side shows depends on what the experiment asks: ask "does it interfere?" and you see a wave; ask "how is its energy handed over, and at which point does it land?" and you see a particle.

## Where Newtonian mechanics works: below the atomic scale, use quantum theory

Newton's laws of motion describe baseballs, blocks, artificial satellites and planets extremely well. But **below the atomic scale (about $10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$)**, the wave side of microscopic particles becomes important, Newton's laws no longer apply, and **quantum theory** is needed. Below, the powers of ten in <Term t="math.scientific-notation">scientific notation</Term> put these scales on one line.

**Example**　Which of these two questions can Newtonian mechanics answer? (A) Where a marble rolling off a table lands; (B) which energies an electron in a hydrogen atom can have.

(A) A marble is about $10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}$, far larger than an atom, so Newtonian mechanics can answer it. (B) The electron is inside an atom, at the atomic scale, so quantum theory is needed: the electron can only have certain energies.

<Perspective title="By energy: a satellite's energy can change smoothly, an electron's in an atom cannot">

In the [last chapter](/en/physics/s116-photons-spectra/) you saw that an electron in an atom can only have certain energies (<Term t="physics.energy-level">energy levels</Term>), so an atom only emits or absorbs light of certain frequencies, giving a spectrum of separate lines.

If you pictured the electron as a "tiny planet" circling the nucleus under Newtonian mechanics, it would be like an artificial satellite. A satellite's orbit can be raised a little or lowered a little, so its energy can take **any value** within a range. Such an electron would emit light of every frequency, and the spectrum would be one continuous band, not separate lines. The line spectra we actually see show that the inside of an atom cannot be described by Newtonian mechanics: it needs quantum theory.

<StudentAsks q="If Newtonian mechanics fails inside atoms, was Newton wrong?">

Not wrong: it has a **range where it applies**. At everyday and astronomical scales, quantum theory predicts almost exactly what Newtonian mechanics does, and Newtonian mechanics is both simpler and accurate, so engineers still use it to design bridges and work out satellite orbits. A new theory widens what we can describe and agrees with the old one where the old one works well. That is a common pattern in how science moves forward.
