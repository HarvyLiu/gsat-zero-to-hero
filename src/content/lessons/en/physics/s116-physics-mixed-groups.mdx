This is an exam-skills chapter of the 116 sprint: it assumes you studied the required physics course <Zh>必修物理</Zh> in school and have worked through the earlier review chapters. It teaches no new physics; it practises reading and joining.

## What the Part 2 item groups look like

As on the 111–115 papers (the 116 layout is not confirmed), the Science paper has two parts:

- Part 1 has $36$ single and multi-select items, $2$ points each, $72$ points in all; physics, chemistry, biology and earth science each have a block of $9$, in an order that rotates by year.
- Part 2 has $6$ mixed or written item groups <Zh>題組</Zh>, $56$ points in all, including cross-subject groups <Zh>跨科題組</Zh>. Choice items inside the groups are worth $2$ points each; written items show their points at the end.

A total of $128$ points has been the custom in these years, not a rule of the brochure: the brochure leaves scoring and full marks to each paper.

Multi-select items inside the groups are scored as in Part 1: <Fact id="exam.multi-select-scoring" />

For written items inside the groups, CEEC says you must show your calculations or reasons, and a bare answer can score zero (for example 115 GSAT Q53); partial credit is set by each item's marking principles, not by a general rule. The last chapter, [Dimension checks and writing out reasoning in non-choice items](/en/physics/s116-physics-reasoning/), practised how to write them.

## Four steps for reading a context

A long passage does not mean hard physics. When you meet an item group, take these four steps:

1. **Read the questions first, then the passage.** Once you know what you are looking for, you know which sentences matter.
2. **Mark the physical quantities, data and units, and the relations the passage gives you.** For example, "the same volume of blood passes every cross-section each second": that sentence is an equation in itself.
3. **Translate unfamiliar terms into ideas you have learned.** A term that is not in your textbook is usually an idea you know, said another way.
4. **Skip the data you do not need.** Contexts often add data that have nothing to do with the question. Decide first which quantities the question needs, then go back and find them.

Each of the three contexts below practises one or two of these steps.

## Context 1: Doppler ultrasound and blood flow (physics + biology)

> When the heart contracts it pushes blood into the arteries. A doctor presses an ultrasound probe on the skin, sends ultrasound of a fixed frequency into a blood vessel, and receives the waves reflected by red blood cells in the blood. Comparing the two frequencies shows which way the blood flows and how fast. If an artery narrows somewhere because of plaque, the same volume of blood still passes every cross-section each second, so the blood speeds up at the narrowing.

Take it apart with the four steps:

| Sentence | Subject | Idea to use |
|---|---|---|
| The heart pushes blood into the arteries | Biology | Circulation: arteries carry blood away from the heart |
| Red cells reflect ultrasound; compare frequencies | Physics | The Doppler effect: higher frequency when approaching, lower when receding, and a larger change for a larger relative speed |
| The same volume passes every cross-section each second | A relation the passage gives | area × speed = constant |

The <Term t="physics.doppler-effect">Doppler effect</Term> was proposed by the Austrian physicist Christian Doppler in 1842. Why does the frequency rise when the source approaches? Draw the wavefronts the source sends out, one per period. Drag $u$ to move the source to the right, toward the probe.

<Graph desc="The wave moves 1 unit per period, and the source sends out one wavefront per period. The four circles are the wavefronts sent at periods 0, 1, 2 and 3, with radii 4, 3, 2 and 1, each centred where the source was at that moment. Slider u is how far the source moves right in one period (u is the ratio of the source's speed to the wave speed). At u=0 the circles are concentric and evenly spaced; at the start u=0.4, the centres shift right, the wavefronts on the probe's side to the right are packed closer (shorter wavelength) and those on the left are spread out. The probe receives more wavefronts per second, so the frequency is higher." x={[-5, 8]} y={[-5, 5]}

<Perspective title="Without the drawing: when does each crest arrive?">

You can see the same thing with times alone. Let the wave move $1$ unit per period; the source starts $6$ units from the probe and moves $0.4$ units toward it each period (the $u=0.4$ in the figure).

| Crest | Sent at | Distance from probe when sent | Arrives at |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$st | $0$ | $6$ | $0+6=6$ |
| $2$nd | $1$ | $5.6$ | $1+5.6=6.6$ |
| $3$rd | $2$ | $5.2$ | $2+5.2=7.2$ |
| $4$th | $3$ | $4.8$ | $3+4.8=7.8$ |

The source sends a crest every $1$ period, but the probe receives one every $0.6$ periods: each later crest starts closer to the probe and has less distance to cover. A shorter interval between arrivals means a higher frequency. With the source at rest, the four crests arrive at $6$, $7$, $8$ and $9$, still $1$ apart.

<NumberLine desc="Times (in periods) at which the probe receives crests. With the source moving toward the probe, the four crests arrive at 6, 6.6, 7.2 and 7.8, 0.6 apart, which is shorter than the interval of 1 for a source at rest, so the received frequency is higher." from={5.5} to={9.5} step={0.5} labelEvery={1} points={[{ x: 6, label: '6' }, { x: 6.6, label: '6.6' }, { x: 7.2, label: '7.2' }, { x: 7.8, label: '7.8' }]} />

The table also shows why only the relative motion counts: what sets the interval is how much closer the source gets each period, not how strong the ultrasound is.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Red cells only reflect the ultrasound; they don't make sound themselves. Does the Doppler effect still apply?">

Yes. A red cell moving toward the probe first acts like an observer moving into the wavefronts, so it receives a higher frequency; then, sending the wave back, it acts like a source moving toward the probe, and the frequency rises once more. Both steps push the same way, so for review just remember: a reflector moving closer gives a higher received frequency, moving away a lower one, and at rest no change.

</StudentAsks>

**Example (our own)**　Continuing the passage, the blood in one artery flows at $30\ \mathrm{cm/s}$; downstream, at the narrowing, the cross-section is only $\frac{1}{3}$ of what it was. How fast does the blood flow at the narrowing? Where does the reflected frequency change more?

The passage gives "area × speed = constant": the area becomes $\frac{1}{3}$ as large, so the speed becomes $3$ times as large, $30\times 3=90\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ at the narrowing. The faster the flow, the larger the relative speed of the red cells and the probe and the larger the frequency change, so the reflected frequency changes more at the narrowing. Note that the first step uses no textbook physics at all, only one sentence of the passage turned into an equation; the Doppler effect comes in at the second step.

## Context 2: a term your textbook doesn't have, LEDs and energy bands (physics + chemistry)

The 114 GSAT physics and chemistry cross-subject group, Q50–54, used a titanium dioxide photocatalyst as its context, and its passage used band terms beyond the curriculum; CEEC's comment is that these can be bridged with the idea of <Term t="physics.energy-level">energy levels</Term>. Here is a passage of the same kind, written by us:

> An electron in an LED can be in the higher-energy conduction band or in the lower-energy valence band; between them lies a range of energies the electron cannot have, called the band gap. An electron that drops from the conduction band to the valence band emits one photon, whose energy is about equal to the band gap. Photon energy (in $\mathrm{eV}$) times wavelength (in $\mathrm{nm}$) is about $1240$.

"Conduction band", "valence band" and "band gap" are not terms of the required physics course, but translate them and they are the energy levels you have learned:

| Term in the passage | Translated into an idea you know |
|---|---|
| conduction band, valence band | a higher and a lower energy the electron can have (like two energy levels) |
| band gap | the energy difference between them (the level difference) |
| an electron drops to the valence band and emits a photon | an electron jumps to a lower energy level and emits a photon whose energy equals the difference |

After translating, the reasoning is two lines you have reviewed: a <Term t="physics.photon">photon</Term>'s energy $E=hf$ is proportional to its <Term t="physics.frequency">frequency</Term>; the speed of light $c=f\lambda$ is fixed, so the higher the frequency, the shorter the <Term t="physics.wavelength">wavelength</Term>. So **the larger the band gap, the more energy each photon carries and the shorter its wavelength**. Drag the band gap $E_g$ and see.

**Example (our own)**　An LED has a band gap of $2.0\ \mathrm{eV}$. About what wavelength is its light?

The photon energy is about the band gap, $2.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, so by the passage's relation the wavelength is about $\frac{1240}{2.0}=620\ \mathrm{nm}$, in the red part of visible light (about $400$ to $700\ \mathrm{nm}$).

<Perspective title="Read it off a graph: wavelength against photon energy">

The passage's relation "energy × wavelength ≈ 1240" can be drawn as the curve $\lambda=\frac{1240}{E}$. Find the band gap on the horizontal axis, go up to the curve and read off the wavelength; between the two dashed lines lies visible light.

The curve also turns "the larger the gap, the shorter the wavelength" into something you can see: it runs down to the right all the way. There is no calculator in the exam, but when you only need to compare, the direction of the curve settles it without working out any wavelength.

</Perspective>

<Perspective title="Where 1240 comes from">

It is just $E=hf$ and $c=f\lambda$ combined: $E\lambda=hf\cdot\frac{c}{f}=hc$. With $h\approx 4.14\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot s}$ and $c=3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (multiply in <Term t="math.scientific-notation">scientific notation</Term>: the powers add):

$$
hc\approx 4.14\times10^{-15}\times 3\times10^{8}\approx 1.24\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV\cdot m}=1240\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm}
$$

So the number is no new physics, just two relations you know and a unit conversion, worked out in advance. If an exam passage gives it, use it; if not, the question usually only needs a comparison.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Where energy bands come from" needs="Solid-state physics (beyond the GSAT)">

An isolated atom's electrons can have only a few definite energies (energy levels). In a solid, a huge number of atoms sit close together and affect one another, so one energy level splits into many energies very close together, packed into a stretch: a "band". Between bands lie ranges of energy the electrons cannot have: the band gaps. The GSAT does not test this; reasoning with a band as if it were an energy level is enough.

</Perspective>

Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura, who invented efficient blue LEDs, won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics. Blue photons carry more energy than red ones, so a blue LED needs a material with a large enough band gap: exactly what the curve above says.

## Context 3: the electromagnetic balance and the new kilogram (physics group)

The 111 GSAT physics group, Q58–60, used the Planck constant as the new mass standard and an electromagnetic balance <Zh>電磁天平</Zh> as its context. The background: since 2019, the kilogram of the <Term t="physics.si-units">International System of Units</Term> (SI) has been defined by fixing the numerical value of the Planck constant. Max Planck put forward the idea of energy quanta in 1900, and the constant is named after him.

A real electromagnetic balance relies on quantum electrical measurements beyond high school, but an item group writes the relations you need into the passage. Here is a simplified passage of our own:

> In a teaching electromagnetic balance, a coil in a magnetic field hangs under the pan. With a current in it, the coil feels an upward magnetic force proportional to the current; when that force equals the weight of the mass, the balance is level. With a $10$ g mass the balance needs $40\ \mathrm{mA}$.

Only one sentence is physics you have learned: a current-carrying coil in a <Term t="physics.magnetic-field">magnetic field</Term> feels a magnetic force (the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field from junior high, which is what turns a motor). "The magnetic force is proportional to the current" and "the balance is level when the force equals the weight" are both given by the passage.

**Example (our own)**　With a $25$ g mass instead, what current is needed?

At balance, magnetic force = weight. Weight is proportional to mass and the magnetic force to the current, so the current is proportional to the mass. The mass goes from $10$ g to $25$ g, $2.5$ times as much, so the current is $2.5$ times as large: $40\times 2.5=100\ \mathrm{mA}$.

Draw "the magnetic force is proportional to the current" as a line through the origin, and the balance condition becomes the point where two lines meet.

<Perspective title="Without the graph: use the unit amount">

Think in unit amounts: $40\ \mathrm{mA}$ holds $10$ g, so each $1\ \mathrm{mA}$ holds $\frac{10}{40}=\frac{1}{4}$ g, which is the <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> of the line in the graph. To hold $25$ g you need $25\div\frac{1}{4}=100\ \mathrm{mA}$.

Scaling, the unit amount and the crossing point in the graph are three readings of the same proportion. Item groups often give one set of "known balance" data and then ask about another: first write down what is proportional to what, and all three methods work.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="What if a relation in the passage differs from what my textbook says?">

Go by the passage. An item group states, in its passage, the relations and assumptions the context uses (for example "blood can be treated as incompressible" or "the magnetic force is proportional to the current"), and these are often simplified models. Your job is to trust the conditions the passage gives and join them with the physics you have learned. If a condition in the passage really seems to contradict an idea you know, reread it first: usually you have misread a quantity.

</StudentAsks>

## Cross-subject groups: separate, then join

What makes a cross-subject group hard is not that one subject is especially hard, but that the ideas of two subjects must be joined. The method: label each sentence with its subject, then find **the quantity the two sides share**.

| Context | The other subject supplies | Physics supplies | The quantity that joins them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doppler ultrasound and blood flow | Biology: circulation, a narrowing vessel | The Doppler effect | Direction and speed of the blood flow |
| Materials such as LEDs and photocatalysts | Chemistry: materials and reactions | Energy levels, photon energy $E=hf$ | Photon energy and wavelength |
| Astronomical observation | Earth science: motion of stars and other bodies | The Doppler effect for light, $c=f\lambda$ | Wavelength of a spectral line |

Look at the last row. **Example (our own)**　An astronomer observes a spectral line in a star's spectrum and finds its wavelength slightly shorter than the same line from the same kind of atom in the laboratory. How is the star moving relative to Earth?

Light shows the Doppler effect too: an approaching source gives a higher received frequency. The speed of light $c=f\lambda$ is fixed, so a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength. So a shorter wavelength means the star is approaching Earth. Here earth science supplies which star is being watched, physics supplies how to read its motion from a spectral line, and the two are joined by the wavelength of that line.

<NumberLine desc="A wavelength number line (nm, illustrative values). The laboratory line is at 500; for a star approaching Earth, the same line is observed at a slightly shorter wavelength, for example 497, shifted toward the blue side. If the star were moving away, the line would shift to the side longer than 500." from={494} to={506} step={1} labelEvery={2} points={[{ x: 500, label: 'laboratory' }, { x: 497, label: 'approaching star' }]} />

<Perspective title="Another view: use the wavefront picture from Context 1">

You can do without $c=f\lambda$. Go back to the wavefront picture in Context 1: when the source moves toward the probe, the wavefronts ahead are packed closer, and the distance between neighbouring crests is the wavelength, so the wavelength ahead is shorter. Replace "probe" by a telescope on Earth and "source" by an approaching star, and the same picture explains why the line shifts toward shorter wavelengths. Both views reach the same conclusion: one reasons from frequency to wavelength, the other looks at the wavelength directly.

</Perspective>

Crossing subjects is the normal state of science. Marie Curie shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903 and won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911, and she was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize: one research question can be physics and chemistry at the same time.
