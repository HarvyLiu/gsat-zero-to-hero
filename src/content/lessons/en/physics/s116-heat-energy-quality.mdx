## The absolute scale: what temperature means for atoms

Matter is made of atoms and molecules that never stop moving. Gas molecules fly about and collide; the atoms of a solid vibrate around fixed positions. How high the temperature is reflects how vigorous this random motion is:

**The higher the absolute temperature, the greater the average kinetic energy of the atoms (molecules).**

The word "average" matters. In one bottle of gas some molecules are fast and some slow, and they keep trading energy in collisions; temperature describes the average over all of them, not any single molecule.

The <Term t="physics.kelvin-scale">absolute temperature scale</Term> (also called the Kelvin scale) puts the lowest possible temperature at $0\ \mathrm{K}$, called **absolute zero**, which is about $-273.15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. Nothing is colder than absolute zero. Each kelvin is the same size as a Celsius degree, so

$$
T\ (\mathrm{K})=t\ (^{\circ}\mathrm{C})+273.15
$$

In exam calculations you usually take $T=t+273$. Some common temperatures (at $1$ atmosphere):

| | Celsius | Absolute scale |
|---|---|---|
| absolute zero | $-273.15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $0\ \mathrm{K}$ |
| melting point of ice | $0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $273.15\ \mathrm{K}$ |
| boiling point of water | $100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $373.15\ \mathrm{K}$ |

**Example**　A gas is heated from $-73\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ to $327\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (use $T=t+273$). (1) By how many $\mathrm{K}$ does its temperature rise? (2) By what factor does its absolute temperature grow?

(1) A temperature *difference* is the same on both scales: $327-(-73)=400$, a rise of $400\ \mathrm{K}$.
(2) For a factor you must change to absolute temperature first: $-73\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ is $200\ \mathrm{K}$ and $327\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ is $600\ \mathrm{K}$, so the factor is $600\div200=3$. The average kinetic energy of the gas molecules has grown too.

The question group 17–18 on the 115 GSAT tested how the average kinetic energy of gas molecules relates to temperature.

<Perspective title="With ratios: why factors need the absolute scale">

Celsius zero is just the melting point of ice, a starting point people chose; absolute zero is the true lower limit of temperature. So a temperature **difference** works on either scale, but a temperature **ratio** only means something on the absolute scale. Compare these three changes:

| Change | Ratio of Celsius readings | Ratio of absolute temperatures |
|---|---|---|
| $10\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\to20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $2$ | $\frac{293}{283}\approx1.04$ |
| $1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\to2\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $2$ | $\frac{275}{274}\approx1.004$ |
| $-10\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\to10\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ | $-1$ (meaningless) | $\frac{283}{263}\approx1.08$ |

In the first two rows the Celsius reading "doubles" both times, yet one warming is ten times bigger than the other; in the third the Celsius ratio is even negative. The absolute temperatures tell a consistent story: the more it warms, the bigger the ratio, and the ratio is always positive. This is why average kinetic energy can only be linked to absolute temperature.

The absolute scale was proposed in 1848 by the British physicist William Thomson, who was later made Lord Kelvin; the unit $\mathrm{K}$ is named after his title.

</Perspective>

## Thermal energy: atomic kinetic energy plus interaction energy

The atoms in a body do not just move; they also interact with one another (the forces between atoms come from the electromagnetic interaction). Together, these two parts are the <Term t="physics.thermal-energy">thermal energy</Term>:

$$
\text{thermal energy}=\text{kinetic energy of the atoms' (molecules') random motion}+\text{interaction energy between the atoms}
$$

The total kinetic and interaction energy of all the atoms inside a body is also called its <Term t="physics.internal-energy">internal energy</Term>; within required physics you can treat thermal energy and internal energy as the same quantity.

The **ideal gas** is the simplest case: the molecules are far apart and interact only at the instant of a collision, so the interaction energy can be ignored and the internal energy is just the total kinetic energy of the molecules. More molecules and a higher temperature mean more internal energy.

Temperature and thermal energy are not the same thing: temperature looks at the **average** kinetic energy, thermal energy at the **total**, and it includes the interaction energy as well.

**Example**　Compare a tub of $100\ \mathrm{kg}$ of bath water at $40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with a cup of $0.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ of tea at $90\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (treat the tea as water). Which is hotter? How much heat can each give out as it cools to $20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$?

The tea is hotter, so its molecules have the greater average kinetic energy. But use the junior-high rule "heat = mass × specific heat × temperature change" (the specific heat of water is $1\ \mathrm{cal/(g\cdot{}^{\circ}C)}$):

- Bath water: $100000\times1\times(40-20)=2000000\ \mathrm{cal}$, that is $2000$ kcal.
- Tea: $200\times1\times(90-20)=14000\ \mathrm{cal}$, that is $14$ kcal.

The bath water is cooler, yet it can give out more than a hundred times as much heat as the tea, because it has far more molecules.

Another example shows the role of interaction energy even more clearly. At $1$ atmosphere, water boiling at $100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ keeps absorbing heat, yet its temperature stays at $100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. With the temperature unchanged, the average kinetic energy of the molecules is unchanged; the energy taken in does not vanish either. It goes mainly into pulling the molecules apart, so what grows is the interaction energy. The thermal energy has increased while the temperature has not.

## Heat: energy moved by a temperature difference

A body has thermal energy; <Term t="physics.heat">heat</Term> is **energy transferred because of a temperature difference**, and it exists only while the transfer is happening. So "how much heat this cup of water contains" is the wrong way to put it. Say "how much thermal energy this water has" and "how much heat it gave out (took in)".

There are two ways to increase a body's thermal energy: **do work on it** (rubbing, compressing) or let it **take in heat** from something hotter.

In the eighteenth century most people pictured heat as an invisible fluid, called caloric. In 1798 Count Rumford (Benjamin Thompson), born in the North American colonies and later serving in Bavaria, watched cannon barrels being bored and found that friction seemed to produce heat without end, which was hard to explain if caloric could run out. In the 1840s James Prescott Joule, from an English brewing family and once a student of the chemist John Dalton, did a series of experiments showing that a given amount of mechanical work always produces a given amount of heat. Heat came to be understood as a way of transferring energy, not as a substance.

Heat **flows by itself from a hotter body to a colder one** until the two reach the same temperature. This is **thermal equilibrium**, and then there is no net heat flow. The direction of heat flow depends only on temperature, not on which body has more thermal energy: when the bath water and the tea from earlier touch, heat flows from the tea into the bath water.

**Example**　You mix $200\ \mathrm{g}$ of hot water at $80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with $200\ \mathrm{g}$ of cold water at $20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, and no heat is lost to the surroundings. What is the final temperature?

Heat flows from the hot water to the cold water until both reach the same temperature $T$. By energy conservation, heat given out by the hot water = heat taken in by the cold water; both are water, so the specific heat cancels:

$$
200\,(80-T)=200\,(T-20)\iff T=50
$$

The final temperature is $50\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. With equal masses, it lands exactly in the middle.

<Perspective title="With a lever: the final temperature is a mass-weighted average">

Write the heat given out by the hot water and the heat taken in by the cold water in general form (the same liquid):

$$
m_1\,(T_1-T)=m_2\,(T-T_2)
$$

The left side is "mass of hot water × degrees it fell", the right side "mass of cold water × degrees it rose". That is exactly a balanced lever: put the two lots of water on a number line at their temperatures, and the final temperature $T$ is the pivot. **The heavier side sits closer to the pivot**, with distances in inverse proportion to the masses.

For example, mix $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ of water at $80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with $2\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. The cold water has $2$ times the mass, so the final temperature is only half as far from the cold end as from the hot end. Split the $60$ degrees from $20$ to $80$ in the ratio $1:2$: the final temperature is $20$ degrees above $20$, so $T=40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. Check with the equation: $1\times(80-40)=40=2\times(40-20)$.

<Perspective title="With collisions: why heat follows temperature">

At the scale of atoms, heat flow is the result of collisions. The molecules of the hotter body have the greater average kinetic energy; those of the colder body have less. Where the two touch, fast molecules hit slow ones; on average the fast ones slow down and the slow ones speed up, so energy passes from the hot side to the cold side.

This goes on until the average kinetic energies on the two sides are equal, which means the temperatures are equal. The molecules still collide, but equal amounts of energy pass each way, so there is no net heat flow.

So what decides the direction of heat flow is the **average kinetic energy** (temperature), not the **total energy** (thermal energy). Bath water has a huge number of molecules and a lot of thermal energy, but on average each of them moves more slowly than a tea molecule, so in collisions it is the tea molecules that hand energy to the bath-water molecules.

</Perspective>

## Work and heat are not symmetric: ordered and disordered energy

When a car brakes, friction in the brakes stops it, and nearly all its kinetic energy becomes thermal energy of the brake pads, wheels and air (plus a little sound). **Work turns easily and entirely into heat.**

The reverse does not work. A parked car never cools down by itself and turns that thermal energy back into motion to drive off. **Heat engines** such as steam engines, car engines and thermal power stations take in heat from a hot place and can turn only part of it into work; the rest of the heat must be released to a colder place (air, river or sea water). **Heat cannot be turned entirely into work.**

Why? Look at the form of the energy:

- While the car moves, all its atoms move together in the same direction. This is **ordered** energy (for example, the kinetic energy of a moving block).
- Once it has become thermal energy, the atoms jiggle in all directions independently. This is **disordered** energy (for example, the heat produced by friction).

Ordered turns into disordered easily; collisions and friction do it all the time. For disordered energy to turn back into ordered energy by itself, every atom would have to move in the same direction at the same moment, which in practice never happens.

<Perspective title="By counting: why jiggling never tidies itself up">

Use coins as a stand-in for the directions in which atoms move. Shake $4$ coins and spread them out: each is equally likely to land heads or tails, so there are $2^4=16$ equally likely outcomes. Sort them by the number of heads:

| Number of heads | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of arrangements | $1$ | $4$ | $6$ | $4$ | $1$ |

The tidy outcome "all heads" has only $1$ arrangement, probability $\frac{1}{16}$; the mixed outcome "half and half" has $6$ arrangements and turns up most often.

With more coins the gap becomes extreme: the probability that $100$ coins all land heads is $\frac{1}{2^{100}}\approx7.9\times10^{-31}$. A cup of water holds more than $10^{24}$ molecules, so the chance that their jiggling lines up in one direction all at once is so small that you need not expect it within the age of the universe. Tidy states have very few arrangements and messy states have a vast number, so systems always go from ordered to disordered.

<Perspective later title="The efficiency limit of heat engines, and entropy" needs="the second law of thermodynamics and entropy (beyond required physics; not on the GSAT)">

In the 1820s the French engineer Sadi Carnot analysed heat engines and showed that their efficiency has an upper limit that depends only on the temperatures of the hot and cold places. In absolute temperatures, an engine running between a hot place at $T_h$ and a cold place at $T_c$ has an efficiency of at most

$$
1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}
$$

For example, with $T_h=500\ \mathrm{K}$ and $T_c=300\ \mathrm{K}$, at most $1-\frac{300}{500}=40\%$ of the heat becomes work. As long as $T_c$ is not $0\ \mathrm{K}$, the limit is below $100\%$: this is the quantitative version of "heat cannot be turned entirely into work". It also shows what the absolute scale is for: the temperatures in this formula must be in $\mathrm{K}$.

In 1865 the German physicist Rudolf Clausius introduced entropy to describe how spread out energy is; in the 1870s the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann linked entropy to the number of microscopic arrangements, the same idea as counting coins in the previous perspective.

</Perspective>

## The quality of energy: ability to do work and energy degradation

Energy conservation tells you the **total amount** of energy stays the same, but not how much it can still do. **Systems with the same energy can differ in their ability to do work.**

Compare two systems that each have $1000\ \mathrm{J}$:

- A moving cart with $1000\ \mathrm{J}$ of kinetic energy. This is ordered energy; with a rope and pulley, almost all of it can be used to lift a load.
- The same $1000\ \mathrm{J}$ added as thermal energy to $1000\ \mathrm{kg}$ of lake water at the same temperature as its surroundings. The specific heat of water is about $4.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot{}^{\circ}C)}$, so the water warms by only $\frac{1000}{1000\times4.2\times10^{3}}\approx2.4\times10^{-4}\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. The energy is spread over the jiggling of countless molecules, and there is nothing colder nearby for heat to flow into, so almost none of it can be used to do work.

**Energy is conserved, but each conversion usually spreads it out further and lowers its ability to do work. This is called energy degradation.** Take a desk lamp: electrical energy becomes light and heat, the light is absorbed by the walls and furniture as thermal energy, and in the end almost all of it becomes thermal energy of the air and objects in the room. Not one joule is missing, yet it can never light the lamp again.

**Example**　A ball starts falling with $100\ \mathrm{J}$ of <Term t="physics.mechanical-energy">mechanical energy</Term>. Suppose each bounce keeps only $80\%$ of the mechanical energy it had before the bounce, and the rest becomes thermal energy and sound. After $3$ bounces, how much mechanical energy is left, and how much energy has become thermal energy and sound?

$100\times0.8^3=51.2\ \mathrm{J}$ of mechanical energy is left; total energy is conserved, so $100-51.2=48.8\ \mathrm{J}$ has become thermal energy and sound. The total is always $100\ \mathrm{J}$, but the part that can lift the ball keeps shrinking.

<Perspective title="Like a waterfall: doing work takes a temperature difference">

Water flowing from high to low can turn a water wheel; if the water levels are equal, no amount of water will turn it. Heat is the same: a heat engine can take work out of heat only while the heat flows from high temperature to low; if everything is at the same temperature, no amount of thermal energy can do work.

Let's work one out. Take two identical metal blocks. In the first case one block is at $400\ \mathrm{K}$ and the other at $200\ \mathrm{K}$; in the second case both are at $300\ \mathrm{K}$. A metal block's thermal energy grows evenly with temperature (each $1\ \mathrm{K}$ rise adds the same amount), so the total thermal energy is the same in both cases: $400+200=300+300$.

- The first case has a temperature difference, so a heat engine connected between the blocks can take out some work while heat flows from the $400\ \mathrm{K}$ block to the $200\ \mathrm{K}$ block.
- The second case has no temperature difference, so no heat flows and no work at all can be taken out.

The same amount of energy, but completely different abilities to do work. Press the two blocks of the first case straight together and heat flows by itself until both are at $300\ \mathrm{K}$: the total energy is unchanged, but the chance to do work is gone. That is energy degradation.

<StudentAsks q="If energy is conserved, why save energy?">

Because what we use up is not the amount of energy but its quality. The chemical energy of fuel and electrical energy are forms that are easy to turn into work; once used, they all end up as thermal energy spread through the environment. Not one bit of the total is gone, but it can hardly do any more work. Saving energy means saving energy that can still do work.

</StudentAsks>
