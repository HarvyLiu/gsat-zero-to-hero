>> turn: q-turn

This is a 116 sprint review. It assumes you took <Zh>自然科學探究與實作</Zh> (inquiry and practice) at school and have finished required physics, chemistry, biology and earth science. This chapter teaches no new subject content: it uses ideas the subject chapters reviewed and links each one.

## 1. Take the item set apart: which subject and which skill each sub-item needs

According to the CEEC, a mixed-format item (<Zh>混合題</Zh>) is an item set of generally 2 to 4 sub-items that starts with choice items and moves on to non-choice items. Every year from 112 to 115, Part 2 had cross-subject item sets built by two subjects. For example, 113 Q40–43 combined physics and biology, and Q43 tested variables and predicting results directly, while Q50–53 combined chemistry and earth science; of the 6 item sets in Part 2 of 115, Q40–43 (biology and chemistry) and Q50–53 (earth science and physics) were cross-subject. Inside one cross-subject set, each sub-item still belongs somewhere: of the 115 non-choice items, Q50 counted as earth science and Q53 as physics.

The sample items in the exam specification are tagged with subject content and also with inquiry and practice sub-items, such as "argumentation and modelling: explaining and reasoning"; Q1 of the <Zh>外傘頂洲</Zh> set is tagged with inquiry sub-items only. So when you get an item set, tag each sub-item with two things: **which subject's idea it uses** and **which inquiry skill it tests**.

Here is this chapter's original item set (illustrative data). A student takes a bottle of soda out of the fridge, opens it and stands it on a desk in a room at 24 ℃. Every 10 minutes she measures the soda's temperature and weighs the whole bottle on an electronic balance that reads to 0.1 g (wiping off the water droplets that condense on the outside before each weighing).

1. The soda's temperature rises more and more slowly and stops at about 24 ℃. Explain why.
2. From the data, work out the mass of carbon dioxide that escaped in the first 30 minutes.
3. Build a model of how the warming and the escaped carbon dioxide are related, and state its range.
4. A student claims: "As the soda warms, the solubility of carbon dioxide gets smaller, so the gas comes out." Evaluate the claim and propose an improved experimental design. (Written item.)

| Sub-item | Subject idea | Inquiry skill |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Physics: heat flows from hot to cold | Explaining and reasoning |
| 2 | Chemistry: gas leaving an open container lowers the reading | Analysing data and presenting evidence |
| 3 | Physics + chemistry: warming against escaped gas | Building models |
| 4 | Chemistry: a gas gets less soluble as the temperature rises | Evaluating and reflecting |

Sub-items 1–3 belong to argumentation and modelling; sub-item 4 to communicating and sharing.

<StudentAsks q="Time is tight in the exam. Isn't tagging the subject and skill first a waste of time?">

Tagging a sub-item takes seconds and saves more than that. Tag it "physics: heat", and you know to take the temperature column and write about which way heat flows. Tag it "evaluate", and you know one textbook idea is not enough: you must also say whether the evidence is enough. An easy mistake is to answer a physics question with a chemistry reason, or an evaluation item with a single textbook conclusion.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Another angle: find the skill from the verb, the subject from the columns">

Without the table above, you can still tag the sub-items yourself. Two clues, each settling one thing.

1. **The verb decides the skill.** "Explain why" is explaining and reasoning; "work out" is analysing data; "build a model" is building models; "evaluate" and "propose an improvement" are evaluating and reflecting, plus drafting a research plan.
2. **The columns a sub-item uses decide the subject.** Sub-item 1 uses only the temperature column, and temperature and heat are physics. Sub-item 2 uses the mass column; gas leaving the bottle lowers the reading, which is chemistry. Sub-item 3 uses both columns, so it needs both subjects. Sub-item 4 asks about solubility, which is chemistry.

The two clues give the same tags as the table. If a column seems to belong to one subject but the question asks about another (say, the temperature column used to ask about solubility), go by what the question asks.

</Perspective>

Read the graph: work out how many degrees the soda rose in each 10 minutes and write them in a row. You get 8, 5, 3, 2, 1, 0: the rise gets slower and slower, and the temperature stops at 24 ℃.

That is the answer to sub-item 1, and it uses physics. The soda is colder than the air, so <Term t="physics.heat">heat</Term> flows from the warmer air into the colder soda and the soda warms up. At 24 ℃ the soda and the air are at the same temperature, there is no net flow of heat, and the temperature stops rising ([Temperature, heat and the quality of energy](/en/physics/s116-heat-energy-quality/)).

Now the mass. In 60 minutes the balance reading fell by $3.6\ \mathrm{g}$ in all.

```dialogue
S: The cap's off, so carbon dioxide gets out and the reading falls. So the 3.6 grams lost is the carbon dioxide that escaped.
T: The neck of the bottle is open the whole time. Is all of the lost mass carbon dioxide?
>> turn: q-control
S: Not necessarily... the bottle is mostly water, and water can evaporate through the open neck too. Evaporated water also makes the reading fall.
T: Right. So next to it the student put an identical bottle holding the same amount of plain water, chilled the same way and also opened, and weighed it every 10 minutes too. Plain water has no carbon dioxide, so its loss is the water that evaporated through the same kind of neck. That bottle of water is the control.
```

| Time (min) | Soda lost (g) | Water lost (g) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 10 | 1.3 | 0.1 |
| 20 | 2.2 | 0.2 |
| 30 | 2.7 | 0.3 |
| 40 | 3.1 | 0.4 |
| 50 | 3.4 | 0.5 |
| 60 | 3.6 | 0.6 |

This is the <Term t="chemistry.conservation-of-mass">law of conservation of mass</Term> in an open container: the total mass is not lost; the gas and the water vapour just left the bottle, where the balance cannot weigh them ([Basic laws of chemistry and atomic theory](/en/chemistry/s116-laws-atomic-theory/)). The answer to sub-item 2 takes away the part lost by evaporation:

$$
2.7-0.3=2.4\ \mathrm{g}
$$

About $2.4\ \mathrm{g}$ of carbon dioxide escaped in the first 30 minutes. Give the unit, and write out the subtraction, because it is your evidence.

<StudentAsks q="Why not measure the carbon dioxide directly, instead of going the long way round with mass?">

The escaped carbon dioxide spreads into the classroom air and is hard to collect and measure. Mass is easy: put the bottle on the balance and read it. Once the control has taken away the evaporation, what is left is the carbon dioxide. Evidence often works like this: you measure something you can measure, then use a well-designed comparison to turn it into the quantity you actually want.

</StudentAsks>

## 3. Build a cross-subject model and find its limits

Sub-item 3 joins the two subjects: the warming on the physics side, the escaping gas on the chemistry side. Put the change in each 10 minutes side by side:

| Interval (min) | Rise (℃) | CO₂ out (g) |
|---|---|---|
| 0→10 | 8 | 1.2 |
| 10→20 | 5 | 0.8 |
| 20→30 | 3 | 0.4 |
| 30→40 | 2 | 0.3 |
| 40→50 | 1 | 0.2 |
| 50→60 | 0 | 0.1 |

Escaped carbon dioxide = mass lost by the soda − mass lost by the water; for 0→10 that is $1.3-0.1=1.2\ \mathrm{g}$. The intervals with more warming also lost more gas. Build a model from the first 30 minutes:

$$
\frac{1.2+0.8+0.4}{8+5+3}=\frac{2.4}{16}=0.15\ \mathrm{g/{}^{\circ}C}
$$

The model: **each 1 ℃ of warming lets out about 0.15 g of carbon dioxide**. It seems to fit "warming makes the gas less soluble" perfectly.

<StudentAsks q="In the last 10 minutes the temperature didn't change, but 0.1 grams of carbon dioxide still went. Was that a measuring mistake?">

It might be: $0.1\ \mathrm{g}$ is the smallest amount this balance can read. But do not delete it just because it does not fit the model. It says the same thing as the bottle in the 5 ℃ bath: the gas gets out even without warming. Data that do not fit the model are often the first clue.

</StudentAsks>

| 0→10 | 0.15 |
| 10→20 | 0.16 |
| 20→30 | about 0.13 |
| 30→40 | 0.15 |
| 40→50 | 0.20 |
| 50→60 | cannot be worked out (rise is 0) |

1. In the first four intervals the ratio stays between 0.13 and 0.16, so the model holds up there.
2. In 40→50 it becomes 0.20; the numbers are very small, so errors matter more.
3. The last interval divides by 0, so there is no ratio at all: the model has no answer under the condition "no warming", which is exactly where the bottle in the bath sits.

The scatter diagram shows you which point is farthest from the line; the ratio table tells you, interval by interval, where the model starts to fail.

</Perspective>

## 4. Evaluate the claim: is the evidence enough, and is there a rival explanation?

Back to the claim in sub-item 4: "As the soda warms, the <Term t="chemistry.solubility">solubility</Term> of carbon dioxide gets smaller, so the gas comes out."

The chemistry idea it uses is right: the higher the temperature, the less soluble a gas is in water ([Solutions, concentration and solubility](/en/chemistry/s116-solutions/)). But evaluating a claim means asking **whether these data support it, and how far**.

Read the diagram: in the data from the bottle on the desk, both arrows are present at once and cannot be pulled apart. Which arrow does the bottle in the 5 ℃ bath remove? The blue "warming" one: that bottle never warmed, so the $2.0\ \mathrm{g}$ it lost can only be credited to "after opening".

<StudentAsks q="The textbook says a gas gets less soluble as the temperature rises. Doesn't that make the claim right?">

A right idea is not the same as an explanation of what you saw this time. The claim says "so the gas comes out", as if warming were the whole cause. The data show that the gas escapes even without warming ($2.0\ \mathrm{g}$), and warming adds only about $1.0\ \mathrm{g}$. Evaluating a claim means using the data to measure how much of the effect the idea accounts for here.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Two ways to evaluate: by the evidence, and by a rival explanation">

These are the two checks from [Argumentation and modelling](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-argue-model/), applied here to the claim in a cross-subject set.

**First way: by the evidence.** The claim predicts "more warming, more gas out", and the bottle on the desk fits that; but the data cover only one bottle in one run.

**Second way: by a rival explanation.** The rival says "taking the cap off makes the gas come out by itself". It predicts that gas escapes even without warming, fastest just after opening. The bottle in the 5 ℃ bath fits it exactly.

Together, the conclusion the data support is: "In this experiment, in 60 minutes the bottle kept in the 5 ℃ bath lost $2.0\ \mathrm{g}$ and the bottle on the desk $3.0\ \mathrm{g}$; warming let out about $1.0\ \mathrm{g}$ more carbon dioxide, but most of the gas escaped even without warming."

To make the conclusion more trustworthy, you could improve the design:

1. Use three bottles in each group, and see whether the result repeats.
2. Add a group at another temperature, for example in water at 15 ℃, to see whether the gas lost rises steadily with temperature.
3. The <Term t="inquiry.manipulated-variable">manipulated variable</Term> is the temperature the soda is kept at (℃); the <Term t="inquiry.responding-variable">responding variable</Term> is the carbon dioxide escaped in 60 minutes (g); the <Term t="inquiry.controlled-variable">controlled variables</Term> are the same kind and amount of soda, opened at the same moment, with the droplets wiped off before each weighing.

</Perspective>

- **Claim**: the claim is only partly right.
- **Evidence**: the bottle kept in the 5 ℃ water bath still lost $2.0\ \mathrm{g}$ in 60 minutes; the bottle on the desk, which warmed to 24 ℃, lost $3.0\ \mathrm{g}$.
