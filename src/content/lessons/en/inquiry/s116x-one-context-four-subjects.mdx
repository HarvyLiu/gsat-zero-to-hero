This is a 116 sprint review. It assumes you took <Zh>自然科學探究與實作</Zh> (inquiry and practice) at school and have finished required physics, chemistry, biology and earth science. The scope of the GSAT 116 science paper is required physics, chemistry, biology and earth science (including inquiry and practice), with the four subjects meant to carry roughly equal shares of the marks. This chapter teaches no new subject content: it uses ideas the subject chapters reviewed and links each one.

## 1. Each subject asks one testable question

The curriculum's inquiry and practice course is cross-subject and driven by themes or issues, and it lists seven <Term t="inquiry.cross-cutting-concepts">cross-cutting concepts</Term>: matter and energy, structure and function, systems and scale, change and stability, interaction, science and daily life, and resources and sustainability. They belong to no single subject, so they make good angles for questions. We use four of them.

Start from four observations and let each subject ask one question. A testable question says **what is changed and what is measured**, and both can be measured:

| Observation | Subject | Testable question |
|---|---|---|
| Thin twigs catch fire more easily than thick logs | Chemistry | For the same mass of wood, does splitting it finer make it burn out in less time? |
| The closer you sit, the warmer you are | Physics | Does a thermometer read lower the farther it is from the fire? |
| Firewood comes from trees | Biology | For saplings of one kind, do more hours of light a day give more dry mass gained in a month? |
| Smoke carries carbon into the night sky | Earth science | How long ago did the carbon in each of the two fuels, wood and coal, leave the atmosphere? |

Each question names the quantity it changes or compares and the quantity it measures:

| Subject | Changed or compared | Measured |
|---|---|---|
| Chemistry | Size of each piece | Time to burn out (min) |
| Physics | Distance from the fire (m) | Temperature (℃) |
| Biology | Hours of light a day (h) | Dry mass gained (g) |
| Earth science | Kind of fuel | Time since the carbon left the atmosphere (years, from data) |

The earth science question is answered by gathering data rather than by an experiment: it compares two fuels and names the quantity to look up, a time (years), so data can test it.

<StudentAsks q="Can't I just ask &quot;What is going on in a campfire?&quot; Why must the question say what is measured?">

It is too big: you don't know where to start measuring, so you can't design an experiment or gather data for it. <Perspective title="Another angle: the cross-cutting concepts as a question checklist">

Above, we picked a subject first and then found a question. You can also go the other way: start with a cross-cutting concept, then see which subjects each question needs.

1. **Matter and energy**: where does the campfire's energy come from, and where does it go? What about the carbon in the wood? This needs physics (fusion, heat), biology (photosynthesis) and chemistry (burning) together.
2. **Systems and scale**: are one campfire and the whole Earth's atmosphere problems on the same scale? This needs chemistry plus earth science (climate change).
3. **Change and stability**: once the fire has burned out, what can return to how it was, and what can't? This needs physics (energy degradation) and chemistry (burning).
4. **Interaction**: how do the fire and the air around it affect each other? The fire uses up the oxygen around it and also heats the air around it.

Starting from a subject gives narrow questions that are easy to test. Starting from a concept gives one question spanning several subjects, which is how you find where the subjects join. Use both.

</Perspective>

## 2. Matter and energy: follow the energy

Four right answers are not yet one explanation. The first thread that ties them together is to follow the energy:

1. **The Sun**: the Sun's energy comes from <Term t="physics.nuclear-fusion">nuclear fusion</Term> and reaches Earth as light ([Mass–energy equivalence and nuclear energy](/en/physics/s116-mass-energy-nuclear/)).
2. **The tree**: photosynthesis stores light energy in glucose and other organic matter. The cellulose in wood is made of many glucose units joined together ([ATP and energy conversion in photosynthesis and respiration](/en/biology/s116-cell-energy/), [Organic substances in daily life](/en/chemistry/s116-organic-life/)).
3. **Burning**: the wood reacts with oxygen and releases chemical energy. A solid reacts only at its surface, and for the same mass thin twigs have a larger contact area, so they react with oxygen faster ([Surface area, reaction rate and advanced technology](/en/chemistry/s116-rate-and-technology/)).
4. **Heat**: <Term t="physics.heat">heat</Term> flows by itself from the hot flames to the cooler people and air ([Temperature, heat and the quality of energy](/en/physics/s116-heat-energy-quality/)).

<StudentAsks q="When the energy ends up spread through the air, is it used up and gone? Wouldn't that break conservation of energy?">

No. Not a joule of the total is lost; the energy is just very spread out. The air is only a little warmer, and the energy can never be gathered back to light a fire. Physics calls this energy degradation: energy is conserved, but its ability to do work falls with every use. What gets "used up" is the quality of the energy.

</StudentAsks>

## 3. Systems and scale: one campfire and the whole Earth

The earth science question asked how long ago the carbon in wood and in coal left the atmosphere. Knowing that carbon goes round, you can answer it from data:

- The wood's carbon was taken from the atmosphere by photosynthesis while the tree was alive.
- Coal formed from dead organisms buried underground over millions of years, so its carbon left the atmosphere millions of years ago. Burning fossil fuels sends large amounts of that carbon back into the atmosphere within one or two centuries, so the carbon dioxide in the atmosphere rises ([Pollution, sustainability and energy](/en/chemistry/s116-environment-energy/)).

Each step right multiplies the time by 10. Compare: coal's carbon took "millions" of years to leave the atmosphere, and burning fossil fuels sends it back within about "a century". How many steps apart are they? About four and a half, so the carbon is returning to the atmosphere tens of thousands of times faster than it was locked away underground.

The earth science chapter on [climate change](/en/earth/s116-climate-change/) says that long-term natural factors cannot fully explain the rapid recent warming, and that current evidence shows human activity is the main factor: burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere and strengthens the <Term t="chemistry.greenhouse-effect">greenhouse effect</Term>.

<StudentAsks q="A campfire gives off carbon dioxide too. So is camping the main cause of warming?">

"Main cause" is about the whole Earth over about the last century. To judge it, first line up the scales: on the whole-Earth scale, compare how much each source contributes and whether its timing matches the warming. The fire in front of you burns for one night, at the far left of the number line: a small system; warming belongs to the whole-Earth system. That is what systems and scale reminds you of.

A school pond is full of algae and water plants. A student puts sensors in the water and records the carbon dioxide in the pond water (relative value) and the pH every 4 hours, from midnight (0 h) to the next midnight (24 h). The cross-cutting concepts this time are change and stability, and interaction.

Each subject explains one stretch:

- **Biology**: by day photosynthesis and respiration both go on, photosynthesis is faster, and the carbon dioxide in the water falls; at night only respiration is left, and the carbon dioxide climbs back.
- **Chemistry**: $\ce{CO2 + H2O <=> H2CO3}$, so carbon dioxide dissolved in water makes it slightly acidic. Less carbon dioxide means less carbonic acid, so the pH rises.
- **Earth science**: day and night come from Earth's rotation. In the Northern Hemisphere summer days are long and nights short, and winter the reverse ([Apparent motion of the stars and the Sun through the seasons](/en/earth/s116-sky-motion/)), so you can predict that the carbon dioxide falls for a longer part of the day in summer than in winter.
- **Physics**: the pond water warms as it absorbs sunlight; at night, if the air is colder than the water, heat flows from the water to the air.

<Perspective title="Another angle: read the trend from the differences">

You can read the trend without drawing anything: as in the [argumentation and modelling](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-argue-model/) chapter, subtract each carbon dioxide reading from the next and list the differences (how much each step changes).

| Interval (h) | Change (relative value) |
|---|---|
| 0→4 | +2 |
| 4→8 | −3 |
| 8→12 | −5 |
| 12→16 | −2 |
| 16→20 | +4 |
| 20→24 | +4 |

1. **The sign** gives the direction: after 4 h the changes turn negative, so the level starts falling; after 16 h they turn positive, so it starts rising. Where negative turns to positive is the lowest point, 16 h.
2. **The size** gives the speed: −5 is the largest, so the fall is fastest from 8 to 12 h, matching the steepest stretch of the graph.
3. The last reading is worth a look too: at 24 h it is back to 14, the same as at 0 h. Within a day it never stops changing, yet from one day to the next it is stable: this is change and stability.

The graph shows the shape at a glance; the differences give exact numbers, handy when you can't easily draw a graph. Plotting itself is practised in [Data reading and written answers in Earth science item sets](/en/earth/s116-earth-data-items/).

</Perspective>

## 5. Join them into one explanation, then conclude

Joined up, the four answers make one chain of cause and effect: Earth's rotation gives the pond day and night (earth science); by day there is light, and photosynthesis takes up carbon dioxide faster than respiration gives it off (biology); the carbon dioxide in the water falls, there is less carbonic acid and the pH rises (chemistry); after dusk the light weakens, at night only respiration is left, the carbon dioxide climbs back and the pH falls, and the next day it all happens again.

Read the figure: the solid arrows are our explanation. The dashed line is another possibility: the water also warms by day, so could warmer water raise the pH? Light and water temperature rise together, so these data alone can't tell them apart.

**A conclusion goes only as far as the evidence.** The conclusion these illustrative data support is: "In this pond, on this day, carbon dioxide was lowest and pH highest at 16 h, which fits the rise and fall of photosynthesis and respiration." It does not say "every pond peaks at 16 h", and it does not say the effect of water temperature has been ruled out.

<Perspective title="Test the conclusion against a rival explanation">

To make the conclusion stand up better, take on the rival explanation on the dashed line head-on: the pH rose because of the water temperature.

Design a fair comparison:

1. Take water and algae from the same pond and divide them between two identical tanks, set in the same warm-water bath so the two tanks always have the same water temperature.
2. Light one tank and cover the other with a lid that lets no light through.
3. Changed: whether there is light. Measured: pH. Kept the same: water temperature, volume of water, amount of algae, starting pH.

If only the lit tank's pH rises, water temperature can't account for the change, and our explanation stands up better; if both rise together, we revise the explanation.

The extra step: write down the rival's claim first, then design an experiment that only the rival can fail.

</Perspective>
