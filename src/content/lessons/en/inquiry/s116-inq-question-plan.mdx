## Four strands, in no fixed order

The curriculum divides the inquiry content of inquiry and practice into four strands, each with a few sub-items:

- <Term t="inquiry.discover-question">Finding problems</Term>: observing phenomena, gathering information, framing or defining questions, proposing testable claims.
- <Term t="inquiry.plan-research">Planning and investigating</Term>: finding variables or conditions, drafting a research plan, collecting data.
- Argumentation and modelling: analysing data and presenting evidence, explaining and reasoning, proposing conclusions or solutions, building models.
- Communicating and sharing: expressing and communicating, collaborating and discussing, evaluating and reflecting.

The curriculum says these items have no fixed order and can be cycled through. This chapter reviews the first two strands; the next two chapters cover the others.

<Perspective title="Write each difference down as a possible cause">

After observing, guess at possible causes. Instead of the graph, list the two days' conditions and write a possible cause for each difference:

| Condition | Day 1 | Day 2 | Possible cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind speed (m/s) | 0.5 | 3 | more wind |
| Relative humidity (%) | 80 | 60 | drier air |
| Air temperature (℃) | 24 | 28 | warmer |

Three differences give three candidate causes. Humidity is not a wild guess: the earth science chapter on [atmosphere, water vapour and clouds](/en/earth/s116-air-vapour-clouds/) said that the drier the air, the faster water evaporates.

There isn't time for all three, so pick a **priority** question. In a classroom wind speed is easiest: change it with an electric fan, measure it with an anemometer. Humidity is hard to adjust there, so keep it the same for every measurement.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="The towel clearly dried faster on Day 2. Doesn't that count as evidence?">

It is evidence, but for more than one explanation: wind, humidity and temperature could each cause the difference, and the records can't separate them. An observation's value is that it **raises a question**, telling you what is worth measuring. To answer it you must set the conditions yourself, which is the job of planning and investigating.

</StudentAsks>

## Gathering information: is this source reliable?

Before doing anything, find out what others already know. Xiaoqing searches online and finds three sources (illustrative):

- **A**: an unsigned online post: "My grandma says clothes always dry faster on a windy day!"
- **B**: a company's advert: "Our power-wind drying rack dries clothes three times faster!" The advert doesn't say how this was measured.
- **C**: a student science-fair report that says which towel was used, how the wind speed was measured and how many times each run was repeated, with the full data table attached.

To judge a source, ask three questions. **Is there evidence** (method and data)? **Does the speaker have something to gain**? **Can it be checked** (has another source found the same)? A has no evidence; B has something to gain and no method; C gives method and data, so it is the most useful.

<Perspective title="Another angle: ask why they would say it">

Above, you looked at **what evidence a source gives**. Another angle is to ask: apart from "it's true", what other reason could make them say it?

- **B, the advert**: even if the claim were false, the company would still have a reason to write it: it sells drying racks. So even with data attached, ask who designed the test and whether bad results would be published. A test by someone with nothing to gain is better.
- **C, the report**: one report could simply have measured wrongly. If another group, with different towels and a different fan, found the same direction, two independent results back each other up.

</Perspective>

## Framing a question and proposing a testable claim

Narrow what you observed and found into one researchable question:

> Does a wet towel lose more mass in 30 minutes when the wind speed is higher?

Next, propose a **claim** (a hypothesis) and write down its **prediction**: "If the water leaves a towel faster when the wind speed is higher, then within 30 minutes the towels at higher wind speeds will lose more mass."

A claim is "testable" if you can say in advance: **this kind of result would show that it is wrong.**

Look at the three possible results: which would show the prediction is wrong? Q (higher wind speed, same mass lost) and R (less lost) both disagree with it; only P supports it. Because you can say in advance that Q or R would overturn it, the claim is testable.

<Perspective title="Check a question with the axes of a graph">

Another check: draw the two axes of a graph for the question. In "the larger X, the more Y?", X goes on the horizontal axis and Y on the vertical, and both must be quantities you can read a number for.

- "Does a wet towel lose more mass in 30 minutes when the wind speed is higher?" The horizontal axis is wind speed (m/s) and the vertical axis is mass lost (g). Both axes can be drawn, so it is researchable.
- "Is it a good thing or a bad thing that towels dry fast?" "Good thing" can't go on any axis, so this question can't be answered with data.
- "Which fabric is best?" First you must say what "best" means, for example "loses the most mass in 30 minutes"; only then can the vertical axis be drawn.

If you can draw both axes, you have found what to change and what to measure: the next section's variables.

</Perspective>

In the exam specification, the Kinmen (<Zh>金門</Zh>) tide item is tagged "finding problems: framing or defining questions"; item 1 of the Waisanding group, mentioned above, tests gathering information and proposing testable claims.

<StudentAsks q="If my hypothesis turns out wrong, was the whole thing a waste?">

No. A hypothesis is a guess waiting to be tested. If the data overturn it, you have learned something and can go round the loop again with a new question. Testable means exactly "could be shown wrong". The real waste is a claim that fits any result, because it tells you nothing.

</StudentAsks>

## Finding variables: change one quantity at a time

The two days' records couldn't separate the causes because several conditions differed at once. A fair experiment does the opposite: **let only one quantity change, and keep every other quantity that might affect the result the same.**

A classmate hangs towel A in front of the fan and towel B across the room. Find every difference between A and B that you can see in the picture.

There are two: A has wind and B almost none; A is thin and B is thick. If A dries first, is it the wind or the thin fabric? You can't tell: the same flaw as the two days' records.

Variables come in three kinds:

- <Term t="inquiry.manipulated-variable">Manipulated variable</Term>: the quantity you deliberately change. Here, the wind speed (m/s).
- <Term t="inquiry.responding-variable">Responding variable</Term>: the quantity that changes as a result and is measured. Here, the mass the towel loses in 30 minutes (g).
- <Term t="inquiry.controlled-variable">Controlled variable</Term>: a quantity that might affect the result, so it is kept the same in every run. Here, the towel's type and size, the starting mass of water, the room temperature, the relative humidity and the way the towel is hung.

Junior-high curriculum content also calls the first two <Zh>自變項</Zh> and <Zh>應變項</Zh> (independent and dependent variable). GSAT 113 item 43 tested identifying variables and predicting results; its CEEC scoring guide required the variables to be physical quantities and the prediction to relate them. The physics chapter on [dimensional checks and written reasoning](/en/physics/s116-physics-reasoning/) shows this way of writing, and the chemistry chapter on [item sets and written items](/en/chemistry/s116-chem-written-items/) uses the three variables too. Item 2 of the Waisanding group is tagged "planning and investigating: finding variables or conditions".

<StudentAsks q='Can I write the manipulated variable as "wind or no wind"?'>

Not really. "Wind or no wind" has two cases and no numbers, so you can't see what happens as the wind gets stronger. Wind speed, measured with an anemometer at 0, 1, 2 and 3 m/s, is a physical quantity that can go on a graph's horizontal axis. Likewise "Is the towel dry yet?" should become "mass lost in 30 minutes (g)".

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Find the variables from the columns of a record table">

Above, the variables came from the question's wording. You can also read them off the record table: each column is one quantity, so see how it changes from row to row.

| Wind (m/s) | Towel | Water (g) | Temp (℃) | Lost (g) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | cotton | 60 | 25 | ? |
| 1 | cotton | 60 | 25 | ? |
| 2 | cotton | 60 | 25 | ? |
| 3 | cotton | 60 | 25 | ? |

- The column you **set** in advance, different in every row: wind speed, the manipulated variable.
- The column you only **measure** afterwards, the ? cells: the mass lost in 30 minutes, the responding variable.
- The columns that are **the same** in every row: towel type, starting water and room temperature (Temp), the controlled variables.

Had a column changed along with the wind speed, say thin towels for all the fast runs, the table would show the flaw at a glance: two columns changing at once.

</Perspective>

## Drafting a plan and collecting data

With the variables in hand, write the plan: method, equipment, procedure and safety.

1. **Equipment**: cotton towels of the same type, an electric fan, an anemometer, an electronic balance, a timer.
2. **Procedure**: wring each wet towel until it holds 60 g of water (check on the balance); find the spots where the wind speed is 0, 1, 2 and 3 m/s with the anemometer; hang the towel, weigh it after 30 minutes and work out the mass lost; repeat each speed 3 times.
3. **Safety**: keep the fan's plug and socket away from water and don't touch the plug with wet hands; hang towels out of reach of the blades.

First do a **trial run**. If a towel loses very little in 30 minutes, the balance can hardly tell the runs apart, so lengthen the time: this is adjusting the conditions until they work.

Data come in two kinds: **quantitative** data are numbers with units, such as "lost 18 g"; **qualitative** data are words, such as "still feels damp". Decide in advance what to record, and design the record table beforehand. Here is Xiaoqing's record (illustrative data):

| Wind speed (m/s) | Run 1 (g) | Run 2 (g) | Run 3 (g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
| 1 | 13 | 15 | 17 |
| 2 | 17 | 19 | 26 |
| 3 | 22 | 24 | 26 |

Read the number line: Xiaoqing has one afternoon, 4 hours. How far does the red stretch run past the end of the day? 8 hours, so the plan won't work.

The fix: run a combination's 3 repeats at once, hanging 3 towels side by side at spots the anemometer shows have the same wind speed. One 30-minute round then finishes a combination, and 8 combinations take $8\times0.5=4$ hours: the whole afternoon, with no slack. Safer: do the cotton towels today and the microfibre ones another time.

<Perspective title="Work backwards: start from the time, then decide how many combinations">

Above, you chose the combinations first, then worked out the time. You can also work backwards from the time:

1. The afternoon has 4 hours and a round takes 30 minutes, so at most $4\div0.5=8$ rounds.
2. With 3 towels hung at once in each round, that is at most $8\times3=24$ measurements.
3. Each combination needs 3 repeats, so at most $24\div3=8$ combinations.

4 wind speeds × 2 kinds of towel is exactly 8; 5 × 2 would be 10, too many. Working backwards tells you, before writing the plan, how many values the manipulated variables can take.

</Perspective>
