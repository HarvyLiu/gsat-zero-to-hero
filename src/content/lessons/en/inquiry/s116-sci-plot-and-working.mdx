## Calculating from a table: first write down which interval you use

The rule behind the opening question works in all four subjects:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\text{average rate of change}\\&\quad=\frac{\text{later amount}-\text{earlier amount}}{\text{later time}-\text{earlier time}}\end{aligned}
$$

The unit of the answer is "the amount's unit ÷ the time unit". The <Term t="chemistry.reaction-rate">reaction rate</Term> in chemistry is defined this way (see [Surface area, reaction rate and advanced technology](/en/chemistry/s116-rate-and-technology/)), and the sea-floor spreading rate in Earth science later in this chapter has the same form. Here are the opening data as a table:

| Time (min) | Gas volume (mL) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 32 |
| 2 | 48 |
| 3 | 56 |
| 4 | 60 |

Each wrong answer to the warm-up matches a common mistake:

- **Counting from 0**: $56\div3\approx18.7$ also counts the gas made between minutes 0 and 1.
- **Forgetting to divide by time**: $56-32=24$ is the change in volume, not a rate.
- **Top and bottom from different intervals**: $56\div2=28$ puts the total so far on top but the interval length underneath.
- **Using an interval the question did not ask about**: $60\div4=15$ is the average over all 4 minutes.

If you copy the "later minus earlier" numbers straight into the working, none of these four mistakes is likely.

## Plotting: placing the axes and choosing a scale

Plotting items have appeared on the GSAT. From CEEC's papers, the first item asking for a curve to be drawn came in 114. Item 50 of the 115 GSAT asked candidates to plot tide data and work out the tidal range, and CEEC's marking notes say most candidates plotted correctly, but only about half got the tidal range right. The basics of plotting (quantities and units on the axes, an even scale, plotting the points, joining them) were practised in the physics and Earth science chapters; here we use them on a new data set.

Recall [Earth's layers and the theory of plate tectonics](/en/earth/s116-earth-layers-plates/): in <Term t="earth.sea-floor-spreading">sea-floor spreading</Term>, new sea floor forms at the mid-ocean ridge and then moves away to both sides, so the farther from the ridge, the older the rock. Here is sea-floor rock on one side of a mid-ocean ridge, with ages in million years (Myr) (illustrative data):

| Rock age (million years) | Distance from the ridge (km) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 27 |
| 2 | 52 |
| 3 | 74 |
| 4 | 98 |
| 5 | 126 |

```dialogue
S: The question says: "On the grid, plot distance against age and draw the line of best fit." So which goes on the horizontal axis?
T: You choose first.
>> turn: q-axis
S: …"Distance against age". My guess is distance goes up the vertical axis and age along the horizontal one.
T: Right: age (million years) on the horizontal axis, distance (km) on the vertical. "Plot A against B" means A on the vertical axis and B on the horizontal, just as the physics graph-reading chapter plotted "I against r" with I on the vertical axis.
```

In an experiment, the <Term t="inquiry.manipulated-variable">manipulated variable</Term> usually goes on the horizontal axis and the <Term t="inquiry.responding-variable">responding variable</Term> on the vertical. These data are observations, where nobody manipulated anything, so you place the axes by the question's wording.

Next, the scale. This chapter assumes the answer sheet's grid has 10 large squares across and 8 up, each large square split into 10 small ones.

<Predict id="axis-step" prompt="The vertical axis has 8 large squares and must reach the largest value, 126 km. Using a value like 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 …, how many kilometres per large square is easiest to read?" answer={20} unit="km">

$126\div8\approx15.75$, but 15.75 km per square is impossible to read. This chapter's rule for scales: make each large square worth 1, 2 or 5 times a power of 10, and pick the smallest such value that fits. At 10 km per square, 8 squares reach only 80, which is too short; at 20 km per square, 8 squares reach 160, which fits, and the data use about eight tenths of the height. Each small square is then 2 km, easy to read. 16 km per square would also fit, but each small square would be 1.6 km, and every reading would need mental arithmetic. The horizontal axis works the same way: the oldest age is 5, over 10 large squares, so 0.5 million years per square fits exactly.

</Predict>

Plot the five points this way, then draw the line of best fit. It should follow the trend of the points, with the points spread roughly evenly on both sides; it need not pass through every point.

## Reading off a graph: read on the line, not at a point

If a question asks for "the slope from the graph", first pick two points on the line you drew. They should be as far apart as possible and sit on easy-to-read grid lines.

Try it first: in the graph above, drag t to 1 and to 5, read the line's distance at each, and work out the slope. On the line, t = 1 gives 25 km and t = 5 gives 125 km, so the slope is $\frac{125-25}{5-1}=25$. Taking the origin and the point (4, 100) on the line gives the same:

$$
\begin{aligned}\text{slope}&=\frac{(100-0)\ \text{km}}{(4-0)\ \text{million years}}\\&=25\ \text{km per million years}\end{aligned}
$$

The unit of the <Term t="math.slope">slope</Term> is "the vertical axis's unit ÷ the horizontal axis's unit", as the physics graph-reading chapter practised. The unit tells you what it means: on this side, the sea floor moves 25 km away from the ridge every million years. That is the spreading rate.

<StudentAsks q="Why not just work out the slope from two data points in the table?">

Because every measurement has some error, and picking just two points throws away what the other three tell you. Using the data points at 1 and 5 million years gives $\frac{126-27}{5-1}=24.75$; using 2 and 4 million years gives $\frac{98-52}{4-2}=23$. Different points, different answers. The line of best fit already combines all five points, so reading from the line gives a steady answer.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="With the table: work out distance ÷ age row by row">

Without a graph, you can add a column to the table and work out "distance ÷ age" for each row:

| Rock age (million years) | Distance ÷ age (km per million years) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 27 |
| 2 | 26 |
| 3 | about 24.7 |
| 4 | 24.5 |
| 5 | 25.2 |

Every row is near 25, but they wander between 24.5 and 27, because each row is the result of a single measurement. The line's slope of 25 falls inside this range: it is the value with all the wandering averaged out. The table shows you how far each row strays; the graph combines them into one line.

</Perspective>

## Showing working: write it out for the marker

Written items in Part 2 of the Science paper must show the calculation or the reasons, or marks are deducted. Exam rules also apply: <Fact id="exam.calculators" />

A bare answer can score zero, as in item 53 of the 115 GSAT. Partial credit is set by each item's scoring guide, not by a general rule: in the 111 GSAT, chemistry item 38 was all or nothing, while item 57 gave half marks for some incomplete answers (see [written items in chemistry](/en/chemistry/s116-chem-written-items/)). So write out the full working for every item, and don't gamble on partial credit.

The question: "From the graph, find the spreading rate on this side, in cm per year." Compare three answers:

- A: "2.5 cm/year." Only the answer.
- B: "25 ÷ 10 = 2.5": you can't tell where 25 and 10 came from, and there are no units.
- C: four steps, as follows.

1. **Relationship**: spreading rate = slope of the line = change in distance ÷ change in age.
2. **Substitute, with units on the numbers**: the line passes through (0, 0) and (4, 100), so rate $=\frac{(100-0)\ \text{km}}{(4-0)\ \text{million years}}=25$ km per million years.
3. **Convert the units**: see the conversion below.
4. **Answer the question**: on this side, the sea floor moves about 2.5 cm away from the ridge each year.

<Graph desc="Unit conversion diagram. The top row shows three words from left to right: km, m, cm, with a blue arrow pointing right between each neighbouring pair, labelled ×1000 and ×100 above. The bottom row shows two words, million years and years, with a red arrow pointing right between them, labelled ×10⁶ above." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 5]} height={200} axes={false} equal={false}
  vectors={[{ tail: [2, 3.6], tip: [3.6, 3.6], color: 'blue' }, { tail: [5.6, 3.6], tip: [7.2, 3.6], color: 'blue' }, { tail: [3.2, 1.2], tip: [5.4, 1.2], color: 'red' }]}
  labels={[{ at: [0.6, 3.3], text: 'km' }, { at: [4.2, 3.3], text: 'm' }, { at: [7.8, 3.3], text: 'cm' }, { at: [2.2, 4.1], text: '×1000' }, { at: [5.9, 4.1], text: '×100' }, { at: [0.2, 0.9], text: 'million yr' }, { at: [5.8, 0.9], text: 'yr' }, { at: [3.6, 1.7], text: '×10⁶' }]} />

Follow the arrows along the top row and multiply the two factors to find how many centimetres are in 1 km. $1000\times100=10^{5}$, so $1\ \text{km}=10^{5}\ \text{cm}$, and $1\ \text{million years}=10^{6}\ \text{years}$. Substituting:

$$
\begin{aligned}25\ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{million years}}&=\frac{25\times10^{5}\ \text{cm}}{10^{6}\ \text{years}}\\&=2.5\ \text{cm per year}\end{aligned}
$$

Answer A is not wrong, but the marker sees no reasoning at all. B's "÷ 10" is really $10^{5}\div10^{6}$, but it never says so. In C you can see where every number comes from, and you can check each step yourself.

<StudentAsks q="Can I write the unit only in the final answer and leave it out of the working?">

Scoring guides differ from item to item, so this chapter cannot promise which way of writing earns the marks. But carrying the units all the way is the quickest way to catch mistakes. Above, if you took "km per million years" to be "cm per year", you would write "25 cm per year", 10 times too big; with the units in the working, the conversion step can't be skipped. This is also what the dimensional checks in the physics chapter are for.

</StudentAsks>

<StudentAsks q="No calculator: what do I do with very large or very small numbers?">

Gather the powers of 10 first, then work out what is left. For $\frac{25\times10^{5}}{10^{6}}$ above, first find $10^{5}\div10^{6}=10^{-1}$, which leaves $25\times0.1=2.5$. Simplify fractions before multiplying, for example turn $\frac{24}{2}$ into 12 first. Writing every step out also makes it easier to find where a slip happened.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Another conversion route: 1 km per million years is 1 mm per year">

Think about it from another direction: $1\ \text{km}=10^{6}\ \text{mm}$ and $1\ \text{million years}=10^{6}\ \text{years}$, so the two factors of $10^{6}$ cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}1\ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{million years}}&=\frac{10^{6}\ \text{mm}}{10^{6}\ \text{years}}\\&=1\ \frac{\text{mm}}{\text{year}}\end{aligned}
$$

So 25 km per million years is 25 mm a year, which is 2.5 cm, the same as the four-step working. Two routes worked out separately that give the same answer make a good check.

</Perspective>

(3) Convert: 1 min = 60 s, so $\frac{12\ \text{mL}}{60\ \text{s}}=0.2$ mL/s. (4) Answer: between minute 1 and minute 3, on average 0.2 mL of gas is produced each second.
