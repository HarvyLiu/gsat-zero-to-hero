This is an exam-skills chapter of the 116 GSAT sprint, for you if you took inquiry and practice (<Zh>自然科學探究與實作</Zh>) and the four required sciences in school. It teaches no new subject content, only how to write it up. All the data in this chapter are illustrative, not real records.

In Part 2 of the Science paper, written items must **show your working or your reasons**. Item 54 of the 115 GSAT was this type, a tick followed by a reason in no more than 20 characters; for how candidates did on it, see the word-limit section of [Earth science data items](/en/earth/s116-earth-data-items/).

Physics, chemistry and Earth science each practised their own way of writing ([dimension checks and written reasoning](/en/physics/s116-physics-reasoning/), [chemistry item sets and written items](/en/chemistry/s116-chem-written-items/), [Earth science data items](/en/earth/s116-earth-data-items/)). This chapter states the rule they share, then practises it in new contexts.

## After the tick: the reason must hold up the one you ticked

A reason that earns points has two parts: **this item's evidence** (a comparison you read off or work out from the chart or table) and a **rule** (a relationship from the textbook that turns the evidence into the conclusion). The answer you tick is the conclusion, so there is no need to say it again in the reason.

Read the figure: at which station is the distance between the two neighbouring isobars shortest? At which is it longest, and how many times the shortest is it?

Suggested answer: A's is shortest and C's longest, about 3 times A's, matching the 100 km and 300 km. The <Term t="earth.pressure-gradient-force">pressure gradient force</Term> is larger where the isobars are closer, and the wind is usually stronger there ([what drives the wind, and weather maps](/en/earth/s116-wind-weather-maps/)). The other reasons in the opening fail: the first only repeats the conclusion, the second has the rule but no evidence from this item, and the fifth reads the evidence backwards.

<Perspective title="Swap the option: does the reason pick out the one you ticked?">

Another check: replace "A" in the reason with B or C, which you did not tick, and see whether the sentence is still true.

| Reason | With "B" swapped in | Holds up A? |
|---|---|---|
| A's isobar spacing is the smallest, so the pressure gradient force is largest | B's spacing is the smallest: false | yes |
| The closer the isobars, the stronger the wind | no station named, true whoever you pick | no |
| Because A's wind is strongest | because B's wind is strongest: no evidence to check | no |

A good reason turns false when you swap the option: it uses evidence only A has.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Both options look right to me. Can I tick both A and B?">

If the question asks you to tick one, tick one. Your reason cannot hold up both anyway: by the swap check, "the spacing is the smallest" is true for one station only. When you can't decide, go back to the data and find the quantity that separates the two, here the isobar spacing, compare it, and then tick.

</StudentAsks>

Read off the graph: how much gas has each group collected at 20 s? When does each stop increasing? Do the two end up with the same amount?

The question: "Which group has the greater <Term t="chemistry.reaction-rate">reaction rate</Term>? Tick it and give your reason in no more than 20 characters." Ticking "crushed" is easy; the reason is the hard part. Here is how a draft gets cut down to 20 characters:

1. <Zh>「根據圖表，我們可以發現磨碎的那一組產生氣體的速度比較快，所以我認為磨碎組的反應速率比較大。」</Zh> ("From the graph we can see that the crushed group produced gas faster, so I think the crushed group's reaction rate is greater.") 45 characters.
2. Cut the restated question and the opening phrases: <Zh>「磨碎的那一組產生氣體的速度比較快，所以反應速率比較大。」</Zh> ("The crushed group produced gas faster, so its reaction rate is greater.") 27 characters. Still over, and "faster, so the rate is greater" says the same thing twice, with no evidence.
3. Swap in evidence you can read off, and connect it to what reaction rate means (the amount of product formed per unit time): <Zh>「20秒內磨碎組產氣量是整顆的3倍，速率大」</Zh> ("In 20 s the crushed group made 3 times the gas of the whole one: greater rate"), $45\div15=3$, 20 characters.

Watch the question's verb too. "Which group" asks for **evidence**. If the question instead asks "**why** does the crushed group react faster", it wants the **cause**, that is, the rule: <Zh>「磨碎後總接觸面積較大，反應較快」</Zh> ("Crushed, its total surface area is larger, so it reacts faster"), 15 characters. That rule is from [surface area, reaction rate and advanced technology](/en/chemistry/s116-rate-and-technology/). Writing only "3 times the gas in 20 s" does not answer "why".

<Perspective title="The other way round: list the scoring points, then build the sentence">

Above, you wrote long and then cut. Another way is to work back from the scoring points: list on your rough paper what the marker needs to see, give each point a few characters, then join them into one sentence.

| Scoring point | In a few characters |
|---|---|
| the quantity compared, and when | <Zh>20秒內產氣量</Zh> (gas in 20 s) |
| the result of the comparison | <Zh>磨碎組是整顆的3倍</Zh> (crushed is 3 times whole) |
| the conclusion | <Zh>速率大</Zh> (greater rate) |

Joined up, that is the same answer as before, and nothing gets cut by mistake: a missing row shows at once.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Do units and symbols count? And if a quantity's name is long, can I abbreviate it myself?">

Go by what the question says. If it doesn't say, the safest assumption is that each symbol such as ℃ or % and each punctuation mark counts as one character: better a few characters short than marked down for going over. When a name is long, use the names or symbols the question already gives; if it calls the groups <Zh>「整顆」</Zh> (whole) and <Zh>「磨碎」</Zh> (crushed), write those. Don't invent abbreviations: if the marker can't read them, there is nothing to check.

</StudentAsks>

The question goes on: "Do the two groups end up collecting the same amount of gas? Give your reason." </TryIt>

Suggested answer: <Zh>「一樣多，都到60毫升，錠量相同」</Zh> ("The same: both reach 60 mL; same amount of tablet"), 15 characters including punctuation. "Both reach 60 mL" is the evidence; "same amount of tablet" is the key to the rule: with the same amount of reactant, the final amount of product is the same, and crushing changes only how fast.

## Variables: write them as quantities you can measure

The chapter on [finding problems and planning an investigation](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-question-plan/) reviewed the <Term t="inquiry.manipulated-variable">manipulated variable</Term>, the <Term t="inquiry.responding-variable">responding variable</Term> and the <Term t="inquiry.controlled-variable">controlled variables</Term>. Item 43 of the 113 GSAT was an inquiry item that tested variables and predicting results directly; under CEEC's scoring guide, variables must be written as physical quantities, and predictions must state the relationship between the variables. On the exam paper, every variable must say **which quantity** it is, followed by its **unit**. The table shows drafts students often write and the fixed versions:

| Subject | Draft | Measurable version |
|---|---|---|
| Biology | how strong the light is | lamp-to-plant distance (cm) |
| Chemistry | whether lots dissolves | largest mass dissolved (g) |
| Physics | height | the ball's release height (cm) |
| Earth science | how windy it is | wind speed (m/s) |

<Perspective title="The unit check: if you can name a unit and an instrument, you can measure it">

Another check ignores the definitions and asks two questions only: what is its unit? What do you measure it with? Only if you can answer both is it a variable you can put on the exam paper.

- "Height": unit cm, measured with a ruler, fine. But the ball has a release height and a height it rises to, so say which.
- "Whether lots dissolves": no unit, no answer. Change it to "largest mass dissolved (g)", measured on a balance.
- "How windy it is": no unit. Change it to "wind speed (m/s)", measured with an anemometer.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="How many controlled variables do I need to write?">

Follow the question first: if it asks for two, write the two most important. If it doesn't say, write the quantities that could affect the responding variable, each one also written as a quantity. For the water-plant experiment you could write the water temperature (℃), the length of the plant (cm) and the volume of water in the tube (mL). "All other conditions the same" names no quantity at all, so the marker has nothing to check.

</StudentAsks>

<TryIt>

A student investigating "how the length of potato strips changes in sugar solutions of different concentrations" wrote: manipulated variable "how sweet the sugar water is", responding variable "how much the potato strip changes". Rewrite both as measurable quantities with units.

</TryIt>

Suggested answer: the manipulated variable is the concentration of the sugar solution (g/100 mL), and the responding variable is the change in length of the potato strip after soaking for 30 minutes (mm). "After 30 minutes" matters too: the length changes over time, so lengths measured at different times can't be compared.

## Writing a prediction

```dialogue
S: I can write variables now. But what do I write for "predict the result of the experiment"?
T: Here's a new experiment. A student wants to find out how, at 25 ℃, the mass of water is related to the largest mass of solid X it can dissolve. He adds X to different masses of water and stirs until no more will dissolve, keeping the water at 25 ℃ throughout. Which prediction is written best?
>> turn: q-predict
S: I was going to pick "the higher the temperature, the more dissolves". It sounds just like the textbook.
T: But the temperature stays at 25 ℃ throughout this experiment: it is a controlled variable, not the quantity you change. The best is the second, "the larger the mass of water, the larger the mass of X that can dissolve": it connects the manipulated and responding variables, and its direction agrees with the rule for dissolving.
```

A prediction must do two things: name the two quantities, **the manipulated and the responding variables**, and say **how they change together**. If there's a rule you can use, you can make the prediction more specific.

<Predict id="sol-water" prompt="At 25 ℃, 40 g of water dissolves at most 12 g of X (illustrative data). At the same temperature, with 150 g of water instead, what is the largest mass of X, in grams, that dissolves?" answer={45} unit="g">

$12\div40=0.3$: at this temperature, each gram of water dissolves at most 0.3 g of X. So 150 g of water dissolves $150\times0.3=45$ g. <Term t="chemistry.solubility">Solubility</Term> is the most solute a given amount of solvent can dissolve at a fixed temperature, so $\frac{150}{40}=3.75$ times the water dissolves 3.75 times the X ([solutions, concentration and solubility](/en/chemistry/s116-solutions/)).

</Predict>

Drag $w$ to 150 and read off about 45 g; then drag it to 100 and read off 30 g, which is exactly how solubility is written: "30 g per 100 g of water". The line passes through the origin, so the two quantities are directly proportional.

<Perspective title="In a table: a constant ratio is what makes it proportional">

Without a graph, you can also fill the prediction in as a table, with an extra column for the ratio:

| water (g) | largest mass of X dissolved (g) | X ÷ water |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | 12 | 0.3 |
| 100 | 30 | 0.3 |
| 150 | 45 | 0.3 |

The ratio is 0.3 in every row, so you can write "directly proportional". Once the experiment is done, fill this column with the real data: if the ratio stays about the same, that supports the prediction; if it keeps growing or shrinking, the relationship is not proportional.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="If one goes up as the other goes up, can I write 'directly proportional'?">

No. Direct proportion needs the ratio of the two quantities to stay the same; "one goes up as the other goes up" gives only the direction. The pendulum in [what physics is](/en/physics/s116-physics-intro/) is an example: making it 4 times longer only doubles its period. Both grow together, but they are not proportional. Without a rule, or data showing a constant ratio, give only the direction.

</StudentAsks>
