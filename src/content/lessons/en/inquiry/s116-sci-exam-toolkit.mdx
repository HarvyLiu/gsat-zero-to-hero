## Tool 1: dimension checks

**Recap (exam skill).** There are only three rules. You can add, subtract or compare only quantities with the same dimensions. When you multiply or divide, the units multiply or divide too and cancel like algebraic symbols. Both sides of an equation must have the same dimensions. A formula whose units don't match must be wrong, but a formula whose units do match is not necessarily right.

The opening problem was a chemistry concentration formula. Now try a chemistry rate formula. Add a little manganese dioxide to hydrogen peroxide solution on a balance and record the loss in mass (illustrative data):

$$
\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}
$$

Assume that all the mass lost is oxygen escaping.

| 1 | 0.80 |
| 2 | 1.28 |
| 3 | 1.60 |
| 4 | 1.76 |

| 6 | 1.84 |

Check: $\dfrac{1.28\ \mathrm{g}}{2\ \mathrm{min}}=0.64\ \mathrm{g/min}$. Suppose the question wants the rate as moles of oxygen per minute. One student writes $r=\dfrac{\Delta m}{M\,\Delta t}$ and another writes $r=\dfrac{M\,\Delta m}{\Delta t}$, where $M=32\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ is the <Term t="chemistry.molar-mass">molar mass</Term> of oxygen. Put the units in:

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm{g}}{(\mathrm{g/mol})\cdot\mathrm{min}}&=\mathrm{mol/min}\\[6pt]\frac{(\mathrm{g/mol})\cdot\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{min}}&=\mathrm{g^2/(mol\cdot min)}\end{aligned}
$$

Only the first is "moles per minute". So $r=\dfrac{1.28}{32\times2}=0.020\ \mathrm{mol/min}$.

<Perspective title="Put in simple numbers, but don't choose 1">

Another check ignores the units and puts in numbers whose answer you know at a glance. For oxygen, $M=32\ \mathrm{g/mol}$. If 32 g escape in 1 minute, that is 1 mole in 1 minute, so the rate should be $1\ \mathrm{mol/min}$.

- $\dfrac{\Delta m}{M\,\Delta t}=\dfrac{32}{32\times1}=1$: right.
- $\dfrac{M\,\Delta m}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{32\times32}{1}=1024$: wrong.

But be careful: $\Delta t=1$ cannot tell whether $\Delta t$ is on top or underneath. Something like $\dfrac{\Delta m\,\Delta t}{M}$ also gives 1. Use 64 g in 2 minutes instead, which is still 1 mole per minute: $\dfrac{64}{32\times2}=1$, right; $\dfrac{64\times2}{32}=4$, wrong. Only numbers other than 1 catch a quantity in the wrong place.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Do chemistry units also have to be broken down into kg, m and s?">

No. Treat mol, L, g and min as symbols and cancel them, as the $\mathrm{g}$ and $\mathrm{g}$ cancelled above to leave $\mathrm{mol/min}$. What you do need is one unit for each kind of quantity: you can't add mL to L, and you can't mix min and s in one formula. Convert first.

</StudentAsks>

## Tool 2: ionic equations conserve both atoms and charge

**Recap (an extension the GSAT has tested).** The number of atoms of each element is the same before and after a reaction. When there are ions, the **total charge** on the two sides must also be equal. With the algebraic method, that means one more equation, for charge, on top of the atom equations, and the answer is written with the smallest whole-number coefficients.

A student writes this ionic equation:

$$
\ce{Cu + Ag+ -> Cu^2+ + Ag}
$$

Count the atoms: copper 1 and 1, silver 1 and 1, both balanced. Count the charge: $+1$ on the left, $+2$ on the right, not balanced. So the equation is not yet balanced.

<StudentAsks q="If the charge balances, do the atoms automatically balance?">

No: count both. The equation you had at $a=2$, $c=1$, $\ce{Cu + 2Ag+ -> Cu^2+ + Ag}$, has charge $+2$ on both sides, but 2 silvers on the left and only 1 on the right. The other way round, the first equation, $\ce{Cu + Ag+ -> Cu^2+ + Ag}$, had balanced atoms and unbalanced charge. One equation for atoms, one for charge: it is balanced only when both pass.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Another angle: count line by line, then see whether it reduces">

Once you have an answer, you can skip the bars, count everything in a table, and then check whether the coefficients reduce. Check another student's answer, $\ce{2Cu + 4Ag+ -> 2Cu^2+ + 4Ag}$:

| Count | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| copper atoms | 2 | 2 |
| silver atoms | 4 | 4 |

Divide them all by 2 to get $\ce{Cu + 2Ag+ -> Cu^2+ + 2Ag}$. The bars let you watch both conservation checks at once as you drag; the table writes them down row by row, and its extra reducing step catches an answer that is balanced but doubled.

</Perspective>

Now one with $\ce{H+}$ and water. Here is an ionic equation in acidic solution, not yet balanced; the <Term t="chemistry.oxidizing-agent">oxidising agent</Term> in it is oxygen:

$$
\ce{O2 + Fe^2+ + H+ -> Fe^3+ + H2O}
$$

<Predict id="tk-o2-fe" prompt="With the smallest whole-number coefficients, what is the coefficient of Fe²⁺?" answer={4}>

Let the coefficient of $\ce{O2}$ be 1, and call the coefficients of $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ $b$, $c$, $e$ and $f$:

1. Oxygen: $2=f$. Hydrogen: $c=2f=4$. Iron: $e=b$.
2. Charge: $2b+4$ on the left and $3e=3b$ on the right, so $2b+4=3b$ and $b=4$.

$$
\ce{O2 + 4Fe^2+ + 4H+ -> 4Fe^3+ + 2H2O}
$$

The coefficient of $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is 4. Check the charge: $4\times(+2)+4\times(+1)=+12$ on the left and $4\times(+3)=+12$ on the right. The coefficients 1, 4, 4, 4 and 2 have no common factor, so they are the smallest whole numbers.

</Predict>

## Tool 3: Lenz's law

**Recap (exam skill).** The three steps for the direction of an induced current (which way the original field points and whether it is getting stronger or weaker; the induced field opposes that change; the right-hand grip rule) are in the Lenz's law section of [Magnetic effects of current and electromagnetic induction](/en/physics/s116-induction/). Here we only practise them in new settings.

The new setting is a vibration sensor of our own design. A horizontal coil lies flat on a table, connected to a current meter. Directly above it a magnet hangs from a spring, N pole down. When the table shakes, the magnet bounces up and down.

```dialogue
S: This is a lot like the bar magnet and coil in the physics chapter, just turned upright.
T: Judge one instant first. The magnet is moving down, towards the coil. Looking down from above, is the induced current in the coil clockwise or anticlockwise?
>> turn: q-lenz
S: The N pole is down, and field lines come out of the N pole, so they pass down through the coil. The original field points down and is getting stronger. The induced field opposes the strengthening, so it points up, towards me looking from above. Right thumb towards me, and the fingers curl anticlockwise.
T: Right: seen from above, it is anticlockwise. But the magnet doesn't keep going down: it bounces.
```

The graph shows the magnet's height above the coil against time: red where the magnet is moving down, blue where it is moving up.

<TryIt>

From the answer you just found, which way does the current go, seen from above, while the magnet moves down (red)? And while it moves up (blue)? At which times does the current change direction, and where is the magnet then?

</TryIt>

Suggested answer: moving down, the magnet approaches, so the current is anticlockwise. Moving up, it recedes: the downward original field gets weaker, the induced field "tops up" what is being lost and also points down, away from you looking from above, so the current is **clockwise**. The current changes direction at 1, 2 and 3 s, when the magnet is at its lowest or highest point and about to turn round: at that instant it is still, the field through the coil is not changing, and the current is zero. The direction depends only on which way the magnet is moving, not on how high it is; the meter's needle swings back and forth, and that is how the sensor "feels" the shaking.

<StudentAsks q="What if I turn the magnet over, so the S pole is at the bottom?">

Everything flips. Field lines go into the S pole, so the original field at the coil now points **up**. As the magnet moves down towards the coil, the upward field gets stronger, the induced field points down, and seen from above the current is clockwise; moving up and away, it is anticlockwise. Don't memorise "approaching means anticlockwise": every time, start again from "which way does the original field point, and is it getting stronger or weaker?"

</StudentAsks>

## Mixed drill: decide which tool first

An exam item won't tell you which tool to use. Here are three problems mixed together, from different subjects.

1. A solution of concentration $C_1$ and volume $V_1$ is diluted with water to volume $V_2$. A student writes the new concentration as $C_2=\dfrac{C_1V_2}{V_1}$. Is the formula reasonable?
2. Balance $\ce{Fe^3+ + I- -> Fe^2+ + I2}$.
3. A horizontal coil lies flat on a table, with a magnet fixed directly above it, S pole down. The coil is slid sideways along the table, away from beneath the magnet. Seen from above, is the induced current in the coil clockwise or anticlockwise?

<TryIt>

Don't work anything out yet. Write down the tool each problem needs, then read on.

</TryIt>

Check: problem 1 is a formula, so use a dimension check; problem 2 is an ionic equation, so conserve atoms and charge; problem 3 asks for the direction of an induced current, so use Lenz's law.

- **Problem 1**: $\dfrac{(\mathrm{mol/L})\cdot\mathrm{L}}{\mathrm{L}}=\mathrm{mol/L}$. The units pass, yet the formula is still wrong. Look at the trend: adding water makes $V_2>V_1$, so the concentration should go down, but this formula makes $C_2$ bigger. The right formula is $C_2=\dfrac{C_1V_1}{V_2}$. Passing a dimension check does not make a formula right.
- **Problem 2**: first make the iodine $\ce{2I-}$. Write $a\,\ce{Fe^3+}+2\,\ce{I-}\longrightarrow a\,\ce{Fe^2+}+\ce{I2}$; charge conservation gives $3a-2=2a$, so $a=2$: $\ce{2Fe^3+ + 2I- -> 2Fe^2+ + I2}$. Check: $+4$ on both sides.
- **Problem 3**: this time the coil moves. Field lines go into the S pole, so the original field at the coil points up; as the coil slides away, the field through it gets weaker, so the induced field points the same way, up, towards you looking from above, and the current is anticlockwise.

<Perspective title="Problem 1 from another angle: the moles of solute don't change">

You can also derive the right formula for problem 1 directly instead of checking one. Adding water adds only solvent, so the moles of solute stay the same. This is how the [Solutions, concentration and solubility](/en/chemistry/s116-solutions/) chapter handles dilution:

$$
\begin{aligned}&n=C_1V_1=C_2V_2\\\Longrightarrow\ &C_2=\frac{C_1V_1}{V_2}\end{aligned}
$$

Test it with numbers: $100\ \mathrm{mL}$ of a $0.50\ \mathrm{mol/L}$ solution diluted to $250\ \mathrm{mL}$ gives $C_2=\dfrac{0.50\times100}{250}=0.20\ \mathrm{mol/L}$, smaller than before, as it should be. This also shows what the three tools share: each looks for something that stays the same. Units, atoms, charge and moles of solute all do.
