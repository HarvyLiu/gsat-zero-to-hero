This is a 116 sprint review: it assumes you took <Zh>自然科學探究與實作</Zh> (natural-science inquiry and practice) in school and have read the two chapters [Finding problems and planning an investigation](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-question-plan/) and [Argumentation and modelling](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-argue-model/). The curriculum splits <Term t="inquiry.share-communicate">communicating and sharing</Term> into three parts: **expressing and communicating**, **collaborating and discussing**, and **evaluating and reflecting**. This chapter reviews them in that order, then looks at how scientific explanations are tested and revised.

## Expressing and communicating: give the quantities, units and range

The good conclusion in the opening does three things:

1. **The quantity you changed** is a measurable quantity with its unit: "water temperature (℃)", not "how hot it is".
2. **The quantity you measured** is also measurable: "time to dissolve completely (s)", not "how well it dissolves".
3. **It only covers the range the data support**: 20 ℃ to 60 ℃. You did not test 80 ℃, so you draw no conclusion about 80 ℃.

Tables and graphs work the same way: column headings and axes read "quantity (unit)". Here is the same data as a graph.

<StudentAsks q="Does a conclusion have to include numbers? Can't I just write 'the hotter the water, the faster it dissolves'?">

A trend statement is fine, but name the quantities: "the hotter the water, the shorter the time to dissolve completely" tells the reader you measured time. Then add the range, "between 20 ℃ and 60 ℃", so they know where the conclusion holds. You don't have to write every number, but the quantities, units and range must be there.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Switch to the reader's side: rebuild the experiment from the report">

When you've written a report, read it again as someone else. Pretend you're in another group and this report is all you have. Can you find these four things in it?

| What the reader looks for | Does the report say? |
|---|---|
| What was changed? | Water temperature: 20, 40, 60 ℃ |
| What was measured, and how? | Time for one sugar cube to dissolve completely (s); the stopwatch is stopped when the cube has disappeared |
| What was kept the same? | Amount of water, whether it was stirred |
| How many times was each condition run? | Not written yet |

If any row has no answer, the report isn't finished. This checks your writing from outside the writer's head: things the writer takes for granted are often exactly what the reader doesn't know.

</Perspective>

## Collaborating and discussing: when two groups' data disagree

```dialogue
T: Several groups in the class did the same experiment. Groups A and B both say they followed the same steps, and each timed one cube at 60 ℃ (illustrative data): group A got 40 s, group B 70 s.
S: That's 30 seconds apart. That's a lot.
T: What should the two groups do first?
>> turn: q-disagree
S: First compare how the two groups actually did it. When they compare, they find the report didn't give the size of the sugar cubes, and group B used a different packet with bigger cubes.
T: The cube size is a condition nobody controlled. Averaging to 55 s, or deleting one group's result, would hide that difference. The two groups switch to the same packet of sugar, repeat under the same conditions, and then compare.
```

In the repeat, each group times three cubes at 60 ℃ (illustrative data).

<Predict id="spread-b" prompt="Both groups' three runs average 40 s. Of group B's three measurements, guess how many seconds the one farthest from the average is away from it." answer={10} unit="s">

Group A's three times are 38, 40 and 42 s; group B's are 30, 40 and 50 s. Group B's farthest run is $50-40=10$ s from the average, while group A's are at most $42-40=2$ s away. The averages are the same, but the data are spread out very differently: group B's largest and smallest values are $50-30=20$ s apart, group A's only $42-38=4$ s.

</Predict>

Read the plot: if you could borrow just one measurement from one group, which group would you borrow from? Why isn't the average enough? Group B's spread may come from judging the moment of "completely dissolved" differently each time. That's why the "how was it measured" row of the report needs to be clear: exactly when the stopwatch is stopped.

<Perspective title="The same data, written a different way">

When you report repeated measurements, you can give the mean and the range together:

- Group A: mean 40 s (38–42 s, 3 runs)
- Group B: mean 40 s (30–50 s, 3 runs)

Writing only "40 s" makes the two groups look identical; adding the range lets readers judge for themselves which group's data are more consistent. This says the same thing as the dot plot above: the plot shows the spread to the eye, and this format states it in words and numbers.

</Perspective>

## Evaluating and reflecting: find the problems in a design, and propose improvements

To judge someone else's experiment, first run through the variables you have already met. Is there only one <Term t="inquiry.manipulated-variable">manipulated variable</Term>? Is the <Term t="inquiry.responding-variable">responding variable</Term> a measurable quantity? Were the <Term t="inquiry.controlled-variable">controlled variables</Term> really kept the same? How many times was each condition run?

**Example**　A student wants to know whether a lid reduces evaporation of the water in a cup. He pours $200\ \mathrm{mL}$ of water into each of two identical cups: cup A gets a lid, cup B doesn't, and after three days he measures the water left. The figure shows where the two cups stood.

<Graph desc="Sketch of where the two cups stood. On the left is cup A, with a lid across its top; the sun is above it, and a dashed ray of light reaches cup A. On the right is cup B, with no lid, under an eave that blocks the sunlight. The water in both cups is at the same level." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 6]} height={220} axes={false}
  circles={[{ center: [1.2, 5.2], r: 0.45, color: 'amber' }]}
  polygons={[{ points: [[1.8, 0.8], [3.6, 0.8], [3.6, 2.4], [1.8, 2.4]], color: 'blue' }, { points: [[6.2, 0.8], [8, 0.8], [8, 2.4], [6.2, 2.4]], color: 'blue' }, { points: [[5.2, 4.4], [9.6, 4.4], [9.6, 4.9], [5.2, 4.9]], color: 'ink' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [0.3, 0.8], to: [9.7, 0.8] }, { from: [1.8, 0.8], to: [1.8, 3.2] }, { from: [3.6, 0.8], to: [3.6, 3.2] }, { from: [6.2, 0.8], to: [6.2, 3.2] }, { from: [8, 0.8], to: [8, 3.2] }, { from: [1.6, 3.35], to: [3.8, 3.35] }, { from: [1.6, 4.8], to: [2.5, 3.7], color: 'amber', dashed: true }]}
  labels={[{ at: [2.1, 0.1], text: 'cup A' }, { at: [6.5, 0.1], text: 'cup B' }, { at: [3.9, 3.2], text: 'lid' }, { at: [5.4, 5.1], text: 'eave' }]} />

Look at the figure, and suppose that after three days cup B has lost less water than cup A. Does that show the lid is useless? Think first, then read the evaluation below.

1. **Find a problem**: the cups differ in more than the lid. They also stand in different places: cup A gets sunlight, cup B is under the eave. The location is an uncontrolled variable, so the result can't tell whether the lid or the location caused it.
2. **Find another problem**: each condition has only one cup and nothing was repeated, so a chance difference can't be ruled out.
3. **Propose an improvement**: put the cups in the same place so that only the lid differs; use three cups for each condition, measure how much water each cup loses ($\mathrm{mL}$), and compare the means and ranges.

In the <Zh>考試說明</Zh> (the CEEC exam specification), the sample item set about <Zh>外傘頂洲</Zh> (waves and the coast) is tagged both <Zh>規劃與研究－尋找變因或條件</Zh> (planning and investigating: finding variables or conditions) and <Zh>表達與分享－評價與省思</Zh> (communicating and sharing: evaluating and reflecting). Evaluating a design often means checking its variables.

<TryIt>

Another group wants to compare how well two flasks keep water hot. Flask A holds 90 ℃ water and flask B holds 70 ℃ water; after an hour, whichever water is hotter is said to come from the better flask. Use the three steps above to write your evaluation: where are the problems, and how would you fix them?

</TryIt>

Suggested answer: the starting temperatures differ (90 ℃ and 70 ℃), so which water is hotter after an hour doesn't show which flask keeps heat better; and each flask is tested only once. Improvement: fill both with the same amount of 90 ℃ water, keep them at the same room temperature, run each three times, and compare how many degrees the water drops in an hour.

<Perspective title="Another angle: let a rival explanation challenge the conclusion">

Besides checking the variables one by one, you can use "check 2" from the [argumentation and modelling](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-argue-model/) chapter: **is there another explanation that fits the same result just as well**? Here it is turned to reflection: while a rival explanation can't yet be ruled out, how should the conclusion be worded?

Suppose that after three days cup A has lost $50\ \mathrm{mL}$ and cup B $20\ \mathrm{mL}$ (illustrative data), and a student concludes that "the lid is useless".

- Explanation 1: the lid has no effect; with or without it, the water evaporates just as fast.
- Explanation 2: cup A is in the sun, may be warmer, and so loses water faster; the lid may well help, but covered cup A still loses more.

Both explanations fit these data, so the conclusion can go only as far as the data hold: "These data can't tell whether the lid helps, because the two cups also stood in different places." Then propose a design that separates the two: put both cups in the same place, and if the covered cup loses less, explanation 1 no longer works.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q='If the other group made a mistake in its design, why not just tell them "you are wrong"?'>

Then they don't know what to change. An evaluation is about the design and the evidence, not the person: say which condition wasn't controlled, how it affects the conclusion and how it could be fixed, so that they can redo it. This is part of collaborating and discussing too: when others check your report the same way, you get concrete improvements back.

</StudentAsks>

## Is the information credible? Check the evidence, not the tone

What you evaluate can also be a piece of information. Here is an online post:

> "After switching to the new bulbs, our electricity bill this month was NT\$200 less than last month. The new bulbs really save electricity!"

The figure shows this household's bills over six months (illustrative data); the bulbs were changed between March and April.

Search the figure: **before** the bulbs were changed, are there two neighbouring months whose bills also differ by NT\$200 or more?

Yes: from January to February the bill also fell by NT\$200, before any bulbs were changed. Bills go up and down from month to month anyway, and the post's evidence is a single comparison, so it can't separate the effect of the bulbs from the usual ups and downs.

In [Finding problems and planning an investigation](/en/inquiry/s116-inq-question-plan/), judging a source meant asking three things: is there evidence, does the speaker have something to gain, and can it be checked. To judge what a piece of information says, you can ask in more detail: "is there evidence" splits into questions 1 and 2 below, "can it be checked" is question 4, and question 3, "is the comparison fair", is new:

1. **Does it say how the result was obtained?** (method and conditions)
2. **Does it give data, not just a conclusion?**
3. **Is the comparison fair?** Apart from the thing being compared, were the other conditions the same?
4. **Can it be reproduced?** If it were done again, or by someone else, would the result be similar?

On question 2 this post gives only one difference, and it fails questions 3 and 4. And as the argumentation and modelling chapter said: two things changing together does not mean one causes the other.

<StudentAsks q="If an expert says it, or it's in the news, isn't it automatically credible?">

Who says something is a clue, but it can't replace evidence. A scientific claim is credible because its method and data are laid out where others can check them and repeat them, and an expert's claim has to pass the same test. So whoever the source is, ask the four questions above.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Turn it round: what evidence would convince you?">

Instead of only picking holes in the post, you can design "good enough" evidence yourself and compare the post with it:

- **Look over a longer time**: compare the average bill over several months before and after the change, not just two months.
- **Find matching conditions**: compare with the same months last year, so the season has a similar effect.
- **Measure the lights on their own**: measure only the electricity the bulbs use, so other appliances don't get mixed in.

Once you can picture what good evidence looks like, you can see what the evidence in hand is missing. This is the same evaluation as before, just started from the designer's side.

</Perspective>

## Scientific explanations can be tested and revised

Back to the sugar cubes. The three points lie exactly on a straight line, and someone proposes a model from it: the time $t$ (s) to dissolve completely and the water temperature $T$ (℃) are related by

$$
t=160-2T
$$

From 20 ℃ to 60 ℃, this model matches the data exactly.

<Predict id="model-80" prompt="Using the model t = 160 − 2T, predict how many seconds a sugar cube takes to dissolve completely at 80 ℃." answer={0} unit="s">

$160-2\times80=0$ s. But dissolving can't happen in an instant, so 0 s makes no sense; at 90 ℃ the model even gives $-20$ s. One group actually measured at 80 ℃ and got 25 s (illustrative data).

</Predict>

When new data don't fit a model, there are two sensible fixes: **state its range** (this line only applies from 20 ℃ to 60 ℃), or **switch to a new model** that also accounts for the 80 ℃ data. What isn't sensible is to call the new data "a mistake" and throw them away.

<StudentAsks q="If scientific explanations keep getting revised, can we still trust science?">

Yes, and precisely because it is willing to revise. Each revision comes from new evidence, and the parts that have passed more tests deserve more trust. The curriculum also points out that scientific knowledge is one explanation of natural phenomena, not the only one, so it has to keep being tested against evidence.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="From the history of science: how evidence rewrote explanations">

The history you reviewed in the subject chapters already has several cases of one phenomenon with different explanations:

- **Burning**: the popular phlogiston theory of the time said that burning gives off phlogiston. Lavoisier used weighing to show that burning, and metals gaining mass when heated, are a substance combining with oxygen. The evidence was mass on a balance. ([Basic laws of chemistry and atomic theory](/en/chemistry/s116-laws-atomic-theory/))
- **Atoms**: Dalton said atoms cannot be divided. Atoms were later found to contain a nucleus and electrons, so that point was revised; but "a reaction only rearranges atoms" still holds for chemical reactions. (Same chapter)
- **The heavens**: through a telescope Galileo saw Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus, which supported Copernicus's Sun-centred model. ([How the study of motion grew: Galileo and Kepler](/en/physics/s116-galileo-kepler/))

What the three have in common: the new explanation won because of new observations or measurements, and only the part that disagreed with the evidence was revised. Revising the sugar-cube model uses exactly the same approach.

</Perspective>

## Writing an evaluation as a reason

Written items often ask you to make a judgement and then give a reason. The method is the same in every science subject: **point to the evidence or the problem, then say how it affects the conclusion.** For example, judging the cup experiment above in no more than 20 characters (Chinese answers):

- <Zh>「不能。」</Zh> ("No.") gives a judgement but no reason.
- <Zh>「不能，因為實驗設計不好。」</Zh> ("No, because the design is bad.") doesn't say what is bad.
- <Zh>「不能，甲乙位置也不同，變因不只一個」</Zh> ("No: A and B also stand in different places, so more than one variable changes") points to the problem (the places differ too), then the consequence (more than one variable, so the cause can't be told). It is 17 characters including punctuation.

For more practice with word-limited reasons, see "word-limited explanations" in [Data reading and written answers in Earth science item sets](/en/earth/s116-earth-data-items/) (115 <Zh>學測</Zh> item 54 asked for a tick and then a reason of at most 20 characters) and "condition, rule, conclusion" in [Answering chemistry item sets and written items](/en/chemistry/s116-chem-written-items/).

Write a one-sentence improvement for the flask experiment above, within 20 characters if you write in Chinese. It must say what to change and what to keep the same. Then count its length.

</TryIt>
