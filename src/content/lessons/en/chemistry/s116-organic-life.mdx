## Organic compounds and hydrocarbons

Most compounds of carbon are called organic compounds; simple carbon compounds such as carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and calcium carbonate are by custom counted as inorganic. The name "organic" comes from the early belief that such substances could only be made by living things. Today laboratories and factories make many organic substances, plastics for example.

There is an astonishing variety of organic substances, and carbon is the reason. A carbon atom has 4 <Term t="chemistry.valence-electron">valence electrons</Term> and can form 4 <Term t="chemistry.covalent-bond">covalent bonds</Term>, and carbon atoms can also join to each other, into long chains, branches or rings. A compound made of carbon and hydrogen only is a <Term t="chemistry.hydrocarbon">hydrocarbon</Term>, such as methane $\ce{CH4}$ and ethylene $\ce{C2H4}$, whose Lewis structures we drew in the [chemical bonds](/en/chemistry/s116-chemical-bonds/) chapter. The alkanes from junior high are all hydrocarbons.

<Predict id="org-octane-h" prompt="Octane is an alkane with 8 carbon atoms. How many hydrogen atoms does one octane molecule have?" answer={18}>

The figure gives 4 for n=1, 6 for n=2 and 8 for n=3: each extra carbon brings 2 more hydrogens. So the number of hydrogens is $2n+2$, and octane has $2\times8+2=18$; its formula is $\ce{C8H18}$.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="Getting 2n+2 from valence electrons">

You can also work it out from the bonds, without counting the figure.

1. Each carbon forms 4 bonds, so n carbons have $4n$ bond "sockets" in all.
2. The carbons sit in a row, and neighbours are joined by one single bond: $n-1$ carbon–carbon bonds, each using 2 sockets, $2(n-1)$ in all.
3. That leaves $4n-2(n-1)=2n+2$ sockets, each taking one hydrogen (hydrogen forms only 1 bond).

So the formula of an alkane is $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}$. If two carbons share a double bond, more sockets are used up and there are fewer hydrogens: ethylene $\ce{C2H4}$ has 2 fewer hydrogens than ethane $\ce{C2H6}$.

</Perspective>

## Carbohydrates: long chains of glucose

<Term t="chemistry.carbohydrate">Carbohydrates</Term> are made of three elements, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and their most important unit is glucose $\ce{C6H12O6}$.

- Monosaccharides: for example glucose and fructose, both $\ce{C6H12O6}$.
- Disaccharides: for example sucrose $\ce{C12H22O11}$, made of one glucose and one fructose joined together.
- Polysaccharides: for example starch and cellulose, both made of many glucose units. They are joined in different ways, so people can digest starch but not cellulose.

When two monosaccharides join, one molecule of water is removed at the join:

$$
\ce{C6H12O6 + C6H12O6 -> C12H22O11 + H2O}
$$

The reverse, adding water to open a join and get the original units back, is called hydrolysis.

A large molecule like starch, built from many small molecules joined one after another, is a <Term t="chemistry.polymer">polymer</Term>. The plastics from junior high are polymers too, for example polyethylene, built from many ethylene molecules; but plastics do not always lose water when they join.

<Predict id="org-tri-mass" prompt="3 glucose units (molecular weight 180) join into one chain. What is the molecular weight of the chain? (H = 1, O = 16)" answer={504}>

3 glucose units have 2 joins between them, and each join removes one molecule of water (molecular weight 18): $3\times180-2\times18=504$.

</Predict>

<StudentAsks q="Carbohydrates are also called 'carbon hydrates'. Is there really water in them?">

No. In glucose $\ce{C6H12O6}$ hydrogen and oxygen are in the ratio $2:1$, as in water, so it can be written to look like "$\ce{C6(H2O)6}$", and that is where the name comes from. But the hydrogens and oxygens in the molecule are each attached to carbon; there are no water molecules inside. And don't mix them up with hydrocarbons: hydrocarbons have no oxygen.

</StudentAsks>

## Proteins and nucleic acids

<Term t="chemistry.protein">Proteins</Term> are built from <Term t="chemistry.amino-acid">amino acids</Term>. Every amino acid has one amino group $-\mathrm{NH_2}$ and one carboxyl group $-\mathrm{COOH}$, so it always contains nitrogen. Proteins are made of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and many also contain sulfur; about 20 kinds of amino acid commonly make up proteins. Amino acids join the same way glucose does: the carboxyl group of one meets the amino group of the next, one molecule of water is removed, and the link formed is called a peptide bond.

The COOH on the left gives up OH and the NH₂ on the right gives up H, making $\ce{H2O}$. The joined molecule still has an amino group at its left end and a carboxyl group at its right end, so another amino acid can join on, and the chain can keep growing.

<Perspective title="Counting atoms: joining by losing water conserves atoms too">

The simplest amino acid is glycine, $\ce{C2H5NO2}$. Two glycines join:

$$
\ce{2C2H5NO2 -> C4H8N2O3 + H2O}
$$

| Atom | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| C | $2\times2=4$ | $4$ |
| H | $2\times5=10$ | $8+2=10$ |
| N | $2\times1=2$ | $2$ |
| O | $2\times2=4$ | $3+1=4$ |

Every kind of atom is conserved: the joined molecule is exactly the two amino acids added together, minus one $\ce{H2O}$. This is the same bookkeeping as two monosaccharides joining into a disaccharide, and it is the common rule for the large molecules of living things: small units lose water and join into long chains.

</Perspective>

<Term t="chemistry.nucleic-acid">Nucleic acids</Term>, DNA and RNA, are the molecules that carry genetic information. Their unit is the nucleotide, and each nucleotide has three parts: a phosphate, a five-carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) and a nitrogen-containing base. So nucleic acids contain five elements: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus. Many nucleotides join one after another into a long chain, so nucleic acids are polymers too. The fats and oils of the next section contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

## Fats and saponification

Lipids are a large group of biomolecules that do not dissolve easily in water; this chapter looks only at the commonest kind, fats and oils, which are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. One fat molecule is one glycerol combined with three fatty acids. In junior high you learned that an organic acid and an alcohol react to give an ester and water, which is called esterification; glycerol is an alcohol and fatty acids are organic acids, so a fat is an ester.

<StudentAsks q="A fat is also made of a few units joined together. Does it count as a polymer?">

No. A fat molecule has just one glycerol and three fatty acids; it is not a long chain of many units joined one after another. In the same way, small molecules such as glucose and sucrose are not polymers. The polymers in this chapter are starch, cellulose, proteins and nucleic acids.

</StudentAsks>

When a fat is heated with sodium hydroxide solution, all three ester joins are broken, giving glycerol and three sodium salts of fatty acids; these sodium salts are <Term t="chemistry.soap">soap</Term>. This reaction is <Term t="chemistry.saponification">saponification</Term>:

$$
\text{fat} + 3\,\ce{NaOH} \longrightarrow \text{glycerol} + 3\,\text{sodium salts of fatty acids (soap)}
$$

## Surfactants: one end oil-loving, one end water-loving

Look at one soap molecule, sodium stearate $\ce{C17H35COONa}$. In water it splits into $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{C17H35COO-}$, and that anion has two very different parts:

- A very long hydrocarbon chain, $\ce{C17H35}$: it is like a hydrocarbon, and fat molecules are also mostly long hydrocarbon chains like this. Oil and water do not mix, but oil mixes with oil, so this end is called the **oil-loving end** (also the water-fearing end).
- The negatively charged $-\mathrm{COO^-}$: it is like an ion in an ionic compound and is easily surrounded by water molecules, so this end is called the **water-loving end**.

A substance whose molecules have both an oil-loving end and a water-loving end is a <Term t="chemistry.surfactant">surfactant</Term>. Soap is one kind; another is the synthetic <Term t="chemistry.detergent">detergent</Term>, such as sodium alkylbenzene sulfonate, whose water-loving end is $-\mathrm{SO_3^-}$ and whose oil-loving end is a long hydrocarbon chain attached to a benzene ring (a ring of six carbons).

<StudentAsks q="Na⁺ is charged too. Does it count as the water-loving end?">

No. In water the $\ce{Na+}$ leaves and is surrounded by water molecules on its own; it is not attached to the chain. The water-loving end is the end attached to the chain, the negatively charged $-\mathrm{COO^-}$. So what actually cleans in water is the anion $\ce{C17H35COO-}$.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Hard water: why soap fails, by pairing charges">

The [separating matter](/en/chemistry/s116-matter-and-separation/) chapter mentioned that <Term t="chemistry.hard-water">hard water</Term> contains more $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$, and that soap forms scum in it. Pair the charges: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ carries $+2$ and the soap anion carries $-1$, so it takes two anions to be electrically neutral:

$$
\ce{Ca^2+ + 2C17H35COO- -> (C17H35COO)2Ca}
$$

The $\ce{(C17H35COO)2Ca}$ formed does not dissolve in water; the precipitate pulls soap anions out of the water, so fewer molecules are left to clean and there is less lather. A synthetic detergent's water-loving end is $-\mathrm{SO_3^-}$, which does not easily form a precipitate with calcium or magnesium ions, so it still cleans in hard water.

</Perspective>

The ring around the outside is made entirely of negatively charged water-loving ends, so the surface of every wrapped oil drop is negatively charged, and two drops that come close push each other away. The whole cleaning process is:

1. The oil-loving ends go into the greasy dirt, and the water-loving ends stay in the water.
2. Scrubbing breaks the grease into many small droplets, each wrapped in soap molecules; such a small cluster of soap molecules is called a micelle.
3. The small droplets have negatively charged surfaces, so they repel each other and stay spread through the water instead of joining up again. This is called emulsification.
4. Rinse with water, and the small droplets leave with it.

So a surfactant always gathers at the boundary between oil and water (or between water and air), with each end on the side it loves.

<Perspective title="Looking at the water surface: a surfactant experiment">

Surfactants do more than wrap up oil. Dropped into water, many of their molecules gather at the surface, water-loving end in the water and oil-loving end sticking up into the air. This weakens the pull that holds the surface water molecules together, the surface tension. You can see it like this:

1. Lay a paper clip gently flat on water and it floats; add a drop of detergent beside it, and the paper clip sinks.
2. A water drop on a waxed surface pulls itself into a bead; a water drop with detergent in it spreads out.

Why does the paper clip sink? Reason with the same rules that wrapped the oil drop:

1. The oil-loving end keeps away from water: it can reach into oil, or up into air. The water-loving end stays in the water.
2. So at the water surface the molecules form a layer, water-loving ends dipping into the water and chains sticking up into the air, like the ring around the oil drop with air in place of oil.
3. These molecules squeeze in between the water molecules at the surface, so the pull holding the surface molecules together weakens. The surface tension drops, the surface can no longer hold the paper clip up, and it sinks.

Both show the same thing: surfactants gather at the boundary and change the properties of the water surface. That is what "surface-active" in the name means.

</Perspective>
