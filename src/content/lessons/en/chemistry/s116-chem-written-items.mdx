This is an exam-skills chapter of the GSAT 116 sprint, for you if you studied required chemistry in school. It teaches no new chemistry; it uses what the earlier chapters covered to practise three things: finding clues in charts and stems, writing working and reasons a marker can follow, and writing equations with the smallest whole-number coefficients. All the items in this chapter were written for it as practice; none is an official exam item.

## The paper first: chemistry in the science paper

Take the 115 paper as an example (the 116 layout is not confirmed): the science paper (<Zh>自然</Zh>) runs $110$ minutes and is worth $128$ points. Part 1 is items 1–36, single and multiple-select, worth $2$ points each, $72$ points in all; in 115, chemistry's items in this part were 19–27, $9$ items. Part 2 has six item sets (<Zh>題組</Zh>) of mixed or written items, worth $56$ points, and each written item shows its points at its end.

According to publishers' analyses of the papers, chemistry was worth $32$ points every year from 112 to 115, about a quarter. Chemistry in Part 2 is not always written: in 115, Part 2 chemistry had $1$ single-choice item, $2$ multiple-select items and two written items of two parts each; the written items were worth $8$ points. Cross-subject items appeared every year: item sets with earth science in 111 and 113, with physics in 114 and with biology in 115; in 112 the cross-subject items were mostly with biology.

Chemistry items also use charts a lot. The figure shows, from the publishers' analyses, the number of chemistry items each year and how many of them were "chart items".

Choice items have rules worth knowing too. Multiple-select items are scored like this: <Fact id="exam.multi-select-scoring" />

<Perspective title="Work the multiple-select scoring through">

Take a $2$-point multiple-select item with $5$ options, so $n=5$: with $k$ options judged wrong you earn $2\times\dfrac{5-2k}{5}$ points. Every option is judged: a right option left unmarked and a wrong option marked each count as one wrong.

| Options judged wrong, $k$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ or more |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\dfrac{5-2k}{5}$ | $1$ | $\frac{3}{5}$ | $\frac{1}{5}$ | negative |
| Points | $2$ | $1.2$ | $0.4$ | $0$ |

Each extra wrong option costs $0.8$ points; at $3$, $5-2k$ turns negative and the item scores zero. So treat every option of a multiple-select item like a small true-or-false item, and go back to the stem for a reason before you decide it.

</Perspective>

## Finding clues in charts and stems

An inquiry item set often describes an experiment first, then gives a table or a chart, then asks several items in a row. One order to read in:

1. **See what the item asks first**, then go back for the data you need.
2. **Mark the units and the basis of each number**, such as "per 100 g of water".
3. **Definitions and conditions in the stem come first**: use them even if your textbook never taught them.
4. **Read a chart's two axes first**, what quantity and what unit, then use the key to tell the lines apart.

**Example (an item set written for this chapter)**　A student wants to know how temperature affects the <Term t="chemistry.solubility">solubility</Term> of two solids, A and B, in water. Each time the student takes 100 g of water, adds solid at a set temperature and stirs until no more dissolves, then records the mass dissolved. The stem also explains: dissolving a solid into a saturated solution at a high temperature and cooling it so that crystals form can be used to purify the solid. (A and B are made-up substances; the data were written for practice.)

| Temperature (℃) | $20$ | $40$ | $60$ | $80$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (g per 100 g water) | $24$ | $36$ | $56$ | $84$ |
| B (g per 100 g water) | $36$ | $37$ | $38$ | $39$ |

<Predict id="cw-crystals" prompt="A saturated solution of A is made with 200 g of water at 80 ℃ and cooled to 20 ℃ (the water stays the same). How many grams of A crystallise?" answer={120} unit="g">

The table is "per 100 g of water", and 200 g of water is twice that. At 80 ℃ it holds at most $2\times84=168$ g of A; cooled to 20 ℃ it can hold only $2\times24=48$ g, so the extra $168-48=120$ g comes out as crystals. You can also work out the $84-24=60$ g per 100 g of water first and then double it. Forget the basis and you answer 60.

</Predict>

Find the point on the A curve whose height is 56: 60 ℃. At 60 ℃ the solution is just saturated, and below that crystals form.

Now use the figure for "which one suits this way of purifying?": cooling A from 80 ℃ to 20 ℃ gives 60 g of crystals per 100 g of water; B gives only $39-36=3$ g. A, whose solubility changes a lot with temperature, suits it; B does not. This method is called <Term t="chemistry.recrystallization">recrystallisation</Term>. Required chemistry does not cover it, but it appeared in options on both the 112 and 114 GSAT. Here the stem has already explained how it works, so reasoning from the stem is enough to answer.

An inquiry experiment has three kinds of variable: the one changed on purpose is the manipulated variable, the one that changes as a result and is measured is the responding variable, and the conditions kept the same for a fair comparison are controlled variables (the physics chapter [Dimension checks and writing out reasoning in non-choice items](/en/physics/s116-physics-reasoning/) uses them too).

Skip the curve and subtract on the table. Cooling from 80 ℃ one step at a time, what A gives in each step is the difference between neighbouring columns:

| Cooling | $80\to60$ | $60\to40$ | $40\to20$ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A crystallised (g) | $84-56=28$ | $56-36=20$ | $36-24=12$ | $60$ |
| B crystallised (g) | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $3$ |

The total matches what the figure showed: 60 g per 100 g of water. The table tells you one more thing: A gives the most in the hottest step, because the curve is steepest there. A table is good for exact numbers and a curve for seeing the trend at a glance; item sets often give both, so answer with whichever suits you and check with the other.

</Perspective>

Not necessarily. Look for its explanation in the stem first. ## Calculations: let the marker see every step

**Example**　Methane burns completely: $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$. How many grams of carbon dioxide form when 3.2 g of methane burns? (Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

<Predict id="cw-co2" prompt="How many grams of carbon dioxide form when 3.2 g of methane burns?" answer={8.8} unit="g">

A complete answer has four steps, each showing where its numbers come from and their units:

1. The equation (balanced): $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$.
2. The <Term t="chemistry.molar-mass">molar mass</Term> of methane is $12+4\times1=16\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so $n_{\ce{CH4}}=\dfrac{3.2\ \mathrm{g}}{16\ \mathrm{g/mol}}=0.20\ \mathrm{mol}$.
3. The coefficient ratio $\ce{CH4}:\ce{CO2}$ is $1:1$, so $n_{\ce{CO2}}=0.20\ \mathrm{mol}$.
4. The molar mass of $\ce{CO2}$ is $12+2\times16=44\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so the mass is $0.20\ \mathrm{mol}\times44\ \mathrm{g/mol}=8.8\ \mathrm{g}$.

</Predict>

Compare two other answers. "8.8 g" alone shows the marker no reasoning at all. "$3.2\div16\times44=8.8$" never says where 16 and 44 come from, or why multiplying them straight through is allowed. The four-step answer lays out the whole <Term t="chemistry.stoichiometry">stoichiometry</Term> chain, "mass → moles → coefficient ratio → mass", and you can check every step yourself.

<Perspective title="By mass ratio: draw one straight line">

You can also work without converting to moles. The equation says $1$ mol of methane gives $1$ mol of carbon dioxide, so 16 g of methane gives 44 g of carbon dioxide. The more methane burns, the more carbon dioxide forms, in proportion: the relationship is a straight line through the origin with slope $\dfrac{44}{16}=2.75$.

The line through the origin has slope 44/16 = 2.75. Drag m (grams of methane, 0.4 to 8): a point on the line and two dashed lines mark its coordinates; at m = 3.2 the height is 8.8." x={[-1.6, 8.6]} y={[-3.2, 24.5]} height={300} axes={false}

Drag $m$ to 3.2 and read the height. You get 8.8, the same as by moles; as a proportion it is $16:44=3.2:x$, so $x=8.8$. The two methods are really one: the mole method splits "16 g to 44 g" into two steps, "mass per mole" and "coefficient ratio". When the coefficient ratio isn't $1:1$, the proportion has to use "coefficient × molar mass" on each side, and then the mole method is harder to get wrong.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="If I get the last step wrong, do the steps I wrote before still earn points?">

There is no general rule; it depends on each item's marking guide. The two chemistry written items on the 111 GSAT were different: question 38 (2 points) was all or nothing, while question 57 gave half the points for certain incomplete answers. The marking guide's introduction only asks you to write your solution clearly. So don't count on "working earns points"; the real gain from clear working is that you can check each step and catch your own mistakes before you hand in.

</StudentAsks>

## Giving reasons: condition, rule, conclusion

For items that ask "why" or "explain", write the answer in three parts:

1. **Condition**: what the item gives you and what changes.
2. **Rule**: which chemistry rule you use, and how it acts here.
3. **Conclusion**: answer what was asked, with the value, unit or direction stated clearly.

**Example**　In this chapter, all acid–base calculations are at 25 ℃. Sodium hydroxide solution of pH 11 is diluted with water to 100 times its volume. What is the pH now? Explain why.

1. **Condition**: pH 11, so $[\ce{H+}]=10^{-11}\ \mathrm{M}$; at 25 ℃, $[\ce{OH-}]=\dfrac{1.00\times10^{-14}}{10^{-11}}=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{M}$. On dilution the moles of $\ce{OH-}$ stay the same while the volume becomes 100 times larger.
2. **Rule**: concentration = moles ÷ volume, so $[\ce{OH-}]$ becomes $10^{-3}\div100=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{M}$; then from $[\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}]=1.00\times10^{-14}$, $[\ce{H+}]=10^{-9}\ \mathrm{M}$.
3. **Conclusion**: after dilution the pH is 9, still basic.

<NumberLine desc="A pH number line from 0 to 14. The point 7 is labelled neutral, 11 before and 9 after; the stretch from 9 to 11 is shaded, showing that diluting 100 times moved the pH 2 towards 7." from={0} to={14} step={1}
  intervals={[{ a: 9, b: 11, color: 'amber' }]}
  points={[{ x: 7, label: 'neutral' }, { x: 9, label: 'after' }, { x: 11, label: 'before' }]} />

Read the line: how many steps did the pH move? Towards 7 or away from it? Diluting 100 times is $10^2$, so it moved 2 steps, towards neutral. This matches the pattern worked out with <Term t="math.common-logarithm">common logarithms</Term> in the [acid–base reactions](/en/chemistry/s116-acids-bases/) chapter.

The conclusion must be complete too. In one part of question 57 of the 111 GSAT, writing only "oxidation reaction" or only "reduction reaction" earned half the points; oxidation and reduction always happen together, so the complete answer is "oxidation–reduction reaction".

<TryIt>

A student wrote: "The pH becomes 9 because it was diluted." Compared with the three parts, which one is missing? Write down your answer before reading on.

</TryIt>

The answer has a condition (it was diluted) and a conclusion (pH 9), but no **rule**: that the moles of $\ce{OH-}$ stay the same so the concentration falls to one hundredth, and that $[\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}]=1.00\times10^{-14}$ turns that back into $[\ce{H+}]$. Without that middle part, the marker cannot see where the 9 came from.

## Equations: the smallest whole-number coefficients

When an item asks you to write an equation, check these once you have written it:

1. Every formula is correct.
2. There are as many of each kind of atom on both sides.
3. When ions take part, the total charge is the same on both sides too.
4. The coefficients are whole numbers with no common factor.

**Example**　Propane, $\ce{C3H8}$, burns completely to carbon dioxide and water. Set the coefficient of propane to 1 first: there are 3 C atoms, so $3\ce{CO2}$; there are 8 H atoms, so $4\ce{H2O}$. The right side then has $3\times2+4=10$ O atoms, and each $\ce{O2}$ on the left brings 2 O atoms.

<Graph desc="A schematic comparing oxygen atoms. The left bar has height 2b, the number of oxygen atoms in b O₂ molecules; a dashed line marks the 10 oxygen atoms in 3CO₂ + 4H₂O on the right. Drag b (the coefficient of O₂, 1 to 8): when the left bar just reaches the dashed line, both sides have the same number of oxygen atoms." x={[0, 4.4]} y={[-1.8, 17.5]} height={280} equal={false} axes={false}
  params={[{ name: 'b', label: 'O₂ coefficient b', min: 1, max: 8, step: 1, value: 1 }]}
  polygons={[{ points: [[0.6, 0], [1.6, 0], [1.6, '2*b'], [0.6, '2*b']], color: 'blue' }, { points: [[2.6, 0], [3.6, 0], [3.6, 10], [2.6, 10]], color: 'amber' }]}
  segments={[{ from: [0.3, 10], to: [3.9, 10], dashed: true }]}
  labels={[{ at: [0.6, -1.3], text: 'O, left' }, { at: [2.6, -1.3], text: 'O, right' }, { at: [2.7, 11], text: '10' }]} />

Drag $b$ until the left bar just reaches the dashed line. What is $b$? Find it on the figure before reading on.

At $b=5$ both sides have 10 O atoms, so

$$
\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}
$$

Watch for two traps. The doubled equation

$$
\ce{2C3H8 + 10O2 -> 6CO2 + 8H2O}
$$

also conserves atoms, but every coefficient divides by 2, so it is not the smallest form. And if a fraction appears after setting one coefficient to 1, as when acetylene, $\ce{C2H2}$, burns and needs $\frac{5}{2}\ce{O2}$, multiply everything by 2 and write

$$
\ce{2C2H2 + 5O2 -> 4CO2 + 2H2O}
$$

<Perspective title="The algebraic method: one equation per kind of atom">

If you'd rather not try values one by one, use the algebraic method from the [chemical equations and stoichiometry](/en/chemistry/s116-equations-stoichiometry/) chapter. Let $a\,\ce{C3H8} + b\,\ce{O2} \longrightarrow c\,\ce{CO2} + d\,\ce{H2O}$:

1. C conserved: $3a=c$.
2. H conserved: $8a=2d$.
3. O conserved: $2b=2c+d$.
4. Set $a=1$: then $c=3$, $d=4$, $2b=6+4$, so $b=5$.

The same answer as the bars. The algebraic method's strength is its fixed steps: if the solution has fractions, multiply by the lowest common multiple of the denominators; if the coefficients share a factor, divide it out. After those two steps the coefficients are certain to be the smallest whole numbers.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Aren't fractional coefficients allowed in thermochemical equations?">

Yes. As the [energy changes in reactions](/en/chemistry/s116-reaction-energy/) chapter said, the coefficients of a <Term t="chemistry.thermochemical-equation">thermochemical equation</Term> stand for moles, and they are often fractions so that one substance comes to exactly 1 mol. But if an item asks for the smallest whole-number coefficients, write whole numbers as it asks; the marking guide for question 57 of the 111 GSAT gave only partial points for fractional coefficients. So first check which kind of equation the item wants.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Enrichment (tested on the GSAT): ionic equations must conserve charge too">

The curriculum notes say required chemistry does not cover balancing redox equations, but question 57 of the 111 GSAT asked for the full coefficients of a redox equation with ions, and its marking guide gave full points only when every coefficient was right. The method from the [oxidation–reduction](/en/chemistry/s116-redox/) chapter is enough: conserve atoms, and conserve charge as well.

Here is an example written for this chapter (not yet balanced):

$$
\ce{MnO4- + Fe^2+ + H+ -> Mn^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O}
$$

Call the coefficients $a,b,c,d,e,f$ in order and set $a=1$:

1. Mn conserved: $d=1$; O conserved: $f=4$; H conserved: $c=2f=8$; iron conserved: $e=b$.
2. Only $b$ is left, and charge decides it: the total charge on the left is $-1+2b+8=2b+7$, and on the right $2+3b$.

<Graph desc="A schematic comparing the total charge on the two sides. The left bar has height 2b + 7 and the right bar 3b + 2, where b is the coefficient of the iron(II) and iron(III) ions.
