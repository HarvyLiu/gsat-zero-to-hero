This chapter connects those ideas to last chapter's equations and moles. ## Exothermic and endothermic: where does the heat go?

First, two words. The reactants and products we care about are the **system**. Everything else (the test tube, the water, the air, your hand) is the **surroundings**. During a reaction, the system and the surroundings can exchange <Term t="physics.heat">heat</Term>.

- <Term t="chemistry.exothermic-reaction">Exothermic reaction</Term>: the system gives heat to the surroundings, and the surroundings get warmer. Examples: burning natural gas or a magnesium ribbon, and the oxidation of iron powder in a hand warmer.
- <Term t="chemistry.endothermic-reaction">Endothermic reaction</Term>: the system takes in heat from the surroundings (or energy from outside), so the surroundings get cooler, or energy has to be supplied the whole time for the reaction to go on. Examples: calcium carbonate decomposes only while it is kept hot, electrolysis of water needs a steady current, and photosynthesis in plants absorbs light energy.

Either way, **total energy is conserved**. When the system's chemical energy goes down, the surroundings gain exactly that much. When the system's chemical energy goes up, the surroundings lose exactly that much.

<StudentAsks q="Natural gas has to be lit before it burns. Lighting it gives it energy, so isn't burning endothermic?">

No. The match only gives the small amount of energy that **starts** the reaction. Once it is burning, the reaction releases far more heat than the match gave, and that extra heat keeps the gas nearby burning without another match. Exothermic or endothermic depends on whether the whole reaction releases or absorbs heat **overall**. Compare calcium carbonate: its decomposition stops as soon as you stop heating, because overall it absorbs heat ($\Delta H>0$). But this doesn't work backwards: a reaction that needs continuous heating is not necessarily endothermic. To decide, look at the sign of $\Delta H$ or the whole energy account.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Seeing it through bonds: breaking bonds absorbs energy, forming bonds releases it">

In the chemical bonds chapter you learned that the atoms in a molecule are held together by covalent bonds. In a reaction the atoms are rearranged, and you can think of it in two steps:

1. **Break** the bonds of the reactants into separate atoms. This step **absorbs** energy.
2. The atoms **form** the bonds of the products. This step **releases** energy.

Take $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$. Two H–H bonds and one O=O bond are broken, and four O–H bonds are formed. Forming the four O–H bonds releases more energy than breaking the old bonds absorbs, so overall the reaction releases heat.

## Energy diagrams and the heat of reaction

Draw the system's energy on the vertical axis, with one horizontal line for the reactants and one for the products. That is an **energy diagram**. The difference in height between the two lines is the heat the reaction releases or absorbs, called the <Term t="chemistry.heat-of-reaction">heat of reaction</Term> and written $\Delta H$:

$$
\Delta H=\text{energy of products}-\text{energy of reactants}
$$

- Exothermic: the products are lower, so $\Delta H<0$ (the minus sign says the system's energy went down).
- Endothermic: the products are higher, so $\Delta H>0$.

The usual unit for the heat of reaction is the kilojoule (kJ).

Drag p below r and the arrow points down: an exothermic reaction, with ΔH=p−r less than 0. Drag p above r and the arrow points up: an endothermic reaction, with ΔH greater than 0." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 10]} height={300} equal={false}

Start with $r=7$ and $p=3$: the arrow points down, $\Delta H=3-7=-4$, exothermic. Now drag $p$ to 9: the arrow turns upward, $\Delta H=+2$, endothermic. When you read one of these diagrams, ask just one thing: **does the arrow go down or up?**

Now a real reaction. At the high temperatures of a lightning bolt or a car engine, nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine into nitrogen monoxide, and the heat of reaction is $\Delta H=+180\ \mathrm{kJ}$ for every 2 moles of $\ce{NO}$ formed. On an energy diagram the products line sits 180 kJ **above** the reactants line, and the arrow points up. The energy the system takes in comes from the very hot surroundings.

<StudentAsks q="The vertical axis on an energy diagram has no zero. So how much energy do the reactants actually have?">

We can't measure how much chemical energy a pile of substance holds "in total", and we don't need to. What we can measure and use is the **difference**: how much heat the reaction released or absorbed. That is why an energy diagram usually has no origin marked; you only look at how far apart the two lines are. It's like measuring your height: you care how far the top of your head is above the floor, not how high the floor is above sea level.

</StudentAsks>

This view warns you about a common mix-up: the sign of $\Delta H$ is about the **system**, but the thermometer measures the **surroundings**. An exothermic reaction has a negative $\Delta H$, yet the water temperature goes up.

</Perspective>

## Thermochemical equations

Write the heat of reaction next to a balanced equation and you have a <Term t="chemistry.thermochemical-equation">thermochemical equation</Term>. For example:

$$
\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}\qquad\Delta H=-572\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

Read it as: when 2 moles of hydrogen gas and 1 mole of oxygen gas react completely to give 2 moles of liquid water, 572 kJ of heat is released (all values in this chapter are rounded to whole numbers).

<Predict id="re-half-hydrogen" prompt="Using the thermochemical equation above, how many kJ of heat are released if you burn only 1 mole of H₂ (giving liquid water)?" answer={286} unit="kJ">

The coefficients are moles, and the heat of reaction is proportional to the moles. One mole of $\ce{H2}$ is half the equation, so it releases $572\div2=286$ kJ. You can write this as

$$
\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(l)}\qquad\Delta H=-286\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

</Predict>

  points={[{ at: ['n', '286*n'] }]} />

Drag $n$ and read the heat at $n=2$, then check it against the equation above: exactly 572 kJ. Then read the heat at $n=0.5$ and at $n=3$, and only then check: 143 kJ and 858 kJ. Heat is proportional to moles, and that is why a thermochemical equation can be "scaled with its coefficients".

A few rules for writing thermochemical equations:

1. **The equation must be balanced.** $\Delta H$ belongs to "the moles given by the coefficients, reacting completely".
2. **Every substance needs its state**: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous solution. When the water formed is a gas,

   $$
   \ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(g)}\qquad\Delta H=-242\ \mathrm{kJ}
   $$

   That is 44 kJ less heat than forming liquid water, because steam condensing into liquid water releases heat as well. Different states, different heats of reaction.
3. **Multiply the coefficients by some factor and $\Delta H$ is multiplied by the same factor.** Coefficients can be fractions, because they stand for moles, not for "how many molecules".

<StudentAsks q="Writing 1/2 O₂ means half an oxygen molecule? Isn't that impossible?">

You really can't cut one oxygen molecule in half. But the coefficients of a thermochemical equation are read as **moles**: $\frac{1}{2}$ mole of $\ce{O2}$ is about $3.01\times10^{23}$ whole oxygen molecules, which is perfectly fine. That is why ordinary equations are usually written with the smallest whole numbers, while thermochemical equations often use fractions so that one chosen substance is exactly 1 mole.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Another way to write it: heat as a term in the equation">

Textbooks also often write the heat right into the equation, as if it were a reactant or a product:

- Exothermic: heat goes on the **products** side.

  $$
  \ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)} + 572\ \mathrm{kJ}
  $$
- Endothermic: heat goes on the **reactants** side. For example, nitrogen and oxygen combine into nitrogen monoxide at high temperature:

  $$
  \ce{N2(g) + O2(g)} + 180\ \mathrm{kJ}\longrightarrow\ce{2NO(g)}
  $$

There is only one rule for translating between the two forms: **heat on the right ⇔ $\Delta H$ negative; heat on the left ⇔ $\Delta H$ positive.** So the second equation can also be written $\ce{N2(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO(g)}$, $\Delta H=+180\ \mathrm{kJ}$.

The advantage of this form is that the energy account is easy to see: "substances plus heat" on each side of the arrow have the same total energy, just like the two bars in this chapter's first figure.

</Perspective>

Drag $s$ from 1 to $-1$: the two lines don't move at all; only the arrow turns round. So **when a reaction is reversed, $\Delta H$ keeps its size and changes sign**:

$$
\ce{2H2O(l) -> 2H2(g) + O2(g)}\qquad\Delta H=+572\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

<Perspective title="Proof by energy conservation: what if both directions released heat?">

Suppose someone claims, "Water decomposing into hydrogen and oxygen releases heat too." Let's see what would happen.

1. Burn 2 moles of hydrogen gas into water: 572 kJ released.
2. Decompose that water back into hydrogen and oxygen, and suppose some more heat $Q>0$ is released.
3. The substances are now exactly back where they started, yet the surroundings have gained $572+Q$ kJ of heat for free.

Repeat these two steps forever and you would make energy out of nothing, which breaks energy conservation. So the reverse reaction must not only absorb heat, it must absorb **exactly** 572 kJ: a little more or a little less, and going round the loop would change the total energy.

</Perspective>

## Calculating with heats of reaction: mass, moles, heat

Find the route on the figure first, then start writing.

**Example**　Methane, the main component of natural gas, burns according to

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\qquad\Delta H=-890\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

How much heat is released by burning 4.0 grams of methane? (H = 1, C = 12)

1. The molar mass is $M(\ce{CH4})=12+4\times1=16\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so $n=4.0\div16=0.25$ mole.
2. Methane's coefficient in the equation is 1, so each mole of methane releases 890 kJ.
3. Heat $=0.25\times890=222.5$ kJ.

The reverse question works the same way: to release 1780 kJ you need $1780\div890=2$ moles of methane, that is $2\times16=32$ grams.

<TryIt>

Using the same equation, work out how much heat burning 1.6 grams of methane releases. Write down the route and your answer before reading on.

</TryIt>

Answer: $1.6\div16=0.1$ mole, and $0.1\times890=89$ kJ.

<StudentAsks q="Should the answer say “releases −222.5 kJ”? Where does the minus sign go?">

The minus sign belongs only to $\Delta H$, and there it already means "the system releases heat". When you say "releases" in words, write the heat as positive: "releases 222.5 kJ". Use one way or the other, never both at once. "$\Delta H=-890\ \mathrm{kJ}$" and "releases 890 kJ" mean the same thing, while "releases −890 kJ" would actually mean absorbing heat.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Per gram: comparing two fuels with a proportion">

The example can also be set up as a proportion: 16 grams of methane releases 890 kJ, and 4.0 grams releases $Q$. Heat is proportional to mass, so

$$
16:890=4.0:Q\quad\Rightarrow\quad Q=\frac{4.0\times890}{16}=222.5\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

Apply the same proportion to "1 gram" and you can compare fuels fairly:

| Fuel | Released per mole | Molar mass | Released per gram |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen $\ce{H2}$ | 286 kJ | $2\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ | $286\div2=143$ kJ |
| Methane $\ce{CH4}$ | 890 kJ | $16\ \mathrm{g/mol}$ | $890\div16\approx55.6$ kJ |

Per mole, methane releases more; per gram, hydrogen releases far more, because hydrogen molecules are very light. "Per mole" and "per gram" can give opposite answers, so read carefully which one a question asks for.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Adding and subtracting equations: Hess's law" needs="Elective chemistry: Matter and Energy (not on the GSAT)">

This chapter has two equations:

$$
\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(l)}\ \ \Delta H=-286\ \mathrm{kJ}\qquad
\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(g)}\ \ \Delta H=-242\ \mathrm{kJ}
$$

Subtract the first from the second. The $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ cancel on both sides, leaving $\ce{H2O(l) -> H2O(g)}$ with $\Delta H=-242-(-286)=+44\ \mathrm{kJ}$: evaporating 1 mole of water absorbs 44 kJ. The rule that "equations can be added and subtracted like algebraic equations, and their heats of reaction add and subtract along with them" is called Hess's law; using it and heats of formation to calculate heats of reaction is elective chemistry. The GSAT asks only for this chapter's skills: telling exothermic from endothermic, writing the equations and scaling with the coefficients.

</Perspective>
