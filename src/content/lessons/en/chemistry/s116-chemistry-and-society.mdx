## Purifying tap water: each step removes something different

The impurities in raw water differ in size and properties, so the waterworks uses several steps, each dealing with one kind:

1. **Coagulation and settling.** Alum (potassium aluminium sulfate) is added. In water it forms jelly-like aluminium hydroxide, which sticks the tiny suspended particles together into bigger clumps; the clumps are heavier and sink to the bottom of the tank. Many suspended particles are the size of a <Term t="chemistry.colloid">colloid</Term> and will not settle on their own, which is why coagulation is needed.
2. **Filtering.** The water runs through a bed of sand, which holds back the particles that did not settle.
3. **Adsorption on activated carbon** (only at some waterworks). Activated carbon has a huge surface area and adsorbs the molecules that cause odours and colour.
4. **Disinfection.** Chlorine is added to kill bacteria and other microbes.

What is left at the end is the calcium and sodium ions, part of the answer to the opening question: dissolved ions pass through the sand, and chlorine does not kill them.

<Perspective title="Another view: every step is a separation method">

In the [separation chapter](/en/chemistry/s116-matter-and-separation/), the rule for choosing a method was "first find which property differs". Use that rule on water treatment:

| Step | Property that differs | Matching separation method |
|---|---|---|
| Settling | clumps are heavier than water | standing, decanting |
| Filtering | particles are bigger than the gaps in the sand | filtration |
| Activated carbon | how strongly things are adsorbed | adsorption, as in <Term t="chemistry.chromatography">chromatography</Term> |
| Chlorine | not a separation but a chemical reaction | see the next section |

The first three steps are physical separations; only disinfection uses a chemical reaction to kill bacteria. Dissolved ions are about the size of water molecules, so none of the first three steps can separate them; removing them takes [distillation or reverse osmosis](/en/chemistry/s116-matter-and-separation/), or the softening methods below.

</Perspective>

## Disinfection: killing germs with an oxidising agent

Chlorine dissolves in water to form hypochlorous acid:

$$
\ce{Cl2 + H2O <=> HCl + HOCl}
$$

In the [redox chapter](/en/chemistry/s116-redox/) we saw that chlorine and hypochlorous acid are both <Term t="chemistry.oxidizing-agent">oxidising agents</Term> on the curriculum list. Hypochlorous acid takes electrons from substances inside bacteria, so those substances are <Term t="chemistry.oxidation">oxidised</Term> and the bacteria die.

An arrow labelled e⁻ points from the right box to the left box, showing electrons moving from the bacterium's substance to the hypochlorous acid. Under the left box it says gains e⁻; under the right box, loses e⁻." x={[0, 10]} y={[-1.2, 2.6]} height={190} axes={false}
  polygons={[{ points: [[0.5, 0], [3.5, 0], [3.5, 2], [0.5, 2]], color: 'amber' }, { points: [[6.5, 0], [9.5, 0], [9.5, 2], [6.5, 2]], color: 'red' }]}
  vectors={[{ tail: [6.3, 1.4], tip: [3.7, 1.4], label: 'e⁻', color: 'blue' }]}
  labels={[{ at: [2, 0.8], text: 'HOCl' }, { at: [7.1, 0.8], text: 'bacteria' }, { at: [1.05, -0.7], text: 'gains e⁻' }, { at: [7.05, -0.7], text: 'loses e⁻' }]} />

Read the arrow: which side do the electrons leave? The bacterium's substance loses electrons, so it is oxidised; hypochlorous acid gains electrons, is reduced, and acts as the oxidising agent.

Tap water leaves the works with a little "residual chlorine", so it can keep killing germs all along the long pipes. The amount is tiny and is often given in <Term t="chemistry.ppm">ppm</Term>.

<Predict id="cs-cl-ppm" prompt="A water sample contains 0.5 ppm of chlorine by mass. Two litres of this water have a mass of about 2000 g. How many mg of chlorine do they contain?" answer={1} unit="mg">

ppm is the mass of solute divided by the mass of solution, times $10^{6}$, so the mass of solute is $2000\ \mathrm{g}\times\dfrac{0.5}{10^{6}}=0.001\ \mathrm{g}=1\ \mathrm{mg}$. A big bottle of water holds only about a milligram of chlorine, yet that is enough to keep it disinfected.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="Three ways to kill germs, compared">

All three kill germs, but they rely on different things and suit different jobs:

| Method | Relies on | Does the germ-killing stay in the water? |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine | the oxidising agent hypochlorous acid | yes: residual chlorine stays in the pipes |
| Ozone, $\ce{O3}$ | an oxidising agent | no: ozone soon breaks down into oxygen |
| Boiling | heat | no: once cool, it can be contaminated again |

Tap water has a long way to travel from the works to every home, and may meet microbes on the way, so it needs germ-killing power it can carry with it. That is why tap water is chlorinated; ozone and boiling both kill germs, but they give no protection by the time the water reaches the tap.

</Perspective>

## Softening hard water

Water with a lot of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is <Term t="chemistry.hard-water">hard water</Term>: soap forms scum in it and makes little lather, and heating it leaves scale. The purification steps above do not remove these ions, so the water has to be softened separately. The separation chapter introduced three methods:

- **Boiling.** Calcium hydrogencarbonate decomposes on heating into a calcium carbonate precipitate; this removes only the part present as hydrogencarbonate.
- **Adding sodium carbonate.** The calcium ions precipitate as calcium carbonate, which is then filtered off:

  $$
  \ce{Ca^2+ + CO3^2- -> CaCO3 v}
  $$
- **Ion-exchange resin.** The resin takes one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and releases two $\ce{Na+}$.

<Predict id="cs-caco3-mass" prompt="One litre of hard water contains 0.0020 mol of Ca²⁺, and sodium carbonate is added to precipitate all of it as CaCO₃. What mass of precipitate forms, in g? (Atomic masses: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16.)" answer={0.2} unit="g">

$\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{CaCO3}$ both have coefficient 1, so the precipitate is also 0.0020 <Term t="chemistry.mole">mol</Term>. The <Term t="chemistry.molar-mass">molar mass</Term> of $\ce{CaCO3}$ is $40+12+3\times16=100\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so the mass is $0.0020\times100=0.20\ \mathrm{g}$.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="Comparing the three methods by counting particles">

Skip the equations and count particles instead. Suppose a glass of hard water holds 4 $\ce{Ca^2+}$: 2 of them go with hydrogencarbonate ions (dissolved as calcium hydrogencarbonate) and the other 2 go with chloride ions.

- Boiling: only the 2 from calcium hydrogencarbonate precipitate, so 2 $\ce{Ca^2+}$ are left.
- Sodium carbonate: each $\ce{Ca^2+}$ precipitated needs one $\ce{CO3^2-}$, that is one $\ce{Na2CO3}$, which brings in two $\ce{Na+}$. Precipitating all 4 $\ce{Ca^2+}$ adds 8 $\ce{Na+}$ to the water.
- Resin: one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is swapped for two $\ce{Na+}$, so 4 are swapped for 8.

<StudentAsks q="Is softened water pure water?">

No. The chart shows why at a glance: sodium carbonate and the resin both swap $\ce{Ca^2+}$ for $\ce{Na+}$, and the original negative ions (chloride, for example) are still there. Soft water is still a solution; it just no longer forms scum with soap. To get almost pure water you need a method such as <Term t="chemistry.distillation">distillation</Term>.

</StudentAsks>

## Antacids: less stomach acid by neutralisation

The acid in gastric juice is mainly hydrochloric acid. When there is too much of it, people can take an antacid. Antacids are all basic substances that react with $\ce{H+}$, and they work by <Term t="chemistry.neutralization">neutralisation</Term>:

$$
\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2H+ -> Mg^2+ + 2H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{Al(OH)3 + 3H+ -> Al^3+ + 3H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{NaHCO3 + H+ -> Na+ + H2O + CO2}
$$

$$
\ce{CaCO3 + 2H+ -> Ca^2+ + H2O + CO2}
$$

The last two contain carbonate or hydrogencarbonate, so they give off carbon dioxide gas as they neutralise.

<Predict id="cs-aloh3" prompt="One dose of a stomach medicine contains 0.78 g of Al(OH)₃. At most, how many mmol of H⁺ can it neutralise? (Atomic masses: H = 1, O = 16, Al = 27.)" answer={30} unit="mmol">

The molar mass of $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ is $27+3\times(16+1)=78\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, so 0.78 g is 0.010 mol. By the coefficients, 1 mol of $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ neutralises 3 mol of $\ce{H+}$, so the answer is 0.030 mol, or 30 mmol.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="Seeing an antacid's effect through pH">

Now use pH from the [acids and bases chapter](/en/chemistry/s116-acids-bases/). Suppose the liquid in the stomach has $[\ce{H+}]=0.1\ \mathrm{M}$, so its pH is 1. </Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Sodium hydroxide neutralises acid fastest, so why isn't it used as a stomach medicine?">

Sodium hydroxide is a strong base: it dissociates almost completely in water, its solution is strongly basic, and it corrodes tissue. None of the four antacids above is a strong base: magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide and calcium carbonate barely dissolve in water, and a sodium hydrogencarbonate solution is only weakly basic. They react with stomach acid gradually, and the body never meets a strong base.

</StudentAsks>

## Anti-inflammatories and painkillers

Not every medicine works by neutralisation. Aspirin (chemical name acetylsalicylic acid, <Zh>乙醯柳酸</Zh>, formula $\ce{C9H8O4}$) brings down fever, relieves pain and reduces inflammation; acetaminophen (<Zh>乙醯胺酚</Zh>) is used mainly to bring down fever and relieve pain, and its anti-inflammatory effect is very weak. The name of salicylic acid (<Zh>柳酸</Zh>, literally "willow acid") comes from the willow tree, and willow bark contains related compounds.

These medicines do not neutralise stomach acid. They enter the blood and affect the chemical reactions in the body that produce inflammation and pain signals. Aspirin is itself an acid and can irritate the stomach. Medicines should be taken as a doctor or pharmacist directs, and this is also where chemistry meets daily life: the textbook tells you what a medicine is and how it works, and the dose is left to professionals.

<Perspective title="Seeing a dose in moles">

Medicine labels give milligrams, but chemists think in particles. Suppose a tablet contains 0.36 g of aspirin (atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16):

1. Molar mass: $9\times12+8\times1+4\times16=180\ \mathrm{g/mol}$.
2. Moles: $0.36\div180=0.0020\ \mathrm{mol}$.
3. Molecules: multiply by <Term t="chemistry.avogadro-number">Avogadro's number</Term>, $0.0020\times6.02\times10^{23}\approx1.2\times10^{21}$.

A tablet of less than half a gram holds about $10^{21}$ molecules. To compare which of two medicines has more molecules, compare moles, not grams.

</Perspective>

## Scientists' contributions: varied genders and backgrounds

In the [basic laws chapter](/en/chemistry/s116-laws-atomic-theory/) you met Lavoisier, who checked conservation of mass with a balance, and Dalton's atomic theory. Here are a few scientists from other backgrounds:

- **Marie Curie** (<Zh>居禮夫人</Zh>) was born in Poland and studied and worked in France. In 1898 she and her husband Pierre Curie discovered two new elements, polonium and radium. She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics and won the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry on her own, becoming the first person to win two Nobel Prizes.
- **Yuan T. Lee** (<Zh>李遠哲</Zh>) was born in Hsinchu, Taiwan. Using crossed molecular beams, he watched how molecules react after a single collision, and in 1986 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with two other scientists.
- **Tu Youyou** (<Zh>屠呦呦</Zh>) is a Chinese pharmaceutical scientist whose team <Term t="chemistry.extraction">extracted</Term> artemisinin, a malaria drug, from the plant sweet wormwood. Her 2015 Nobel Prize was in Physiology or Medicine, but the method she used was chemical separation.

## Chemistry's effects on society, and its future

Chemistry's effects can be seen from four sides: **daily life** (clean water, medicines), **society** (public health), **the economy** (the materials and products made by the chemical industry) and **the environment**. The same product often cuts both ways: plastics make life convenient, but once used they become waste; environmental problems such as industrial wastewater and air pollution are the subject of the next chapter.

One direction for chemistry's future is **green chemistry**: reducing waste right from the design of a reaction, and switching to safer starting materials and solvents. Another is new materials. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots, nanometre-sized semiconductor crystals; questions 44–46 of the 115 GSAT were built on quantum dots.

<Perspective title="Seeing one product from four sides">

Take plastics as an example and set the four sides out as a table:

| Side | What it brings | What problem it brings |
|---|---|---|
| daily life | light, durable, cheap containers and packaging | often used once and thrown away, so more rubbish |
| society | disposable medical equipment, which reduces cross-infection | rubbish has to be collected and handled by the public |
| economy | a major product of the chemical industry, providing jobs | recycling and clean-up cost money |
| environment | recycling saves new raw materials | hard to break down, so it stays in the land and sea |
