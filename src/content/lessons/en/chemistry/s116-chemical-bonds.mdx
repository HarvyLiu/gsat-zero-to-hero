## Why atoms bond: getting to look like a noble gas

The noble gases (helium, neon, argon) hardly react with anything: helium has 2 valence electrons, neon and argon have 8 each. Other atoms, when they combine, often **lose, gain or share** valence electrons so that they end up with the same number of electrons as a noble gas, with 8 valence electrons around them (hydrogen needs only 2, like helium). This rule of thumb is the **octet rule** <Zh>八隅體規則</Zh>.

Reading the figure: metals with only 1 to 3 valence electrons (sodium, magnesium, aluminium) are far from 8, so giving their valence electrons away is easier; nonmetals with 6 or 7 (sulfur, chlorine) are only 1 or 2 short, so gaining electrons is easier. Carbon and silicon have 4, and losing or gaining 4 is too many, so they tend to **share** with other atoms. Follow each of the four arrows: the number of steps it crosses is the number of electrons lost or gained. An arrow down to 0 means the ion keeps neon's electron count: sodium $11-1=10$, magnesium $12-2=10$ (compare the table in the next section).

These three moves match three kinds of chemical bond: one gives and one takes is an ionic bond, sharing is a covalent bond, and a whole crowd of metal atoms pooling their electrons is a metallic bond.

<StudentAsks q="Why does hydrogen need only 2 electrons, not 8?">

Hydrogen has just 1 electron, and the noble gas closest to it is helium, which has only 2. When hydrogen shares one pair with another atom, it has 2 electrons around it, like helium. That is why, in a Lewis structure, hydrogen always has exactly one bond and never carries a lone pair.

</StudentAsks>

## Ionic bonds: one gives, one takes

A sodium atom hands its single valence electron to a chlorine atom. Sodium, one electron short, becomes the positively charged <Term t="chemistry.cation">cation</Term> $\ce{Na+}$; chlorine, one electron extra, becomes the negatively charged <Term t="chemistry.anion">anion</Term> $\ce{Cl-}$. The electrostatic attraction between the positive and negative ions is the <Term t="chemistry.ionic-bond">ionic bond</Term>. When a metal combines with a nonmetal, an ionic bond often forms.

</Perspective>

In solid sodium chloride, each $\ce{Na+}$ is surrounded by several $\ce{Cl-}$ and each $\ce{Cl-}$ by several $\ce{Na+}$, on and on, and no $\ce{Cl-}$ belongs to any one $\ce{Na+}$. A compound of cations and anions held by ionic bonds is an <Term t="chemistry.ionic-compound">ionic compound</Term>. First count the + and − in the picture: 8 of each, a $1:1$ ratio, matching the formula. This is why ionic solids are **hard but brittle**: hit one with a hammer and it doesn't bend, it shatters.

<StudentAsks q="Is sodium chloride made of NaCl molecules, the way water is made of H₂O molecules?">

No. Water really contains separate $\ce{H2O}$ molecules; sodium chloride contains only a huge crowd of alternating $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$, with no small unit that is "one NaCl". The formula $\ce{NaCl}$ only says that sodium ions and chloride ions are in the ratio $1:1$.

</StudentAsks>

## Covalent bonds and Lewis structures

When two nonmetal atoms both want electrons, neither will give any away, so each puts electrons forward to **share**. A pair of electrons shared by two atoms forms one <Term t="chemistry.covalent-bond">covalent bond</Term>: one shared pair is a single bond, two shared pairs a double bond. A drawing with valence electrons as dots and shared pairs as short lines is a <Term t="chemistry.lewis-structure">Lewis structure</Term> (the curriculum writes it <Zh>路易士結構</Zh>). It is named after the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis, who in the 1910s proposed explaining chemical bonds with shared electron pairs.

**Worked example**　Draw the Lewis structure of water, $\ce{H2O}$.

1. Count valence electrons: O has 6 and each H has 1, so $6+1+1=8$ in all, or 4 pairs.
2. Put oxygen in the middle and let it share one pair with each hydrogen. That uses 2 pairs: H–O–H.
3. The other 2 pairs go on the oxygen. A pair that takes no part in bonding is a **lone pair** <Zh>孤對電子</Zh> (also called an unshared pair <Zh>未共用電子對</Zh>).
4. Check: oxygen has 2 bonds (4 electrons) plus 2 lone pairs (4 electrons), 8 in all; each hydrogen has 2. Everything fits.

Ammonia and methane follow the same steps. $\ce{NH3}$ has 8 valence electrons, 3 $\ce{N-H}$ bonds and 1 lone pair left on the nitrogen; $\ce{CH4}$ has 8 valence electrons, 4 $\ce{C-H}$ bonds and no lone pair on the carbon.

Now carbon dioxide: carbon in the middle, an oxygen on each side. Guess first, then read the reasoning.

<Predict id="co2-lone-pairs" prompt="In the Lewis structure of CO₂, how many lone pairs are there on the two oxygen atoms together?" answer={4} unit="pairs">

Carbon has 4 valence electrons and each oxygen has 6: $4+6+6=16$ in all, or 8 pairs. If carbon formed only a single bond to each oxygen, carbon would have only 4 electrons around it, not 8. So carbon shares **two pairs** with each oxygen, forming two double bonds, O=C=O, which use 4 pairs. The remaining 4 pairs go on the two oxygens, 2 on each, 4 in all. Check: each oxygen has one double bond (4 electrons) plus 2 lone pairs (4 electrons), 8 in all; carbon has two double bonds, also 8.

</Predict>

Ethylene, $\ce{C2H4}$, has a double bond too: a double bond between the two carbons, and a single bond from each carbon to each of its two hydrogens. All 12 valence electrons are in bonds, so there are no lone pairs.

<Perspective title="By calculation: work out the shared pairs first">

Without drawing anything, you can work out how many shared pairs to draw. Each atom "needs" a certain number of electrons around it: hydrogen 2, the others 8. A shared pair is counted by both atoms around themselves, so each shared pair lets the total need be met with 2 fewer electrons. So

$$
\text{shared pairs}=\frac{\text{electrons needed}-\text{valence electrons}}{2}
$$

- Water: needed $8+2+2=12$, available 8, so $\dfrac{12-8}{2}=2$ shared pairs, exactly the 2 $\ce{O-H}$ bonds.
- Carbon dioxide: needed $8\times3=24$, available 16, so $\dfrac{24-16}{2}=4$ shared pairs. The skeleton has only 2 links (oxygen–carbon and carbon–oxygen), so the 4 pairs go 2 per link: two double bonds. The leftover valence electrons, $16-2\times4=8$, make 4 lone pairs, the same answer as above.

When there are more shared pairs than links in the skeleton, the extra pairs are double bonds.

</Perspective>

Water, ammonia, methane, ethylene and carbon dioxide are all made of separate molecules, with the atoms inside each molecule joined by covalent bonds. A compound made of molecules is a <Term t="chemistry.molecular-compound">molecular compound</Term>. The attraction between one molecule and the next is much weaker than the covalent bonds inside a molecule, so molecular compounds usually have low melting and boiling points: at room temperature, water is a liquid and carbon dioxide is a gas.

## Covalent network solids and allotropes

```dialogue
S: Carbon dioxide and silicon dioxide have almost the same formula, and carbon and silicon both have 4 valence electrons.
T: Yet dry ice sublimes away at room temperature, while quartz (mostly silicon dioxide) is very hard and melts only at a very high temperature. Where do you think the difference lies?
>> turn: q-mid
S: So silicon dioxide isn't made of separate molecules at all!
T: Right. When dry ice sublimes, the molecules are only pulled apart from each other, and not one C=O bond inside a molecule breaks. To melt quartz, you have to break the covalent bonds between atoms.
```

In silicon dioxide, each silicon atom is joined to 4 oxygen atoms by covalent bonds and each oxygen atom to 2 silicon atoms, on and on in one huge network with no separate molecules. A solid of atoms joined into a network by covalent bonds is a **covalent network solid** <Zh>共價網狀固體</Zh>: very high melting point, very hard, and usually not a conductor. The element silicon is itself a covalent network solid, with each silicon joined to 4 silicons; it conducts electricity somewhere between a conductor and an insulator, so it is a semiconductor.

<Perspective title="A formula from counting neighbours">

A network solid has no molecules, so where does the formula $\ce{SiO2}$ come from? Count it off the picture. Each silicon is joined to 4 oxygens, but each oxygen is shared by 2 silicons, so only half of each oxygen "counts" toward any one silicon. One silicon gets $4\times\frac12=2$ oxygens, so silicon and oxygen are in the ratio $1:2$: $\ce{SiO2}$.

Check again with valence electrons: silicon has 4, forms 4 single bonds and has 8 around it; oxygen has 6, forms 2 single bonds and keeps 2 lone pairs, so it also has 8. Both views give the same structure.

</Perspective>

The element carbon also comes in several structures. Substances made of the same single element but with different structures are <Term t="chemistry.allotrope">allotropes</Term> of each other, like oxygen $\ce{O2}$ and ozone $\ce{O3}$ from junior high. The allotropes of carbon include:

- **Diamond**: each carbon is joined to 4 carbons by covalent bonds in a three-dimensional network. It is the hardest natural substance and does not conduct.
- **Graphite**: each carbon is joined to only 3 carbons, in flat layers of hexagons. The attraction between layers is weak and they slide apart easily, so graphite is soft and slippery; pencil leads contain graphite. Each carbon still has 1 valence electron that can move within the layer, so graphite conducts and can be used for electrodes.
- **Graphene**: a single layer of graphite.
- $\ce{C60}$: a ball-shaped molecule of 60 carbon atoms. It is made of separate molecules, not a network solid.

<StudentAsks q="Graphite is a nonmetal, so how can it conduct?">

Conducting needs charged particles that can move; they don't have to come from a metal. Each carbon in graphite uses only 3 valence electrons for bonds, and the fourth can move within the layer, so graphite conducts along its layers. Diamond uses all 4 valence electrons for bonds and has no electrons that can move, so it does not conduct. Same carbon, different structure, different properties.

## Metallic bonds: cations in a sea of electrons

Metal atoms have few valence electrons and lose them easily. In a piece of metal, every atom lets its valence electrons go; these electrons belong to no particular atom and move freely through the whole piece, like a sea around the neatly arranged metal cations. The attraction between the cations and this "electron sea" is the <Term t="chemistry.metallic-bond">metallic bond</Term>.

The electron-sea model explains the properties of metals:

- **Electrical conduction**: the electrons in the sea can move, so connecting a battery makes a current.
- **Heat conduction**: the mobile electrons also help carry heat through the metal.
- **Malleability and ductility** <Zh>延展性</Zh>: metals can be hammered into thin sheets and drawn into fine wires.

<Perspective title="Connect a battery: what moves?">

Another angle on the electron sea: instead of hammering, pass a current. Conduction needs charged particles that can move, so each time ask "what moves?":

| Connected to a battery | Charged particles that can move | Conducts? |
|---|---|---|
| copper wire (solid) | electrons in the sea drift toward the positive end; the copper ions stay put | yes |
| solid sodium chloride | none: the ions are fixed in the crystal | no |
| molten sodium chloride | $\ce{Na+}$ moves toward the negative end, $\ce{Cl-}$ toward the positive end | yes |

A metal conducts by electrons, so it conducts as a solid; an ionic compound conducts by ions, so it must be melted or dissolved before its ions can move. Hammering shows the same difference: sodium chloride slid one place puts + against + and splits, while a slid metal's cations are still in the electron sea. Whether the negative charges are fixed anions or flowing electrons decides both properties.

</Perspective>

## From structure to properties

Here are the four kinds of substance side by side:

| | Ionic compounds | Molecular compounds | Covalent network solids | Metals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{MgO}$ | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, sugar | diamond, silicon, $\ce{SiO2}$ | copper, iron |
| Particles | cations and anions | molecules | atoms | cations and the electron sea |
| Melting point | high | low | very high | mostly fairly high (mercury is a liquid at room temperature) |
| Conduction | not as a solid; conducts when molten or in water | not as a pure substance | usually does not conduct (graphite conducts; silicon is a semiconductor) | conducts as a solid and as a liquid |
| Other | hard but brittle | often a gas or liquid at room temperature | very hard (graphite is the exception) | malleable and ductile, with a shine |

Many ionic compounds dissolve in water, but not all: calcium carbonate, which you met in junior high, hardly dissolves in water.

<StudentAsks q="Sugar water doesn't conduct. But hydrogen chloride is a molecular compound too, so why does hydrochloric acid conduct?">

Pure hydrogen chloride indeed does not conduct. But when hydrogen chloride dissolves in water it dissociates into $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$, giving ions that can move, so hydrochloric acid conducts. Sugar stays as separate molecules in water, with no ions, so sugar water does not conduct. To decide whether a solution conducts, ask whether there are ions once the substance is in the water.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="The other way round: from properties to bonding">

Faced with a solid you don't know, ask three questions in order:

1. Does the solid conduct? If it does and it is malleable: a metal. (If it conducts but cannot be hammered out, think of graphite.)
2. The solid doesn't conduct, but the melt or a solution in water does: an ionic compound.
3. Nothing conducts: a low melting point means a molecular substance; a very high melting point and great hardness mean a covalent network solid.

For example, a white solid has a high melting point and does not conduct as a solid, but does once melted: question 1 "no", question 2 "yes", so it is an ionic compound, like magnesium oxide. Another white solid melts when heated in a pan and still does not conduct once melted: you reach question 3, and it is a molecular substance, like sugar.

</Perspective>
