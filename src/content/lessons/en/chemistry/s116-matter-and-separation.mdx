## Pure substances and mixtures

The junior-high classification once more:

- A **<Term t="chemistry.pure-substance">pure substance</Term>** is one substance with a fixed composition, written with one chemical formula. It is an element (one kind of atom, such as $\ce{O2}$ or $\ce{Fe}$) or a compound (two or more elements in a fixed ratio, such as $\ce{H2O}$ or $\ce{CO2}$).
- A **<Term t="chemistry.mixture">mixture</Term>** holds two or more pure substances. Its composition can vary, and each part keeps its own properties. It is homogeneous if it looks the same everywhere (salt water, air, an alloy) and heterogeneous if you can see different parts (muddy water).

When your eyes can't tell, test the properties. At a given pressure a pure substance has a fixed melting point and boiling point; a mixture doesn't.

<Graph desc="Schematic temperature–time graph for heating liquids at one atmosphere. The horizontal axis is heating time (arbitrary units) and the vertical axis is temperature in °C. The blue line is pure water: the temperature rises to 100 °C and then stays level until the water is gone. The red line is salt water: it boils above 100 °C, and as water evaporates and the solution gets more concentrated, its temperature keeps creeping up, with no level part." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 120]} height={300}

  labels={[{ at: [6.5, 88], text: 'Pure water', color: 'blue' }, { at: [6.5, 109], text: 'Salt water', color: 'red' }]} />

Find the level part on each line. A level part means one substance alone is boiling; a temperature that keeps creeping up means the composition is changing, so it's a mixture. So a clear liquid that boils at one atmosphere while its temperature slowly rises from 101 °C to 104 °C is a mixture (salt water, say); pure water would stay at 100 °C.

<StudentAsks q="If a liquid looks clear and perfectly even, isn't it a pure substance?">

Not necessarily. Salt water and sugar water are clear but are homogeneous mixtures: the dissolved particles are too small to see. Test a property instead: evaporate it to dryness and see whether a solid is left, or check whether its boiling temperature stays fixed. "Clear" tells you it's even, not that it's pure.

</StudentAsks>

## Gas pressure: molecules hitting the walls

The phase diagrams in the next section have pressure on the vertical axis, so first: where does a gas's pressure come from?

Gas molecules move fast in every direction and keep hitting the container walls. Each hit pushes the wall a little, and a huge number of hits add up to the force on each unit of wall area: the gas pressure. Qualitatively:

- Same container, same temperature: more molecules make more hits per second, so the pressure is higher.
- Same number of molecules, heated: the molecules move faster and hit more often and harder, so the pressure rises.

<Perspective title="Think of rain on an umbrella">

The push on an umbrella in the rain comes from the hits of single drops:

1. Drops just as fast, but more of them: more hits each second, a bigger push. This is "more molecules, higher pressure".
2. Just as many drops, falling faster: harder and more frequent hits, a bigger push. This is "higher temperature, higher pressure".

Where the analogy breaks: rain falls only from above, but gas molecules move every way, so every wall feels the same pressure. The exact relations between pressure, volume, temperature and amount are the gas laws of elective chemistry, which the exam doesn't test.

</Perspective>

## Phase diagrams: temperature and pressure set the state

Whether a substance is solid, liquid or gas depends on both temperature and pressure. Put temperature across and pressure up, and you get a <Term t="chemistry.phase-diagram">phase diagram</Term> (often called a three-phase diagram). Read it three ways:

- **Regions**: the region a point falls in is the state.
- **Lines**: on a line, the states on both sides exist together. The solid–liquid line is the melting point at each pressure, the liquid–gas line the boiling point, the solid–gas line sublimation.
- **<Term t="chemistry.triple-point">Triple point</Term>**: where the three lines meet and all three states coexist. Water's is at about 0.01 °C and 0.006 atmospheres.

The solid region is on the left, the liquid region at the top middle, and the gas region at the lower right. The dashed line is one atmosphere; it meets the solid–liquid line at 0 °C and the liquid–gas line at 100 °C. The orange point is placed by the T and P sliders." x={[0, 10]} y={[0, 10]} height={340}

<StudentAsks q="Why does water boil below 100 °C high in the mountains?">

The air pressure up there is lower, which moves the dashed line down. The liquid–gas line bends down to the left, so the crossing moves left: the boiling point drops below 100 °C and food cooks more slowly. A pressure cooker does the opposite: higher pressure, crossing moves right, water boils above 100 °C.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Turn a level line on the phase diagram into a heating curve">

Heating at a fixed pressure means walking right along a level line on the phase diagram:

1. Inside a region there is one state; the heat raises the temperature, and the heating curve slopes.
2. On a line two states coexist; the heat changes the state, the temperature holds, and the curve is level.
3. So the number of lines crossed equals the number of level parts.

| Case | Lines crossed | Level parts |
|---|---|---|
| Water, one atmosphere | solid–liquid, liquid–gas | 2: 0 °C and 100 °C |
| Carbon dioxide, one atmosphere | solid–gas | 1: about −78 °C |
| Carbon dioxide, 10 atmospheres | solid–liquid, liquid–gas | 2 |
| Water, below 0.006 atmospheres | solid–gas | 1 (ice sublimes) |

In a phase-diagram item you can turn the situation into a heating curve, or the other way round.

</Perspective>

## Separating mixtures: choose a method by property

```dialogue

>> turn: q-method

```

| Method | The difference it uses | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| Filtration (junior high) | one part is undissolved grains that stay on the paper | muddy water |
| <Term t="chemistry.distillation">Distillation</Term> | boiling point or volatility | water from ink; desalination |
| <Term t="chemistry.extraction">Extraction</Term> | the solute dissolves to different extents in different solvents (between two liquids, the solvents must not mix) | flower or leaf pigments into an organic solvent |
| <Term t="chemistry.chromatography">Chromatography</Term> | how strongly each part is held, and how well it dissolves in the solvent | the pigments in ink or leaves |

### Distillation: ink and seawater

The curriculum's experiment is distilling ink, a mixture of water and pigment. On heating the water boils off, while the pigment doesn't evaporate and stays in the flask. The vapour is cooled in the condenser and runs into a conical flask as a colourless distillate.

<Perspective title="Another road to fresh water: reverse osmosis">

Distillation must boil the water, which takes a lot of heat. Reverse osmosis works differently:

1. A special membrane separates seawater from fresh water. Water molecules pass through it; dissolved salt ions don't.
2. A large pressure on the seawater side squeezes water molecules through the membrane.
3. The salts stay behind, the seawater side gets more concentrated and is drained away, and fresh water collects on the other side.

Distillation uses "water evaporates, salt doesn't" and works by heat; reverse osmosis uses "water passes the membrane, salt ions don't" and works by pressure. Same separation, different property: the theme of this section.

</Perspective>

### Hard water and softening

Water with a lot of calcium ions, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and magnesium ions, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, is <Term t="chemistry.hard-water">hard water</Term>. Soap and these ions form an insoluble solid (soap scum), so there's little lather; and heated hard water deposits scale (mainly calcium carbonate) in kettles and pipes.

Softening means removing those ions. One way is to make them a precipitate and filter it off:

- **Boiling**: calcium hydrogen carbonate breaks down into a calcium carbonate precipitate:

  $$
  \ce{Ca(HCO3)2 -> CaCO3 v + H2O + CO2 ^}
  $$

  This removes only the part present as hydrogen carbonates.
- **Adding sodium carbonate**:

  $$
  \ce{Ca^2+ + CO3^2- -> CaCO3 v}
  $$

Filtering hard water directly does nothing: the ions are dissolved and pass through the paper. They must become a solid first.

<Perspective title="Another way to soften: swap the ions">

Instead of making the calcium ions leave as a solid, an ion-exchange resin swaps them. Its surface holds exchangeable sodium ions; as hard water flows past, the resin grabs calcium ions and releases sodium ions.

### Extraction: move a solute into another solvent

Extraction uses a solute dissolving to different extents in different solvents. The curriculum's experiment soaks crushed flowers or leaves in an organic solvent: the pigments dissolve into it and leave the rest of the plant behind.

Between two liquids you use a separatory funnel. Iodine dissolves only a little in water but well in hexane <Zh>正己烷</Zh>, and hexane and water don't mix. Shake iodine water with hexane in the funnel and let it stand: two layers form, most iodine moves into the hexane, which turns purple, and you run off the lower layer through the tap.

<Perspective title="Three conditions for choosing an extraction solvent">

Treat extraction as "choose the solvent". A good one must:

1. dissolve the solute far better than the original solvent does;
2. not mix with the original solvent, so two layers form;
3. not react with the solute.

**Example**　To extract iodine from iodine water, ethanol or hexane? Both dissolve iodine better than water, so both pass condition 1. But ethanol mixes with water in any ratio, so no layers form: it fails condition 2. Hexane passes all three, so choose hexane.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="In a separatory funnel, is the organic solvent always on top?">

No. Density decides: the less dense liquid floats. Hexane is less dense than water, so it's on top, but some organic solvents (dichloromethane, for one) are denser and sink. Use the densities an item gives, or see which layer changed colour.

</StudentAsks>

### Chromatography: which part runs farther

<Term t="chemistry.chromatography">Chromatography</Term> (the curriculum writes <Zh>色層分析</Zh>) uses differences in how strongly each part is held by a fixed material and how well it dissolves in the moving solvent. In thin-layer chromatography (TLC):

1. Draw a pencil starting line near the bottom of a coated plate and spot the sample on it (pencil, because ink would itself separate).
2. Stand the plate in a little solvent, with the solvent surface below the line so the sample doesn't wash into it.
3. The solvent climbs and carries the parts along; the more strongly held a part is, the slower it moves. The highest point the solvent reaches is the solvent front.

Spots keep moving, so we compare a ratio:

$$
R_f=\frac{\text{distance of the spot from the starting line}}{\text{distance of the solvent front from the starting line}}
$$

Under the same conditions a substance always has the same $R_f$, which lies between 0 and 1, so $R_f$ identifies parts.

<Predict id="rf-value" prompt="On a TLC plate, spot A is 2.4 cm from the starting line and the solvent front is 6.0 cm from it. What is spot A's Rf value?" answer={0.4}>

$R_f=\dfrac{2.4}{6.0}=0.4$. The figure below shows exactly this at t = 1.

</Predict>

<Perspective title="No ratio needed: compare side by side">

Spot known pigment A, known pigment B and unknown X side by side on one plate and develop them together:

| Sample | Spot heights (cm) |
|---|---|
| A | 1.5 |
| B | 3.6 |
| X | 1.5 and 3.6 |

X's two spots match A and B, so X most likely contains both. On one plate the front is equally high for every lane, so comparing heights is comparing $R_f$. And two spots show X isn't a pure substance, which would give one.

</Perspective>

<Perspective later title="Recrystallization: using solubility that changes with temperature" needs="solubility and solubility curves (the solutions chapter); not in the required course, but it appeared in options on the 112 and 114 GSAT" href="/en/chemistry/s116-solutions/">

<Term t="chemistry.recrystallization">Recrystallisation</Term> purifies a solid. Dissolve it, with its little impurity, in a small amount of hot solvent, then cool slowly. The main substance dissolves less when cold, so the excess crystallises; the small amount of impurity stays dissolved. Filter, and the crystals are purer. It suits substances whose solubility changes a lot with temperature. Options in item 30 of the 114 GSAT mentioned it, and items 25 to 27 of the 115 GSAT, an inquiry set on extracting potassium salts from bananas, also used solubility.

</Perspective>
