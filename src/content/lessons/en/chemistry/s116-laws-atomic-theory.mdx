## Lavoisier: watching reactions with a balance

In the second half of the eighteenth century, the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier <Zh>拉瓦節</Zh> brought precise weighing into chemistry. Three of his contributions matter for this chapter:

- **The concept of an element**: an element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. In a chemistry textbook he published in 1789 he gave a list of elements. Some items on it later turned out not to be elements, but the idea that an element is where breaking down ends is still used today.
- **Burning and oxidation**: the popular phlogiston theory <Zh>燃素說</Zh> of the time said that a burning substance gives off something called "phlogiston". Lavoisier used weighing to show that burning, and metals gaining mass when heated in air, are really a substance combining with oxygen. This is the junior-high idea "oxidation: a substance combining with oxygen".
- **Conservation of mass**: he ran reactions in sealed vessels, weighed carefully before and after, and confirmed that the total mass does not change.

The third point is the <Term t="chemistry.conservation-of-mass">law of conservation of mass</Term>: **in a chemical reaction, the total mass of the substances is the same before and after.**

<Predict id="mg-oxygen" prompt="2.4 g of magnesium ribbon burns completely in air. All the white powder formed (magnesium oxide) is collected and weighs 4.0 g. How many grams of oxygen combined with the magnesium?" answer={1.6} unit="g">

The magnesium plus oxygen before the reaction weighs the same as the magnesium oxide after it, so the oxygen is $4.0-2.4=1.6$ g.

</Predict>

As an equation, the reaction is $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$. Before the reaction, the magnesium and oxygen together weigh $2.4+1.6=4.0\ \mathrm{g}$; after it, the magnesium oxide also weighs $4.0\ \mathrm{g}$. The ribbon only seems to get heavier because at the start we weighed the magnesium and not the oxygen that came from the air.

Lavoisier's wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier <Zh>瑪麗－安妮</Zh>, was part of the laboratory work: she recorded experiments, drew the illustrations of the apparatus for his book, and translated English chemistry works into French.

<StudentAsks q="Does conservation of mass mean the mass of each substance stays the same?">

No: what is conserved is the **total** mass. As the reaction runs, the reactants (magnesium and oxygen) get used up and the product (magnesium oxide) builds up, so each of their masses changes; but what the reactants lose is exactly what the product gains. In the figure above, the red part grows by exactly as much as the blue and orange parts shrink.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Draw the system's boundary first: open and sealed containers">

Conservation of mass is about all the substances that take part. If gas enters or leaves the container while the balance only weighs what is inside, the reading changes. So before solving, ask: **does anything cross the container's boundary?**

- **Open container, a candle burning**: the wax burns to carbon dioxide and water vapour, which drift into the air, so the reading falls.
- **Open container, magnesium ribbon burning**: oxygen from the air comes in and combines with the magnesium, the product stays in the container, so the reading rises.
- **Sealed container**: nothing goes in or out, so whatever burns inside, the reading stays the same.

A changing reading does not mean conservation of mass has failed; it means the balance weighed only part of the system. Lavoisier insisted on weighing in sealed vessels precisely to keep everything taking part in the reaction on the balance.

## Definite proportions: one compound, one mass ratio

In the 1790s the French chemist Joseph Proust <Zh>普魯斯特</Zh> compared samples of the same compound from different sources and made by different methods, and found that the mass ratio of its elements was always the same. This is the <Term t="chemistry.definite-proportions">law of definite proportions</Term>: **a given compound, whatever its source or method of preparation, always has the same mass ratio of its elements.**

Water is an example: whether it is distilled from a river or made by burning hydrogen, pure water always has hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio $1:8$.

<Predict id="water-oxygen" prompt="In pure water the mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen is 1 : 8. If 36 g of pure water is completely electrolysed, how many grams of oxygen gas do you get?" answer={32} unit="g">

Split 36 g into $1+8=9$ shares of 4 g each. Oxygen takes 8 shares, $8\times4=32$ g; hydrogen takes 1 share, 4 g. Together $4+32=36$ g, which also agrees with conservation of mass.

</Predict>

**Example**　A student burns different masses of magnesium ribbon in air and weighs the product each time. Which group's data does not fit the law of definite proportions, and why?

| Group | Magnesium (g) | Product (g) | Oxygen = product − magnesium (g) | Mg : O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.2 | 2.0 | 0.8 | $3:2$ |
| B | 3.0 | 5.0 | 2.0 | $3:2$ |
| C | 4.5 | 7.5 | 3.0 | $3:2$ |
| D | 3.6 | 5.2 | 1.6 | $9:4$ |

1. First use conservation of mass to find the oxygen that combined in each group: product minus magnesium.
2. Then find the magnesium : oxygen mass ratio. Groups A, B and C all give $3:2$; group D gives $3.6:1.6=9:4$, which is not magnesium oxide's fixed ratio.
3. Group D took up less oxygen than it should have, so some of the magnesium did not burn. Its product is a mixture of magnesium oxide and magnesium, not a pure compound, and the law of definite proportions only ever applies to a pure compound.

<StudentAsks q="If I burn a lot of magnesium in a sealed jar with only a little oxygen, will the magnesium oxide contain a larger share of magnesium?">

No. Once the oxygen runs out, the extra magnesium simply stays unreacted, still magnesium. In the magnesium oxide that forms, magnesium and oxygen are still in the mass ratio $3:2$. Only if the "product" you weigh is mixed with unreacted magnesium does the ratio look wrong, like group D in the example.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Seen as a graph: mass against mass is a line through the origin">

## Multiple proportions: the same two elements, more than one compound

Carbon and oxygen form two compounds: carbon monoxide $\ce{CO}$ and carbon dioxide $\ce{CO2}$. Their carbon : oxygen mass ratios differ, but that does not break the law of definite proportions, because they are **two** compounds. Is there any link between the two ratios?

<Predict id="co-co2" prompt="In carbon monoxide the carbon : oxygen mass ratio is 3 : 4, and in carbon dioxide it is 3 : 8. If both contain the same amount of carbon, how many times as much oxygen is in the carbon dioxide?" answer={2} unit="times">

Give both 3 g of carbon: carbon monoxide then has 4 g of oxygen and carbon dioxide 8 g. $8\div4=2$, so it is twice as much.

</Predict>

This is the pattern Dalton noticed early in the nineteenth century, called the <Term t="chemistry.multiple-proportions">law of multiple proportions</Term>: **when two elements form two or more compounds, fixing the mass of one element makes the masses of the other come out in a simple whole-number ratio.**

**Example**　In water $\ce{H2O}$ the hydrogen : oxygen mass ratio is $1:8$, and in hydrogen peroxide $\ce{H2O2}$ it is $1:16$. With the hydrogen fixed, what is the ratio of the oxygen masses in the two?

1. The hydrogen is 1 in both ratios, so it is already fixed; no rescaling is needed.
2. The oxygen masses are $8:16=1:2$, a simple whole-number ratio, as the law says.

The method is always two steps: **first make one element the same in both, then compare the other.** If the first terms of the two ratios differ, multiply each ratio by a number so that they match.

Drag $c$ to a few different values, read the heights of the two points each time, and work out how many times higher the red point is. That multiple does not change with $c$: this is what "fixing the mass of carbon" means.

<StudentAsks q="Oxygen O₂ and ozone O₃ have oxygen in different proportions. Does that count as multiple proportions?">

No. Oxygen and ozone are each made of oxygen alone; they are not compounds. The law of multiple proportions is about **two elements** forming **two or more compounds**. In the same way, $\ce{NaCl}$ and $\ce{KCl}$ don't count either, because their elements are not the same pair.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective title="Another angle: fix the other element">

You can fix either element. Take the same data and fix the oxygen this time:

1. Carbon monoxide has carbon : oxygen $=3:4$; multiply by 2 to get $6:8$.
2. Carbon dioxide has carbon : oxygen $=3:8$.
3. Now the oxygen is 8 in both, and the carbon masses are $6:3=2:1$.

Fixing the carbon gives oxygen $1:2$; fixing the oxygen gives carbon $2:1$. They are reciprocals, and both are simple whole-number ratios. In an exam, fix whichever element makes the arithmetic easier.

Finally, keep the two laws apart: **definite proportions looks at one compound**, whose mass ratio is fixed; **multiple proportions looks at two or more compounds of the same two elements**, and compares the mass of one element once the other is fixed.

</Perspective>

## Dalton's atomic theory: explaining the three laws with atoms

The English scientist John Dalton <Zh>道耳頓</Zh> taught for many years in Manchester. He put forward his atomic theory around 1803 and published it as a book in 1808. It has four main points:

1. Matter is made of tiny particles called atoms, and atoms cannot be divided.
2. Atoms of one element all have the same mass and properties; atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties.
3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in simple whole-number ratios.
4. A chemical reaction only rearranges atoms into new combinations; atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

```dialogue
T: In junior high you learned that an atom contains electrons and a nucleus. Let's use that to test Dalton's points.
T: Which of the four points clashes with "an atom contains electrons and a nucleus"?
>> turn: q-dalton
S: Point 1. There are smaller particles inside the atom, so atoms are not "indivisible" after all.
T: Right. But in a chemical reaction the kinds and numbers of atoms don't change, so the atomic theory still explains the three laws perfectly well.
```

**Conservation of mass**: by point 4, the number of atoms of each kind is the same before and after the reaction; by point 2, each atom has a fixed mass. With neither the numbers nor the masses of the atoms changing, the total mass cannot change.

**Definite proportions**: by point 3, the ratio of magnesium atoms to oxygen atoms in magnesium oxide is fixed at $1:1$; by point 2, each atom has a fixed mass. The atomic weights you learned in junior high are relative masses: magnesium 24, oxygen 16. However many pairs there are, the magnesium : oxygen mass ratio is $24:16=3:2$, exactly the ratio of groups A, B and C in the example.

**Multiple proportions**: atoms can only combine as whole atoms, never as half an atom, so the ratios must be whole-number ratios. The particle picture below shows this.

Look at the blue circles first: both rows have 2, which is "fixing the mass of carbon". Now count the red circles: 2 in the top row and 4 in the bottom, a ratio of $2:4=1:2$. Every oxygen atom has the same mass, so the oxygen masses are also in the ratio $1:2$.

Check with atomic weights: in carbon monoxide carbon : oxygen $=12:16=3:4$, and in carbon dioxide $12:32=3:8$, the same ratios used in the "Predict first" question earlier. The whole-number ratio of particles is where the simple whole-number ratio of masses comes from.

</Perspective>

<StudentAsks q="Could Dalton see atoms? How did he know they exist?">

He couldn't see them. Dalton's atoms were inferred from weighing data. The atomic theory was accepted because it explained both conservation of mass and definite proportions, and it also predicted that when two elements form different compounds, the masses should come in whole-number ratios, which is exactly the law of multiple proportions. This is often how scientific theories are built: an unseen model explains data you can see.

</StudentAsks>

<Perspective later title="Later revisions to the atomic theory" needs="atomic structure and isotopes (next chapter)" href="/en/chemistry/s116-atomic-structure/">

Later discoveries revised two of the theory's points:

1. Atoms can be divided: an atom is made of smaller particles, with a nucleus at the centre and electrons around it.
2. Atoms of the same element can have different masses; these are called isotopes. Hydrogen, for example, has three: protium, deuterium and tritium <Zh>氕、氘、氚</Zh>.

But "compounds are atoms combined in whole-number ratios" and "a reaction only rearranges atoms" still hold for chemical reactions, so none of the three laws was overturned. What is inside the atom is the subject of the next chapter.

</Perspective>
